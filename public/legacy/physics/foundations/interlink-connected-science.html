A journey and its arrow

Walk along one side of a playground, then turn and walk along the next. Your route has two pieces. Your change of place needs only one arrow.

Distance adds the lengths you actually travel. Displacement points directly from your starting position to your finishing position. Physics needs both because route and result differ.

The coloured arrows show the two parts of the walk. Place the second arrow’s tail at the first arrow’s head. The blue arrow gives their combined displacement.

Its length represents how far the finish lies from the start. Its arrowhead represents direction. It need not follow the route you walked.

A vector is a quantity needing both size and direction. Displacement is one. A quantity needing only size, such as distance, is called a scalar.

That is all the vector machinery we need here: follow arrows head to tail; draw one arrow from the first start to the final finish.

On a straight line, signs do the drawing

Choose right as positive. A displacement of +3 m means three metres right; −3 m means three metres left. The minus sign supplies direction.

Three metres right, then two metres left, gives a displacement of +1 m. The distance is 5 m because both pieces contribute to the route.

displacement = final position − starting position

Positions are measured from the same chosen origin. Moving the origin changes the two position numbers together; their difference, the displacement, stays the same.

“If I travelled a long way, my displacement must be large.” This seems natural because both use metres.

But a complete lap ends at its start: distance is positive and displacement is zero. The arrow records the change of place, not the effort of travelling.

Two people follow different routes between the same start and finish. Their displacements match. Their distances need not. Later, force and velocity arrows will use this same language.

Arrows compare like quantities: add displacements to displacements, or forces to forces. A force arrow cannot be added to a velocity arrow just because both point right.

Can a traveller have a longer route than a friend but a smaller displacement? Invent a route that makes both true.

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

A route can include detours or a return towards the starting point. Compare endpoints separately from the length travelled; a complete lap is the limiting case.

One arrow can describe two changes at once — Describe the same arrow through two perpendicular changes.

Shared electrons can make molecules or networks — Molecular geometry decides whether directional charge effects cancel.

One arrow can describe two changes at once

Walk diagonally across a square floor. Your position changes both eastward and northward. One displacement arrow contains both changes.

Imagine projecting the arrow onto the floor's east–west and north–south lines. The two signed lengths are its components: directional parts of one vector.

These are not extra journeys you secretly made. They are a way of describing the same start and finish using two perpendicular directions.

To combine several displacements, add all east–west changes together and all north–south changes together. Then draw the arrow to that combined endpoint.

For perpendicular components x and y, geometry connects their lengths to the resultant size r through a right triangle.

r² = x² + y²

x and y are signed displacement components; r is the non-negative length. All have units of length, so every term in this equation has units of length squared.

If one component is zero, the arrow lies along the other direction. Reversing one component mirrors the arrow without changing its length.

Do not add perpendicular lengths as though they point along one line. A diagonal is shorter than travelling the two sides of the right triangle.

The same method applies to force or velocity, using their units. Components describe an existing vector; they do not invent two additional physical forces.

Later, sine and cosine become names for right-triangle ratios linking an angle to these components. The physical picture comes first: how much points each way?

Two equal force arrows point at right angles. Why is their combined size less than the arithmetic sum of their sizes?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

They do not reinforce along one direction. Their resultant is the diagonal of a right triangle, whereas the arithmetic sum follows both sides. Use direction before adding sizes.

Shared electrons can make molecules or networks — Molecular shape decides whether directional charge effects reinforce or cancel.

A journey and its arrow — Use the head-to-tail construction to see why component addition reaches the same endpoint.

Components describe how much of one vector points along each chosen direction; their geometry preserves the original arrow.

Speed, velocity and viewpoint

A bus travels along a road. A seated passenger is still relative to the bus but moving relative to a tree. “Moving” needs a stated viewpoint.

A reference frame is the set of surroundings we measure positions against. For everyday road journeys, the ground is usually a convenient choice.

Speed tells how quickly distance is covered. Velocity tells how quickly position changes, including direction. Returning to the start separates them immediately.

average speed = total distance / total time

average velocity = displacement / total time

Both have units m/s. Average speed cannot be negative. Along a chosen line, negative average velocity means the overall displacement points in the negative direction.

A speedometer estimates speed over a short interval. A whole-journey average cannot reveal each stop or burst of speed. These are different questions about the journey.

On this position–time graph, moving right means later time. Moving upward means a more positive position; the vertical axis is not the road’s physical height.

The slope is position change divided by time: velocity. A steeper rising line means greater positive velocity. A horizontal line means the position is unchanged.

A falling line means motion towards more negative positions. It need not mean slowing down. Equal slopes mean equal velocities, even at different positions.

Area under this graph is not distance. A graph’s meaning comes from its axes. We will use area with a velocity–time graph instead.

Walking forwards along a steadily moving bus adds your motion to the bus’s motion, as seen from the road. Walking backwards subtracts it.

The passengers and roadside observer describe different velocities for the same person.

Half the journey time at one speed and half at another permits a simple average of those speeds. Equal distances do not: the slower part occupies more time.

Always return to total distance divided by total time.

Later, instantaneous velocity describes the value approached as we shorten the measuring interval around a moment. The mathematics lesson explains why this is not division by zero.

Two cyclists meet while moving in opposite directions. Which quantities can match at that instant, and which need not?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Their positions match in the chosen frame. Their speeds can match, but their velocities have opposite directions. Their accelerations require separate information.

Why a shrinking interval becomes useful — Shrink an interval to understand the origin of an instantaneous rate.

A graph can show change or accumulated effect — The axes decide what a slope or area represents.

A journey average uses the whole interval; a local velocity describes signed position change near one instant in a stated reference frame.

Changing velocity needs its own quantity

Two trolleys can have the same velocity now, yet behave differently next second. One keeps moving steadily; the other gains speed. Velocity alone cannot describe that difference.

Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes. It can describe speeding up, slowing down, or turning. Direction matters because velocity is a vector.

average acceleration = change in velocity / time taken

The unit m/s² means “metres per second of velocity change, per second”. It is a rate of change of a rate, not a new kind of distance.

Choose right as positive. Leftward acceleration reduces a rightward velocity. But it increases the speed of an object already travelling left. Negative does not automatically mean slowing.

Here the vertical axis is velocity, not position. Slope means acceleration. A horizontal line means constant velocity; a steeper rise means greater positive acceleration.

For the same velocity change, doubling the time halves average acceleration. For the same time, doubling the velocity change doubles it.

When acceleration stays constant

Equal velocity changes in equal times give a straight graph. Let u be starting velocity, v final velocity, a constant acceleration and t elapsed time.

velocity gained = at, so v = u + at

The product’s units explain it: (m/s²) × s = m/s. Add that velocity change to the starting velocity. This rule needs constant acceleration.

Each narrow strip under a velocity–time graph represents velocity × time: a little displacement. Adding the strips gives the whole displacement.

For the straight graph, average velocity is (u + v)/2. Therefore displacement s = (u + v)t/2. The rectangle plus triangle makes the same area.

Area below the time axis contributes negative displacement. Adding the sizes of all areas gives distance.

At the top of a thrown ball’s flight, upward velocity is momentarily zero while downward acceleration continues: zero velocity does not require zero acceleration.

Writing s = ut + ½at² separates the same graph into a rectangle and triangle. Here s is displacement, u initial velocity and a constant acceleration.

The first term is displacement from keeping the starting velocity. The second accounts for velocity gained. This form describes the same straight velocity–time graph.

A trolley is moving left while its acceleration points right. What happens before and after its velocity passes through zero?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

It first slows while moving left, then speeds up right if the acceleration continues. The acceleration does not need to reverse when the velocity reverses.

What changes motion? — Ask which interaction changes the velocity described by the graph.

How much changes, and how quickly? — A reaction amount and its rate differ just as position and velocity do.

Acceleration describes how velocity changes, so its direction must be compared with velocity before predicting whether speed rises or falls.

What changes motion?

Push a trolley, then let go. It slows on a rough floor. Everyday experience suggests that motion needs a continuing push. Now imagine reducing the friction.

The trolley keeps moving longer. Remove all opposing interactions in the ideal model and there is no reason for its velocity to change.

Newton’s first law: with zero net external force, an object keeps the same velocity. That includes remaining still. Inertia names this persistence of motion.

Several objects may push or pull on the trolley. Their combined effect, added with directions, is the net force. A force is an interaction, measured in newtons, N.

This is a force diagram: isolate one object and draw only forces acting on it. Name their sources. An arrow showing motion would be a different kind of arrow.

How strongly will the velocity change?

For the same mass, a greater net force produces greater acceleration. For the same net force, a greater mass produces less acceleration.

acceleration = net force / mass, or Fnet = ma

Here m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in m/s². One newton changes a 1 kg mass’s velocity by 1 m/s each second.

Double net force while mass stays fixed: acceleration doubles. Double mass while net force stays fixed: acceleration halves. Reverse net force: acceleration reverses.

Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity. Balanced forces can act on a moving object. Newton’s second law describes ordinary motion of a fixed mass.

As the bus accelerates, your feet move with the floor while the rest of you initially keeps its earlier velocity. You lean back relative to the bus.

From the roadside, no mysterious backward force is needed; the bus is moving ahead beneath you.

A book rests on a table. Earth pulls it down; the table pushes it up. Equal forces on this one book give no acceleration.

Next we must distinguish this balance from an interaction pair.

A table supports a book because neighbouring atoms resist being compressed into a different arrangement. Electrical interactions and quantum behaviour supply the microscopic contact-force mechanism.

Two objects have the same acceleration under different net forces. What does that suggest about their masses within this model?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Force divided by mass must match. The object experiencing greater force must have proportionally greater mass; matching acceleration does not imply matching force.

A bond is a stable interacting arrangement — Contact forces emerge from interacting atomic arrangements.

Falling faster changes the air's response — Balanced forces can accompany continuing motion and energy transfer.

The vector sum of external interactions changes velocity, and mass determines the response to a given net force.

Weight and falling together

Press your palm against a wall. Your hand pushes the wall, and the wall pushes your hand. Both forces belong to one interaction and occur together.

Newton’s third law: the forces two objects exert on each other are equal in size and opposite in direction. They act on different objects.

The wall’s force on your hand belongs on your hand’s force diagram. Your hand’s force on the wall belongs on the wall’s diagram. They do not cancel there.

The book’s upward support and downward weight can balance, but they are not a third-law pair: both act on the book and have different partners.

Mass, weight and support are different

Mass measures inertia and is measured in kg. Weight is Earth’s gravitational pull, measured in N. Near Earth’s surface, weight is W = mg.

Here g is the downward acceleration of free fall, about 9.8 m/s², also expressible as 9.8 N/kg. “Newtons per kilogram” says how much pull each kilogram experiences.

Double mass at the same location: weight doubles. Move where g is smaller: weight decreases while mass stays the same.

Gravity gives a heavier object more force but it also has proportionally more inertia. Ignoring air resistance, a = mg/m = g: both effects grow together.

Support force is a surface’s push, perpendicular to it; “normal force” is its technical name. A scale senses support. A freely falling scale reads zero although gravity acts.

Release both at the same height and time, with no initial vertical velocity. Ignoring air resistance, their downward motion matches. The thrown ball also travels sideways.

The two motions combine; the sideways launch does not delay its fall.

A ball thrown upward has zero vertical velocity at its highest point. Gravity is still changing that velocity downward.

Before the top it slows the rise; afterwards it speeds the fall. The interaction never switched off.

Outside an approximately spherical body, gravitational strength decreases with distance squared. Doubling distance from its centre quarters the field, with source mass held fixed.

This explains why altitude changes weight without changing mass. Height above the ground differs from distance measured from Earth’s centre.

Uniform downward acceleration lets us describe sideways and vertical motion separately, then combine their displacement arrows. This becomes projectile motion without requiring new force laws.

A lift accelerates upward while carrying a scale. Does the person’s mass, gravitational weight or scale reading need to increase?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

The scale’s support force must exceed weight to accelerate the person upward. Mass and gravitational weight at the same location need not change.

Falling faster changes the air's response — Orbiting astronauts and their spacecraft fall together.

Pressure: the same push, differently spread — Weight distributed through a liquid creates a depth-dependent pressure.

Gravity sets weight, mass sets inertia, and a support force can change independently of both.

Friction and turning motion

Push a heavy box gently. It stays still even though your push is real. The floor exerts an opposing sideways force: static friction.

Push harder while the box still stays still. Friction increases to match you. It adjusts up to a maximum; it is not automatically at that maximum.

At contact, tiny regions deform and adhere. Friction is a combined effect of these microscopic interactions. Roughness alone cannot predict it reliably.

Once sliding begins, sliding friction opposes the relative sliding of the surfaces. It is often smaller than maximum static friction, though materials can behave differently.

Pressing the same surfaces together more strongly usually increases the available friction. For many dry contacts, a useful model makes that maximum proportional to the normal force.

Friction need not point backwards

When you walk forwards, your shoe tends to push the ground backwards. Static friction from the ground pushes you forwards. Friction opposes slipping at contact, not all motion.

A driven tyre similarly pushes backwards on the road. The road’s forward force accelerates the vehicle. Without enough grip, the tyre slips instead.

Turning is also acceleration

Imagine a ball travelling around a circle at constant speed. Its velocity arrow continually changes direction. A continuing net force is therefore required.

For steady circular motion, that net force points towards the centre. Centripetal means “centre-seeking”; it names the required direction, not a new force source.

For a car turning on a level road, tyre friction can supply it. For a ball on a string, tension — the string’s pull — supplies it.

“A released ball flies radially outwards.” Circular motion makes this feel plausible.

But remove the inward force and the ball initially continues along its present velocity: the tangent, touching the circle at the release point.

The curve needs a cause; straight motion does not need a continuing push.

Take the same bend faster. The velocity direction must change more quickly, and a larger inward force is needed.

If grip cannot supply it, the car cannot follow that same circular path.

A useful dry-contact model writes maximum static friction as μₛN. N is normal force; μₛ is a dimensionless coefficient describing the pair of surfaces.

Actual static friction ranges from zero up to this maximum. The equation specifies a limit, not a force that must always act at full size.

For circular motion at speed v and radius r, inward acceleration is v²/r. Greater speed makes the velocity arrow turn more quickly while being longer.

Doubling speed therefore quadruples the required inward force at fixed mass and radius. The relation F = mv²/r names the required net force, not an additional force.

A car rounds a bend at steady speed. Is zero acceleration consistent with that observation? Where must the net force point?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

No. Its velocity direction changes. The horizontal resultant must point towards the curve’s centre for circular motion, often supplied by tyre friction.

Mobile electrons explain several metal properties — Deformation and surface behaviour depend on atomic structure.

A bicycle: chemistry becomes motion through interactions — Road friction can drive a bicycle forward while other interactions dissipate energy.

Friction opposes slipping at contact, while circular motion requires a net inward force to keep changing velocity direction.

Falling faster changes the air's response

Drop an object through air. Gravity pulls down from the beginning, while the air's upward drag grows as the object falls faster.

Choose downward as positive. The net downward force is weight minus drag. It is this difference, not weight alone, that determines acceleration.

downward acceleration = (weight − drag) / mass

Initially, weight exceeds drag, so downward speed increases. Growing drag reduces the net force and therefore reduces acceleration.

Eventually drag can equal weight. Net force and acceleration then become zero, while downward speed remains non-zero. This steady falling speed is terminal speed.

Open a parachute. Its greater effective area suddenly increases drag at the existing speed. The net force becomes upward while the motion is still downward.

The falling object slows. As speed decreases, drag decreases until it again matches weight, now at a lower terminal speed.

For many everyday fast flows, drag is approximately proportional to speed squared when shape, orientation and air density stay fixed. This is a model, not universal behaviour.

Doubling speed can then require roughly four times the opposing force. A changed posture or parachute changes the relationship itself, so hold conditions explicit.

At terminal speed, kinetic energy stops increasing. Yet gravity still transfers energy through the continuing downward displacement; air motion and warming receive it.

Balanced force therefore does not imply zero energy transfer. Steady motion and zero net work on the falling object's kinetic energy can coexist with large transfers.

In orbit, air resistance may be very small, but gravity remains. An orbiting object continually falls while moving sideways, so its path keeps curving around Earth.

Astronauts float relative to their spacecraft because both fall together. Floating is evidence of little support force, not evidence that gravity has disappeared.

A falling object has reached terminal speed. Does it lose gravitational potential energy, gain kinetic energy, or warm its surroundings? Which claims can hold together?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

It continues losing gravitational potential energy and transfers energy to air and surroundings. Its kinetic energy stays constant because its speed is constant.

Track the complete system rather than only one store.

Energy, power and efficiency — Follow steady energy transfers even when a visible quantity stops changing.

At terminal speed, drag balances weight and kinetic energy stays steady while gravitational energy continues transferring to the surroundings.

Forces can turn objects

Push a door near its hinge, then near its handle. The same sideways push turns it more effectively at the handle. Distance from the turning axis matters.

The relevant distance is the shortest, perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force’s line of action. We call it the lever arm.

turning effect = force × perpendicular lever arm

The turning effect is called moment of a force, or torque. Its unit N m records a force acting with a lever arm. It describes turning, not energy.

With force fixed, doubling the lever arm doubles torque. With the lever arm fixed, doubling force doubles torque. A force directed through the pivot has zero lever arm.

Choose clockwise as positive, or anticlockwise; either works if used consistently. Opposite torques can balance even when the individual forces have different sizes.

Where should you support an object?

Balance a ruler on a finger. Shift some mass towards one end and the balancing point shifts towards it. More mass there means more gravitational pull there.

The centre of mass is the average location of an object’s mass, weighted towards its heavier parts. In uniform gravity, its weight acts as if concentrated there.

A uniform ruler’s centre lies halfway along. An uneven object’s need not. The centre of mass can even lie in empty space, as for a ring.

For an upright object on a level base, the vertical line through this centre must fall within the support area. Beyond the edge, weight produces a tipping torque.

Spreading your feet widens the support area. You can lean farther before the line of your weight passes outside it.

Carrying a heavy bag changes the combined centre of mass, so you adjust your posture.

No overall acceleration needs balanced forces. No change in rotation also needs balanced torques. Two equal opposite forces at different places can form a turning pair.

Later, this will explain why a motor coil rotates.

A spinning skater can turn faster by pulling their arms inward when external turning effects are negligible. Their angular momentum, a measure of rotational motion, remains approximately constant.

The changed mass distribution makes the same rotational account correspond to a greater spin rate. Muscles do work, so rotational kinetic energy can increase.

Can two equal opposite forces leave an object’s centre of mass unaccelerated while changing its rotation?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Yes. Acting at different places, their forces cancel but their torques can reinforce. The object choice and force locations matter.

Motors and generators — Forces at different places can turn a coil without a net push.

A bicycle: chemistry becomes motion through interactions — A pedal uses a lever arm to turn the crank.

A force’s turning effect depends on its line of action as well as its size, so force balance alone cannot guarantee rotational balance.

A push lasts: momentum and impulse

Stopping a moving trolley requires an interaction sustained for a time. Compare equal velocities: the heavier trolley needs more force, more time, or both.

We combine mass and velocity into momentum, a vector that helps describe how motion is exchanged in interactions.

momentum p = mv

Its unit kg m/s means mass multiplied by velocity. At fixed velocity, doubling mass doubles momentum. At fixed mass, reversing velocity reverses momentum. Rest gives zero momentum.

Now let a steady net force act for time Δt. The symbol Δ means “change” or “interval”. From Fnet = ma and a = Δv/Δt:

Fnet Δt = m Δv = Δp

The left side is impulse: force multiplied by the time it acts. Its unit N s equals kg m/s. The right side is the momentum change of a fixed mass.

If the force varies, use its average over the interval. The area under a force–time graph gives impulse: each strip contributes force × a little time.

For the same stopping change, increasing stopping time reduces average force. Moving your hands back while catching a ball applies this idea.

Interactions exchange momentum

When two skaters push apart, equal opposite forces act for the same time. They gain equal opposite momentum changes. Their combined momentum stays unchanged.

This conservation holds for a chosen system with zero net external impulse. Individual momenta may change greatly. Internal forces exchange momentum within the boundary.

The rocket pushes exhaust backwards; the exhaust pushes the rocket forwards. For rocket plus exhaust, momentum can balance without air to push against.

The rocket alone loses mass, so we must account for the momentum carried away.

If external impulse is negligible, total momentum is conserved whether objects bounce or stick. Their kinetic energy need not be conserved: deformation, sound and warming can receive energy.

Equal opposite momenta can cancel; positive kinetic energies cannot.

Gas pressure will reuse this idea: each collision transfers momentum, while countless collisions give a nearly steady average force on the wall.

A light trolley and a heavy trolley have equal momentum. Under equal opposing forces, do stopping time and stopping distance both match?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Stopping times match because impulse must remove equal momentum. The lighter trolley initially moves faster and travels farther during its stop, so distances differ.

Pressure comes from many changes of momentum — Wall collisions turn microscopic momentum changes into pressure.

Why kinetic energy contains speed squared — Equal momentum does not necessarily mean equal kinetic energy.

Force accumulated through time changes momentum, while the system boundary determines whether the total can stay constant.

Pressure: the same push, differently spread

Press a book against your palm, first flat and then edge-on, using the same force. The smaller contact area concentrates the push. Force alone misses that difference.

pressure p = perpendicular force F / area A

One pascal, Pa, means one newton per square metre: N/m². Pressure describes force per area; it is not an extra force alongside the contact force.

At fixed area, doubling force doubles pressure. At fixed force, doubling area halves pressure. Snowshoes spread a person’s load; a sharp blade concentrates a force.

A fluid can push in every direction

A gas pushes through countless molecular collisions. A stationary liquid also transmits pressure in all directions. At a surface, its pressure force acts perpendicular to that surface.

Why does pressure increase with depth? Imagine a vertical water column with cross-sectional area A and height h. The lower water must support the column’s weight.

Density, ρ (rho), is mass per volume: ρ = m/V, in kg/m³. Thus the column’s mass is ρAh, and its weight is ρAhg.

Divide that weight by A: the pressure increase is ρgh. The area cancels because a wider column weighs more but spreads its weight over proportionally more area.

pressure at depth = pressure at the surface + ρgh

Here g is gravitational field strength and h is vertical depth. This model uses stationary liquid of uniform density and approximately constant g.

Double depth or density, holding the other quantities fixed: double the pressure increase. At h = 0, that increase vanishes; surface pressure need not be zero.

The bottom experiences greater pressure than the top. Their forces leave an upward result: buoyancy. It equals the weight of fluid displaced.

Floating requires this upward force to balance weight; density alone is not a force.

A hollow steel boat can float although solid steel is denser than water. Its large outer volume displaces enough water before it is submerged.

For floating, count the boat and its enclosed air together.

A confined, nearly incompressible liquid can transmit an added pressure. Equal pressure on a larger piston produces a larger force because F = pA.

There is no free energy gain: ideally, the larger-force piston moves a shorter distance. Fluid-volume continuity links the two displacements.

Weight density means weight per volume: ρg, in N/m³. Mass density ρ is in kg/m³; changing gravity changes the first without changing the second.

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Force acting through a distance

Push a trolley along the floor. Your force changes its motion while the trolley moves. Physics calls this mode of energy transfer work.

Energy is tracked through motion, stored arrangements and internal changes. It can transfer between systems. Work names one such transfer, not a separate substance.

For a constant force acting along an object’s displacement, more force or more distance means more transferred energy.

work W = Fd

Here F is force in newtons and d is displacement along the force in metres. The unit is N m, called a joule, J.

At fixed force, doubling distance doubles work. At fixed distance, doubling force doubles work. The equation applies directly when force and displacement point the same way.

An opposing force does negative work: it removes kinetic energy from the object. A perpendicular force does zero work on its motion along that displacement.

For a slanting force, only its part along the displacement contributes. The sideways part changes direction instead. This is one reason force needs an arrow.

Following the energy account

If an object starts from rest and gains speed, it gains kinetic energy: energy associated with motion. Net work on an object changes its kinetic energy.

net work = change in kinetic energy

“Net” matters again. Your push may do positive work while friction does negative work. Add the work of all forces to find the kinetic energy change.

“Holding a heavy bag still must count as work.” Your muscles tire, so this seems obvious.

But the bag has no displacement: you do no mechanical work on it while holding it still. Chemical processes within your muscles still use energy and warm your body.

In ideal steady circular motion, the inward force is perpendicular to the instantaneous motion. It changes velocity’s direction without changing speed or kinetic energy.

Forces can change direction without transferring energy to that motion.

Force acting through time changes momentum; force acting through distance transfers energy. The units N s and N m distinguish impulse from work.

This particle-style work–kinetic-energy description isolates translational motion. Deformable bodies and rotation require additional parts of the energy account.

Why kinetic energy contains speed squared

Imagine identical trolleys brought to rest by the same steady braking force. One starts twice as fast. Predict the stopping distance before looking at an equation.

Same force and same mass give the same deceleration. The faster trolley has twice as much speed to lose, so it takes twice as long to stop.

During each stop, speed falls steadily to zero. The faster trolley’s average speed is also twice as large.

Twice the average speed for twice the time gives four times the distance.

The vertical axis is speed; the horizontal axis is time. Area means distance because speed × time has units of metres. Each stopping graph is a triangle.

The larger triangle is twice as high and twice as wide. Its area is four times as large. The same braking force therefore does four times as much stopping work.

Compress the reasoning into an equation

The triangle gives d = ½vt. Substituting the stopping time is allowed because it describes this same stop: d = mv²/(2F).

kinetic energy K = ½mv²

Steady braking made the graph easy to read. For ordinary speeds, ½mv² describes translational kinetic energy regardless of how the object was accelerated.

Storing energy in an arrangement

Lift a book slowly. You push upward while it moves upward, transferring energy. When released, it can regain motion. What changed was the book–Earth arrangement.

The stored energy is gravitational potential energy. Near Earth, weight mg is approximately constant. Lifting through height h against that force requires work mgh.

increase in gravitational potential energy = mgh

Here m is mass, g gravitational field strength and h the rise in height. The units kg × N/kg × m give N m, or joules.

At fixed g and height, double mass: double the increase. At fixed mass and g, double height: double it. A sideways move at the same height makes no gravitational change.

We choose a reference height where gravitational potential energy is zero. Heights below it can have negative values. The physically useful quantity is the energy difference.

A spring stores energy differently

Stretch a spring gently. The farther you stretch it, the stronger it pulls back. For an ideal spring within its elastic range, pulling-force magnitude F = kx.

Here x is extension from the relaxed length, and k is stiffness in N/m. Double x at fixed k: double the force.

The spring’s restoring force points back towards its relaxed position.

On a force–extension graph, each strip represents force × a little extension: work. The total area under the straight line is a triangle.

elastic energy = ½ × x × kx = ½kx²

The one-half appears because force rises from zero, rather than staying at kx throughout. Double extension within this model: stored energy quadruples. At zero extension it is zero.

A marble in a bowl loses gravitational potential energy as it rolls towards the bottom. At rest there, a small displacement creates a restoring tendency: stable equilibrium.

On an upside-down bowl, a small displacement carries it farther away: unstable equilibrium. Energy and force describe the same arrangement from different viewpoints.

A spring stretched too far may stay deformed. Then F = kx and ½kx² are no longer reliable. A model’s boundary matters as much as its equation.

Energy, power and efficiency

A pendulum speeds up while descending and slows while rising. Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy exchange. With negligible friction, their sum stays constant.

Real pendulums gradually stop. Air and the pivot receive energy as internal motion and sound. Mechanical energy — kinetic plus potential — decreases, while total energy remains accounted for.

For an isolated system, no energy enters or leaves, so total energy stays constant. For a system with transfers across its boundary, include those transfers in the account.

The same energy can be transferred at different rates

Two motors lift the same load to the same height. Their useful work matches. The one completing the lift sooner has greater average power.

average power P = energy transferred / time taken

One watt, W, is one joule per second. At fixed energy, halving the time doubles power. At fixed time, transferring twice the energy doubles it.

If power stays constant, energy transferred = Pt. A power–time graph’s area gives energy: add the contributions from successive intervals when power varies.

Be careful: W as a unit means watt; W as a symbol earlier meant work. The surrounding words and units tell you which is intended.

Useful for which purpose?

A lamp transfers energy into visible light and thermal energy. If our purpose is lighting, only some output is useful. The rest still exists.

efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input

Multiply by 100% to express the fraction as a percentage. The ratio has no units because both energies use the same unit. The useful purpose must be stated.

“Friction destroys energy.” Motion disappearing makes this believable. But rough surfaces deform and excite microscopic motion: they warm.

Energy is conserved; energy concentrated in organised motion becomes dispersed and harder to recover fully as useful work.

Two objects stick together in an isolated collision. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy may decrease. Ask where that energy went before calling it “lost”.

Chemical energy depends on the arrangement of atoms and electrons together with possible reaction partners. Breaking bonds costs energy; forming new interactions can release more.

Nuclear processes change nuclei rather than only electron arrangements. Some fission and fusion reactions release energy because their final products have lower total rest mass.

Later physics relates rest energy E and mass m through E = mc²; c is light speed in vacuum. Released energy corresponds to decreased system mass.

Useful output depends on purpose. Warming from a lamp is unwanted for lighting, while an electric heater is designed to warm its surroundings.

Heat is energy in transit

Rub your hands together. Their organised motion slows, yet your hands warm. Friction transfers energy into the microscopic motion and interactions of particles.

The energy associated with these microscopic motions and arrangements is internal energy. We distinguish it from a whole object’s motion across the room.

Temperature tells us about thermal state and predicts which way energy transfers spontaneously through thermal contact. At the same temperature, there is no net heat flow between objects.

For an ideal gas, temperature in kelvin is directly related to average random translational kinetic energy per particle. In solids and liquids, interactions also matter.

So “temperature is total energy” cannot be right. A large amount of warm water may have more internal energy than a tiny amount at a higher temperature.

Heat names a transfer

Bring a warm spoon into cooler water. Interactions transfer energy from spoon to water. Heat means energy transferred because of a temperature difference; its unit is the joule.

Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or kelvin. It is not measured in joules. Internal energy belongs to a system; heat describes energy crossing its boundary.

If two bodies are at different temperatures, spontaneous net transfer goes from hotter to colder. Individual microscopic exchanges occur both ways; their net effect favours that direction.

As the temperatures approach equality, the net transfer weakens. Thermal equilibrium is the state with no temperature difference driving net heat transfer.

Why a metal handle feels colder

Metal and wood in the same room can have the same temperature. Metal transfers energy away from a warm hand faster, so it often feels colder.

Touch senses the resulting energy transfer as well as temperature. It is not a reliable thermometer for comparing materials.

“Cold flows into my hand.” The sensation encourages that picture. In this situation, energy flows out of your warmer hand into the cooler object.

Cold is not a second flowing substance. To cool something, energy must be removed from its internal store.

Warming commonly increases random particle motion; melting can instead change particle arrangements while temperature stays steady. Internal energy includes both.

That distinction will explain a flat part of the heating graph.

In the ideal-gas model, raising kelvin temperature increases average translational kinetic energy, not every particle’s speed equally. A distribution of speeds still exists.

Three routes for thermal energy

Put one end of a metal spoon in hot soup. The handle warms without soup travelling through the spoon. Energy can move through material without bulk flow.

Conduction passes energy through interactions among neighbouring particles. In metals, mobile electrons also carry energy through the material. The particles themselves are never completely motionless.

With other conditions fixed, a larger temperature difference usually gives faster conduction. A thicker layer slows transfer; a larger area gives more paths for it.

Air conducts poorly. Trapping it in small pockets restricts circulation too, helping insulation work. Conductivity describes transfer; it differs from a material’s capacity to store energy.

A fluid can carry its internal energy with it

Denser surrounding water produces a greater upward buoyant force than the warmed region’s weight. The warmed water rises; cooler water moves into its place.

This circulation transfers energy by convection. It connects microscopic expansion to density, pressure, buoyancy and visible motion. The same force ideas explain the thermal effect.

Radiation crosses empty space

Sunlight reaches Earth across space. Thermal radiation carries energy as electromagnetic waves; it needs no material medium. You will meet these waves again in optics.

All ordinary objects emit and absorb radiation. Hotter objects generally emit more intensely. Net exchange with surroundings depends on both temperatures and surface properties.

Dark, matt surfaces often absorb and emit thermal radiation effectively. Polished metallic surfaces often reflect it. Visible colour alone does not determine every infrared property.

The vacuum gap greatly reduces conduction and prevents convection across the gap. Reflective walls reduce radiative transfer. A stopper reduces transfer through the opening.

The contents still cool eventually because the insulation is not perfect.

energy for the change of state Q = mL

An oscillation passed from place to place

Displace a hanging mass on a spring and release it. The spring pulls it back. At the middle, its velocity carries it onwards before the spring slows it.

A restoring force and inertia together produce repeated motion, called an oscillation. Without damping, energy moves between kinetic and stored forms. Damping transfers energy to the surroundings.

Now connect many neighbouring particles. Disturb one: its interaction with the next passes the disturbance along. A wave is a travelling pattern of disturbance.

Each point of a rope moves mainly up and down while the wave travels sideways. The movement of the pattern differs from the movement of the material.

In this simple model, energy travels along the rope without the rope travelling along with it. Real waves may also produce secondary flows; the basic distinction remains useful.

Two directions to compare

In a transverse wave, disturbance is perpendicular to travel: the rope moves up and down while the pattern moves horizontally.

In a longitudinal wave, disturbance is parallel to travel. Push and pull one end of a spring: crowded and spread-out regions travel along it.

Sound in air is longitudinal. Air particles oscillate backwards and forwards as compression patterns travel. Water surface waves are more complicated: particles generally move in loops.

Does every wave need a substance?

Rope and sound waves need a medium, the material carrying the disturbance. Its inertia and restoring interactions together determine how quickly the disturbance propagates.

Electromagnetic waves, including light, can travel through a vacuum. Their changing electric and magnetic fields carry energy; no material chain of particles is required.

“If the wave reaches the far end, the original particles must reach it.” Watching a moving crest encourages this. Mark one rope point: it moves locally as the crest passes.

Waves grow naturally from the force and energy ideas already developed.

Photograph a repeating rope wave. The distance from one crest to the next is a spacing in space. Watching one marked point gives a repeating pattern in time.

Wavelength, λ (lambda), is distance between successive matching points, in metres. Period, T, is the time for one complete cycle, in seconds.

Amplitude, A, is the largest displacement from equilibrium. For a rope, it is measured in metres. Peak-to-trough height is twice the amplitude.

Build the speed relationship

During one cycle, the repeating pattern advances one wavelength. Distance travelled by the pattern divided by time gives its speed:

wave speed v = λ/T = fλ

Read fλ as “cycles each second × metres in each cycle”. The result is metres per second. The v here is pattern speed, not particle speed.

For a given rope tension and mass per length, small waves travel at approximately fixed speed. Driving twice as frequently then produces half the wavelength.

Do not hold v, f and λ independently fixed. If f doubles while v remains fixed, λ must halve. The equation constrains how the quantities fit together.

Sound is a pressure pattern

A loudspeaker cone moves forwards. It crowds nearby air slightly, raising pressure. That air pushes on the next region, so the compression travels away.

When the cone moves backwards, a lower-pressure region follows. Compression means pressure and density above their undisturbed values; rarefaction means below them.

Sound needs matter: a gas, liquid or solid can carry suitable disturbances. A vacuum has no material particles and interactions to carry ordinary sound.

What sets the speed?

Compressing matter produces a restoring response. Greater stiffness tends to pass a disturbance on more rapidly; greater inertia tends to resist rapid changes.

The balance of these properties determines sound speed. “Particles are closer together” is not a complete explanation. Sound often travels faster in solids, but density alone cannot predict it.

For small sound waves in unchanged air, making the source louder or raising its pitch does not significantly change travel speed. Frequency changes wavelength instead.

Why an echo needs a round trip

distance to reflector d = vt/2

Here v is sound speed in the medium and t the round-trip time. Metres per second multiplied by seconds gives metres; the two divides out the return journey.

With sound speed fixed, twice the delay means twice the distance. If the reflector is very close, the return may blend into the original sound instead of sounding separate.

A bat emits sound and uses returning echoes to learn about objects. Sonar follows the same travel-time idea in water.

The method needs the relevant sound speed; the speed in air cannot simply be reused in water.

Why can sound pass through still air? “Still” describes the air’s average bulk motion. Its particles can still oscillate locally, and those oscillations transmit a changing pressure.

No steady wind from speaker to ear is needed.

Pitch, loudness and resonance

A tuning fork vibrates rapidly, producing repeating pressure changes. The number of cycles per second largely determines pitch: higher frequency is usually heard as a higher note.

How large those pressure changes are helps determine loudness. Amplitude and frequency answer different questions: how strong each disturbance is, and how often it repeats.

For a simple travelling sound wave in the same medium, intensity is proportional to pressure-amplitude squared. Double that amplitude and intensity becomes four times as large.

The square echoes kinetic energy: greater particle motion carries disproportionately more energy. This relationship concerns small waves and fixed medium properties.

Why decibels do not count evenly

A sound intensity level in decibels, dB, compares intensity with a reference using a compressed scale. Adding 10 dB means ten times the intensity.

Another increase of 10 dB multiplies by ten again. This is a ratio scale, not “ten extra units” of intensity. Zero dB marks the reference, not total silence.

Resonance: energy supplied with the right timing

A child on a swing moves at a natural rhythm. Small pushes timed with the motion add energy repeatedly. Poorly timed pushes may remove energy instead.

A vibrating system driven near one of its natural frequencies can build a large response: resonance. Energy accumulates because the driving force repeatedly does positive work.

Damping removes energy as the system oscillates. The final amplitude reflects the balance between energy supplied and energy dissipated; resonance does not create energy.

Air in instruments and strings have natural vibration patterns. Resonance helps select strong notes. A vibrating surface can also transfer energy to surrounding air more effectively.

Why a passing siren changes pitch

Imagine a siren emitting successive wavefronts at equal time intervals. If it stays still, each front expands around the same position.

Now move the siren forwards. Before releasing the next wavefront, it has moved closer to the one ahead. Fronts become crowded in front and spread out behind.

In still air, the sound travels at the air’s sound speed. The source’s motion changes the spacing between successive fronts, not that speed through the air.

An observer ahead meets the closer fronts more frequently: a higher received frequency. An observer behind meets wider-spaced fronts less frequently: a lower received frequency.

This shift in received frequency because of relative motion is the Doppler effect. The source can keep vibrating at the same frequency throughout.

A moving listener provides a second route

Keep the source still and move towards it. You meet oncoming wavefronts more often, even though their spacing in the air is unchanged.

Move away and you meet them less often. For sound, motion of source and listener relative to the medium matters; their effects have different detailed formulas.

We do not need those formulas to predict the basic result: approaching usually raises received frequency; separating usually lowers it, for ordinary motion along the sound’s path.

What the changed note tells you

An approaching siren is heard higher than its emitted note; a receding one lower. Passing the observer changes the direction of approach into retreat.

Loudness may also change because distance and sound direction change. That is a separate effect: getting louder does not cause the Doppler frequency shift.

At the listener, v = fλ still fits the travelling sound in stationary air. Closer fronts mean smaller λ and higher received f.

The siren’s emission rate and the listener’s encounter rate are different quantities.

Walk alongside a slowly moving siren at the same speed, keeping separation fixed in still air.

Their source and listener effects cancel: you meet the emitted cycles at their emission rate. Relative motion and “events per second” are the ideas doing the work.

Light travels; mirrors redirect it

You see a lamp because light reaches your eyes. You see a book because it redirects some incident light towards you. Seeing needs a path into the eye.

Light is an electromagnetic wave. In vacuum it travels at c, about 300 million metres per second (3 × 10⁸ m/s). It needs no material medium.

Visible light occupies a small frequency range. From lower to higher frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.

The same vacuum speed applies to all these waves. Since c = fλ, higher frequency means shorter wavelength. Different frequencies interact differently with matter.

Reflection and the normal line

A ray marks light’s travel direction. At a reflecting surface, draw a line perpendicular to the surface: the normal. Measure both ray angles from that line.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection

Rough surfaces also reflect, but their differently tilted patches send rays in different directions. A smooth mirror preserves an orderly pattern of reflected directions.

Your eye interprets incoming light as travelling straight. Extend reflected rays backwards on paper: they seem to meet behind a plane mirror.

That apparent source is a virtual image. Light does not actually meet behind the mirror. The image is the same size and distance behind as the object is in front.

The usual “left–right reversal” describes how we compare ourselves with the image. Geometrically, a plane mirror reverses the direction perpendicular to its surface: front versus back.

Curved mirrors change the pattern

A concave mirror, curving inward, brings rays parallel to its axis towards a focus. Far objects can form real, inverted images; objects inside the focal distance give enlarged virtual images.

A convex mirror, bulging outward, spreads rays. They appear to come from a smaller upright virtual image behind the mirror, giving a wider field of view.

A real image forms where rays actually meet and can be caught on a screen. A virtual image forms where their backward extensions meet.

“Virtual” means apparent ray origin; it does not mean the image cannot be seen.

Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma radiation belong to the electromagnetic spectrum. Across this sequence, typical frequency rises while wavelength decreases.

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in vacuum. In matter, propagation and absorption depend on both material and frequency.

Microwave heating involves interaction with materials, including changing polar-molecule orientations and mobile charges. Infrared often excites molecular vibrations. Neither is explained by wavelength alone.

Higher frequency means greater energy per photon, but brightness also depends on how many photons arrive. Later spectroscopy links these exchanges to chemical structure.

Refraction and lenses

A wavefront enters glass at an angle. The part entering first slows first, turning the front. Light’s direction bends towards the normal. This is refraction.

Refractive index, n, compares vacuum speed c with light speed v in the material:

n = c/v

The speeds’ units cancel. Greater n means slower light in that material. This is an optical property, not simply mass density. Frequency stays unchanged at a stationary boundary.

Entering a higher-index medium obliquely bends light towards the normal; entering a lower-index medium bends it away. At normal incidence, speed changes without a direction change.

Since v = fλ, lower speed at unchanged frequency means shorter wavelength. Refraction therefore connects a ray’s direction with a wave’s spacing.

Two surfaces can work together

A glass convex lens in air is thicker in the middle. Refraction at its surfaces bends parallel incoming rays towards a focus: it is converging.

A concave lens is thinner in the middle and spreads incoming parallel rays: it is diverging. Their backward extensions meet at a virtual focus.

Focal length is the distance from a thin lens’s centre to its focus. Shorter focal length means stronger convergence or divergence for parallel incident rays.

A converging lens can make a real inverted image on a camera sensor or projector screen.

Place an object within its focal length and it acts as a magnifier, producing an upright virtual image.

The eye’s cornea and lens together focus light on the retina. Changing the lens’s shape adjusts focus as object distance changes.

In a short-sighted eye, distant light focuses in front of the retina; a diverging lens reduces convergence.

In a long-sighted eye, the available convergence is insufficient in relevant viewing conditions; a converging lens helps focus onto the retina.

The water did not move the bottom; it changed the ray paths.

The constant h sets the size of the relation; its unit J s converts cycles per second into joules. The relationship is experimental physics, not ordinary geometry.

Charge, interaction and energy per charge

Rub a balloon against hair. Electrons may transfer between them. An object gaining electrons becomes negatively charged; losing them leaves a positive imbalance.

Charge is measured in coulombs, C. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. An overall neutral object contains positive and negative charges whose totals balance.

Charge is conserved: transfer changes where it is, not the total of an isolated system. In this example, protons remain bound inside atomic nuclei.

The balloon can attract a neutral wall because charges in the wall shift slightly. Opposite charge is drawn nearer; the nearer attraction can exceed the more distant repulsion.

A field separates source from test object

Earth produces a gravitational field; a mass placed there feels a force. Electric charges similarly produce an electric field; another charge responds to it.

electric field strength E = force on a positive test charge / its charge

Its unit N/C means force per coulomb. Field direction is the force direction on a positive charge. A negative charge experiences the opposite force direction.

The test charge must be small enough not to change the source arrangement appreciably. At fixed field, doubling the test charge doubles force. Field and force are not interchangeable.

Voltage is an energy comparison

Moving charge between two places can change electric potential energy. Potential difference, or voltage, is that energy change per charge, measured in J/C, called volts.

potential difference = change in electric potential energy / charge

One volt means one joule per coulomb. Double the transferred charge at the same voltage difference: double the associated energy, not the voltage.

Like gravitational height, electric potential needs a reference; potential differences are what matter for transfers. Electric potential energy belongs to the interacting charge arrangement.

Charge: how much electric quantity? Field: what force would each positive coulomb experience here? Potential difference: how much energy changes per coulomb between these places?

A battery uses chemical processes to maintain a potential difference between its terminals.

Humid conditions often let surface charge leak away more readily through adsorbed moisture. Static charging depends on material and surroundings, not just how vigorously you rub.

An electric field commonly points towards decreasing electric potential. A steeper potential change across distance means a stronger field in that direction.

This is another gradient: how much a quantity changes from place to place. In a uniform field along a line, field size equals voltage change divided by distance.

The units V/m equal N/C. A volt is a joule per coulomb, and a joule is a newton metre, so the two field descriptions agree.

No. The same electric field produces opposite force directions on opposite charges. Field direction is defined using a positive test charge.

Electron transfer creates ions; attraction binds the lattice — Electric attraction holds many oppositely charged ions in a lattice.

Field describes force per test charge at a point; voltage describes energy difference per charge between points.

Current, voltage and resistance

Choose a cross-section of a wire and count the charge passing it. More charge in the same time means a larger current.

average current I = charge Q / time t

One ampere, A, means one coulomb per second. Twice the charge in the same time gives twice the average current. The same charge taking twice as long gives half.

In a metal, mobile electrons drift through the material. Conventional current points in the direction positive charge would move; electron drift is opposite.

Closing a circuit establishes an electric field through it. Electrons already throughout the wire respond; a lamp need not await electrons travelling all the way from the battery.

Voltage and current answer different questions

Voltage V measures energy transferred per charge across a component. Current I measures charge passing per second.

A battery can have a terminal voltage while an open circuit has zero current.

In a resistor, electrons interact with the material, transferring energy to it. This impedes drift. Resistance compares the voltage across a component with its current.

resistance R = V/I; equivalently V = IR

The unit ohm, Ω, means volt per ampere. Larger R means less current for the same applied voltage. At fixed voltage, doubling resistance halves current.

Ohm’s law makes an additional physical claim: for an ohmic conductor under fixed conditions, especially temperature, current is proportional to voltage, so R stays constant.

On this I–V graph, voltage increases rightwards and current upwards. A straight line through the origin shows that proportionality. Its slope is 1/R; a steeper line means lower resistance.

“Every component has one fixed resistance.” A simple resistor encourages this model. But a hot filament changes resistance, and a diode or LED has a strongly non-proportional I–V relationship.

One measured V/I ratio does not predict its behaviour at every voltage.

A circulating flow helps picture charge per second; a pump helps picture energy supplied. But voltage is energy per charge, not literal pressure, and electrons are not consumed like fuel.

Circuits conserve charge and energy

Join two resistors end-to-end with a battery. In a steady state, charge cannot continually pile up between them. The same current must pass through both.

This is a series circuit: one path. Across each resistor, electrical energy is transferred. Around the loop, total energy supplied per charge matches total energy transferred per charge.

series: I is the same; Vtotal = V₁ + V₂

For resistors, substitute V = IR. Since the same I multiplies both resistances, Rtotal = R₁ + R₂. More series resistance gives less current at fixed supply voltage.

Parallel paths share endpoints

Now connect each resistor across the same two battery terminals. Both span the same potential difference. This is a parallel circuit.

In steady operation, charge entering a junction each second equals charge leaving. Therefore:

parallel: V is the same; Itotal = I₁ + I₂

At fixed terminal voltage, lower-resistance branches carry more current. Adding a branch increases total current, lowering equivalent resistance.

Existing branches retain their voltage if the supply maintains it. Real batteries have internal resistance, so heavy loading can reduce terminal voltage and dim lamps.

The battery supplies energy, not fresh charge

Charge circulates around the complete conducting loop. A component receives energy while passing charge onwards. Current is not gradually “used up” through successive components.

“The first lamp gets more current because it is nearer the battery.” This resembles consuming fuel along a route. But sustained unequal currents would accumulate charge between lamps.

In a steady series circuit, current is the same throughout; energy per charge is shared between the components.

Household appliances use parallel branches so each can receive the supply voltage and be switched independently. An open break in one branch need not stop the others.

A short circuit is different: a very low-resistance path can draw excessive current and activate protection.

The current rule at a junction expresses conservation of charge. The voltage rule around a loop expresses conservation of energy. Circuit rules grow from ideas already used in mechanics.

Electrical power: two “per” quantities multiply

A battery transfers V joules of energy per coulomb. A current of I amperes carries I coulombs per second. Multiply the two rates of comparison.

power P = IV

The units explain why: (C/s) × (J/C) = J/s. The coulombs cancel, leaving watts. Voltage says energy per charge; current says charge per time.

At fixed current, doubling voltage doubles power. At fixed voltage, doubling current doubles power. For constant power, energy transferred in time t is E = Pt.

An electricity meter totals energy, not instantaneous power. A kilowatt-hour, kWh, is the energy transferred by 1 kW for one hour; it equals 3.6 million joules.

Two forms that need their conditions stated

For a resistor obeying V = IR, replace V in P = IV. This gives P = I²R. At fixed R, twice the current gives four times the heating power.

Alternatively use I = V/R, giving P = V²/R. At fixed V, twice the resistance halves the power. These are compatible statements under different fixed conditions.

Microscopic interactions turn electrical energy into internal energy in the resistor. A heater exploits this; supply wires generally should minimise this unwanted transfer.

Why transmit at high voltage?

To deliver the same power, increasing voltage allows smaller current because P = IV. Smaller current means less heating of the same wires, following I²R.

This comparison holds transmitted power and wire resistance fixed. It does not mean high voltage always makes every device produce less heat.

Magnetic fields and electromagnets

A compass turns near a magnet. The small magnet in the compass responds differently at different places. A magnetic field describes that local influence.

Draw an arrow showing where the compass’s north-seeking end points. Repeat at nearby positions. Joining those directions produces a field-line diagram.

Lines are our representation, not material threads. Their direction shows field direction; closer spacing conventionally represents greater strength. Outside a bar magnet, lines run from north to south.

A bar magnet has two poles. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Cut an ordinary bar magnet and each piece has both a north and a south pole.

Magnetised materials contain many microscopic magnetic contributions aligned enough to reinforce. Cutting the material divides these aligned regions; it does not detach an isolated pole.

Current produces a magnetic field

A compass near a current-carrying wire turns. Around a straight wire, the magnetic field forms circles. Reverse the current and the field direction reverses.

Wind the wire into a coil. The turns’ fields reinforce inside, producing a bar-magnet-like pattern. Such a coil is called a solenoid.

An iron core can strengthen the field by aligning magnetic regions. More current or more turns per length generally strengthens a similar coil, until material limitations matter.

An electromagnet can lift suitable iron or steel objects when energised.

A doorbell uses a related cycle: current attracts an arm, contact breaks, a spring returns the arm, and contact closes again.

The circuit changes its own motion, which changes the circuit: feedback.

Earth’s field helps align a compass approximately north–south. The north-seeking end points towards the northern geographic region, which behaves magnetically like a south pole.

Geographic names and magnetic attraction must be kept distinct.

Electric fields act on charge. Magnetic fields act on moving charges and magnetic objects.

A compass maps field direction, but that arrow is not automatically the force direction on every moving charged particle. Different interactions need their own force rules.

An unmagnetised piece of iron can contain locally aligned magnetic regions pointing in different directions. A net magnetisation appears when their contributions reinforce sufficiently.

Why might cutting a magnet into smaller pieces reduce each piece’s overall effect without producing isolated north and south poles?

Motors and generators

A current-carrying wire placed suitably in a magnetic field experiences a force. The field acts on moving charges; their interaction with the wire transfers the force to it.

Force direction depends on both current and field directions. Reversing either reverses the force; a wire parallel to the field has no magnetic force from this effect.

A motor uses a turning pair

In opposite sides of a coil, currents run opposite ways. A suitably directed magnetic field gives opposite forces on those sides. Because they act at different places, they produce torque.

Remember the door: force at a distance from an axis can turn an object. The coil’s forces can have zero net push while their turning effects reinforce.

A simple DC motor reverses coil current each half-turn using a split-ring contact, a commutator, so the useful torque continues in the same rotational direction.

Electrical input supplies mechanical output and internal-energy increases. A magnetic field helps direct the interaction; it is not a source of unlimited motor energy.

A generator changes the magnetic situation

Move a magnet relative to a coil, or turn a coil in a magnetic field. A changing magnetic field through the coil can produce an induced voltage: electromagnetic induction.

A complete conducting path allows that voltage to drive current. An open coil can have voltage without a sustained current.

Mere motion is insufficient if the magnetic situation does not change appropriately.

Faster change or more coil turns generally gives a larger induced voltage. Reversing the change reverses its direction.

The induced current’s magnetic effect opposes the change producing it: Lenz’s law. A generator supplying a load resists the driving motion. Mechanical work supplies electrical energy, with losses.

If induction helped its own cause without an energy source, the energy account would fail.

In regenerative braking, a moving vehicle drives its motor as a generator. The vehicle’s kinetic energy supplies electrical energy, and the opposing torque slows it.

The motor need not rotate backwards; the direction of energy transfer changes.

A loudspeaker uses force on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field. Changing current moves the attached cone, producing the pressure waves studied in sound.

A changing current in a nearby stationary coil can also induce voltage. Physical movement is one way to change the magnetic situation, not an essential requirement.

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Induced voltage can remain. Opening the path suppresses load current and output power, though friction and other losses can remain.

Transformers: changing voltage

Place two coils around a shared iron core. A changing current in the first changes the magnetic field through both. The second coil receives an induced voltage.

The first is the primary; the second is the secondary. This arrangement is a transformer. The core guides a large part of the magnetic field through both coils.

If each turn links approximately the same changing magnetic field, each receives the same induced voltage per turn. More turns add more of these contributions.

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np

Here V means voltage and N number of turns; subscripts s and p mean secondary and primary. This is the ideal transformer relation for corresponding AC voltage measures.

Twice as many secondary turns gives twice the secondary voltage. Fewer secondary turns gives a smaller voltage. Turns are counts, so both ratios have no units.

Higher voltage does not mean extra energy

For an ideal transformer powering a resistor, input and output powers match. Use effective AC values — values giving the same heating as DC:

VpIp = VsIs

At the same transferred power, stepping voltage up steps current down. Real transformers also transfer some input energy into heating and other losses.

Alternating current, AC, continually changes direction and magnetic field. A steady direct current, DC, gives no sustained induction in the secondary after switching effects end.

Follow one connected energy story

A turbine turns a generator. A transformer raises voltage for lower-current transmission. Wires then waste less power through I²R heating. Other transformers reduce voltage near users.

Phone chargers add switching and control circuits. Induction cooktops use changing fields to drive currents that heat a pan; the pan can warm the cooktop.

Connect a motor to a generator and feed the generator’s output back. Could it keep producing surplus energy?

No: even ideal conversion gives no extra energy, and real losses leave less useful output than input. Following each transfer exposes the mistake.

Interactions change motion or energy; equations connect their sizes, directions and rates, while conservation keeps the complete account consistent.

Photovoltaic solar cells convert absorbed light through electronic processes; they do not require a spinning generator. Different devices can connect the same energy domains by different mechanisms.

High-voltage DC transmission is also possible with suitable conversion electronics. A conventional transformer’s need for changing flux does not mean DC can never transmit power.

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

A phone: a small circuit with several energy destinations — A charger combines conversion, control and chemical energy storage.

Identity, charge and mass answer different questions

A copper wire can bend, melt or carry current while remaining copper. Something persists through those changes: the identity of its atoms.

An atom has a tiny nucleus (central region) containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons. Negatively charged electrons occupy the surrounding region.

The number of protons defines the element. Six means carbon; eight means oxygen. Changing the proton number changes the element, not merely its charge.

atomic number Z = number of protons

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Remove an electron and positive charge exceeds negative charge: the atom becomes a positive ion.

An ion (an atom or group with a net charge) forms through an imbalance, not through a proton becoming “more positive”.

charge in elementary units = proton count − electron count

Neutrons alter mass and nuclear behaviour without adding electric charge. Atoms of the same element with different neutron counts are isotopes (versions with different nuclear masses).

mass number A = protons + neutrons

The mass number counts particles; it is not a mass measured in kilograms. Subtracting Z from A gives the neutron count because the protons were counted in both.

Most atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus. The electron region occupies nearly all the volume. Neither statement means matter should pass freely through other matter.

A short-range nuclear interaction binds the nucleus. Electric repulsion between protons opposes that binding; neutron count can therefore change nuclear stability without changing electron arrangements.

When atoms approach, electron arrangements and electrical interactions resist further compression. Quantum rules also matter; “mostly empty” does not mean “no interactions”.

An electron cloud is a map of where electrons can be found. It is not a fuzzy material fog or a miniature solar system.

Ordinary reactions rearrange electrons and atomic partners while preserving nuclei. Radioactive decay changes nuclei and can change the element. This separates chemistry from nuclear processes.

Isotopes usually have closely similar chemistry because their electron structures match. Their differing masses can still produce measurable chemical differences, so “identical chemistry” is too absolute.

Carbon-14 is radioactive. After a once-living sample stops exchanging carbon with its surroundings, its changing isotope ratio can help estimate elapsed time under suitable assumptions.

Dating requires an initial-ratio model and checks for contamination or later exchange. Chemical similarity explains how the isotope enters organisms; nuclear instability supplies the clock.

A particle gains one neutron; another loses one electron. Which might become a new isotope, and which becomes more positively charged? Has either necessarily become a new element?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Adding a neutron changes the isotope but not proton number. Losing an electron increases positive charge but keeps elemental identity. Neither operation, by itself, changes the element.

Charge, interaction and energy per charge — Electric charge explains the forces between nuclei and electrons; mass and charge remain different properties.

Repeated multiplication needs a different scale — A radioactive population can have a predictable half-life without a predictable decay time for one nucleus.

Proton count fixes element identity, neutron count distinguishes isotopes, and electron imbalance sets ionic charge.

A compound contains different elements chemically combined in a characteristic ratio. Water molecules contain two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom.

H₂O describes the contents of one water molecule

Counting particles by weighing them

A formula counts atoms, but a balance measures mass. Chemistry needs a bridge between the invisible count and the measurable sample.

Imagine identical beads. Knowing the mass of each bead lets you infer how many beads a bag contains. The bag need not be opened.

For particles, the numbers are enormous. A mole, mol, is a counting unit: one mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities.

Like a dozen, a mole specifies a count. Unlike a dozen, its size suits atomic samples. Always name the entities: atoms, molecules, ions or formula units.

Different entities have different masses. One mole of carbon atoms and one mole of water molecules contain the same number of entities but have different masses.

Molar mass M means mass per mole, commonly in g/mol. Multiplying by the amount n in moles rebuilds the sample's mass.

mass m = amount n × molar mass M

The units show why: mol × g/mol = g. Rearranging to n = m/M means dividing total mass by the mass of one counting portion.

At fixed substance, doubling mass doubles the amount of particles. At fixed mass, heavier particles mean fewer particles.

Chemical equations use ratios of particle counts, not equal masses. Two hydrogen molecules react with one oxygen molecule to form two water molecules.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

The large coefficient multiplies the whole formula. Two H₂O molecules contain four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. Both element counts match the left side.

The same ratio works for two moles of H₂ and one mole of O₂. Multiplying every count by one common large number preserves the ratio.

You do not need numerical mole exercises yet. Keep the bridge: formulas count particles; molar mass connects those counts to what a balance can measure.

Equal masses of two pure molecular gases are placed in separate identical containers. Must they contain equal numbers of molecules?

“Per” compares equal portions — Molar mass is another meaningful “per” quantity.

Why properties repeat

Arrange neutral atoms by increasing proton number. Each step also adds an electron. The arrangement of those electrons changes in an organised pattern.

Electrons occupy allowed energy states. A shell (a group of related electron states) is a useful introductory way to organise those states.

Outer electrons interact most directly with other atoms. When similar outer arrangements recur, similar bonding and reaction patterns often recur too.

That repetition is periodicity (a repeating pattern). The periodic table places related patterns into columns called groups; rows are called periods.

Lithium and sodium differ in total electron count, but each has one outer electron in the simple shell model. Their related chemistry is therefore unsurprising.

For the early main-group elements, a useful count is 2 electrons in the first shell, then 8 in the next. Later filling becomes more complex.

Do not turn this model into “every shell holds eight”. Nor does moving to the next period mean all lower-energy possibilities have universally filled.

Group 1 contains alkali metals, excluding hydrogen's distinctive chemistry. Group 17 contains halogens. Group 18 contains noble gases.

Some school books call the halogens Group 7 and noble gases Group 0. This guide uses the modern 1–18 numbering.

Noble gases generally react little under ordinary conditions. A full outer arrangement is a useful pattern associated with stability; it is not a desire inside an atom.

The table also shows metals mainly on the left and centre, non-metals mainly towards the upper right, and intermediate behaviour around the boundary.

Similar patterns suggest predictions, not guarantees about every property. Members of one group can differ in colour, physical state and detailed reactivity.

Mendeleev used recurring properties to leave gaps and predict undiscovered elements. Later observations tested those predictions; organising known facts became a way to discover missing ones.

The same allowed electron energies also affect the light an atom exchanges. Periodic patterns and spectral lines are two ways of observing electron structure.

A noble gas can condense into a liquid under suitable conditions despite reacting very little. Does this contradict its chemical stability?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

No. Condensation brings atoms into a different physical arrangement without requiring ordinary new chemical bonds. Weak between-particle attractions can matter even when chemical reaction is difficult.

State change and reaction answer different questions.

Recurring outer-electron arrangements produce recurring chemical patterns, which the periodic table organises into related families.

Distance, shielding and opposite trends

Hold two magnets near one another, then separate them. The interaction weakens with separation. This illustrates distance dependence, though atoms require a different, quantum model.

In an atom, negatively charged electrons are attracted towards the positive nucleus. Inner electrons partly reduce the nucleus's pull felt by outer electrons: shielding.

Moving down a main group adds occupied shells. The outer electrons are typically farther from the nucleus and more shielded.

An alkali metal often reacts by losing its outer electron as part of a complete reaction. Weaker retention helps explain the increasing reactivity trend down common members.

A halogen often reacts by gaining electron density or an electron. Greater size and shielding generally weaken its ability to attract an incoming electron.

Thus common halogens show the opposite group trend. “Lower in the table” has no universal meaning until we identify whether gaining or losing is relevant.

Across an early period, nuclear charge increases while added electrons occupy the same broad outer shell. The increased attraction generally draws the electron distribution inward.

That helps explain decreasing atomic size across the period. It is a trend under a model, not a ruler marking sharply bounded balls.

Reaction tendency depends on the full energy account: electron removal, electron gain, bond formation, solvation and conditions. One factor rarely predicts every reaction alone.

Rate is different again. Aluminium has a strong tendency to react but can react slowly because a protective oxide layer blocks access.

Patterns become powerful when tested against new cases. A successful prediction supports the model; an exception tells us which missing factor needs attention.

Avoid extrapolating an ordinary trend into an untested dramatic claim. The most unfamiliar or unstable elements can require a more detailed model.

A metal appears unreactive until its surface film is removed. Does that observation contradict the idea that its reactions can be energetically favourable?

A bond is a stable interacting arrangement

Imagine separating two attracting magnets. You must transfer energy to their arrangement. Allow them to approach again, and that stored energy can transfer elsewhere.

Atoms are not magnets, but the energy comparison carries over. Pulling bonded atoms apart usually requires energy; forming the corresponding stable bond releases energy.

As two atoms approach, attractions and repulsions compete. Attraction alone would pull them together indefinitely; short-range repulsion prevents collapse.

Their combined energy can have a minimum at a particular separation. A small displacement from that minimum creates a restoring tendency towards the preferred spacing.

This stable arrangement is a chemical bond. The minimum is an energy balance, not a decision made by atoms seeking happiness or complete shells.

On an energy–separation graph, moving right means farther apart. Moving up means greater energy of the interacting arrangement.

Far-apart atoms provide a convenient zero reference. A bonded arrangement below that reference has negative potential energy; “negative” means below the chosen zero.

To separate the atoms completely, supply the energy difference back up to the distant reference. That is why breaking a bond costs energy.

Pushing the atoms too close also raises the energy, so they resist compression. Around the minimum, small vibrations can resemble a mass on a spring.

The spring picture explains a local restoring response. It does not mean a chemical bond is a literal wire, or that it stretches proportionally without limit.

Electron behaviour determines the detailed energy curve. Shell-filling rules help predict many simple structures; they summarise patterns rather than replacing the force-and-energy explanation.

One crucial distinction follows: bond strength concerns separation energy; reaction energy compares complete initial and final arrangements, containing many interactions.

A reaction breaks several strong bonds but still releases energy overall. What must be true of the complete set of interactions formed?

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

Formation of the new interactions must release more energy than the initial separation and rearrangement require. “Strong bonds break” alone cannot determine the net result.

total positive charge + total negative charge = 0

Shared electrons can make molecules or networks

Two hydrogen atoms can form a stable arrangement with electron density between their nuclei. Both positive nuclei attract that shared negative charge.

This is covalent bonding (bonding involving shared electron density). A drawn line represents a bond; it is not a miniature stick between solid balls.

Small connected groups form molecules, such as H₂O or CH₄. Covalent bonds can also continue throughout a giant network, as in diamond.

Heating a simple molecular substance usually separates molecules from one another. It need not break the strong covalent bonds inside each molecule.

Even non-polar molecules attract. Temporary uneven electron distributions can induce unevenness in neighbours, producing dispersion attractions. Larger, more easily distorted electron clouds often interact more strongly.

That distinction explains why a substance can have strong internal bonds yet a modest boiling point. Ask which interactions the change must overcome.

In diamond, there are no separate small molecules to release. Deforming or disrupting the giant network involves its extended covalent structure.

Graphite has strong bonding within sheets but weaker interactions between them. Mobile electrons within its structure also explain why this carbon material can conduct electricity.

The shape of charge matters

In water, oxygen attracts shared electron density more strongly than hydrogen. Oxygen's side is partly negative; the hydrogen sides are partly positive.

The bent molecular shape keeps these effects from cancelling. The molecule is polar: its electric charge is unevenly distributed even though its total charge is zero.

Neighbouring water molecules attract through their differently charged regions. Particularly significant attractions involving hydrogen are called hydrogen bonds.

Hydrogen bonds between molecules are different from the covalent O–H bonds within one molecule. Boiling water mainly changes the former arrangement while preserving H₂O molecules.

Carbon dioxide has polar bonds but a symmetrical linear structure. Their effects cancel, so the whole molecule has no permanent electric dipole of this kind.

Geometry therefore belongs inside chemistry: the same local charge effects can reinforce or cancel depending on their directions.

In the diagrams, arrows point towards each bond's electron-rich end. They represent bond polarity, not forces or electron trajectories.

The barrier and the destination are different

A fuel can sit beside oxygen without immediately burning. Yet once ignition starts a suitable reaction, energy can transfer rapidly into the surroundings.

Two questions need separate answers. Is the final arrangement lower in energy? And can particles reach a pathway that connects the initial and final arrangements?

Separating initial bonds costs energy. Forming new bonds releases energy. The difference between these contributions determines the overall reaction energy in a simple bond account.

energy change ≈ energy needed to break bonds − energy released by forming bonds

For gaseous molecules, average bond energies give an estimate. Real reactions may also involve lattices, solutions and changes of state, which require their own contributions.

If formation releases more than separation requires, products finish lower in energy. Energy transfers out: the reaction is exothermic.

If the final arrangement is higher, energy must come from elsewhere: the reaction is endothermic. An overall change can be positive even though some steps release energy.

ΔE = final energy − initial energy

Δ means “change in”. Negative ΔE means the chosen chemical system finishes lower in energy; positive ΔE means it finishes higher. Total energy still balances.

A reaction-energy diagram moves from reactants on the left to products on the right. The horizontal coordinate represents reaction progress, not necessarily elapsed time.

The peak marks an energy barrier. Activation energy is the rise from reactants to the barrier's top, not the difference between reactants and products.

A catalyst provides another pathway with a lower barrier. It can speed the approach to the same final equilibrium without changing the reactant–product energy difference.

The catalyst participates in steps but is regenerated overall. It is not an energy source, and it does not make unlimited product from a fixed supply.

Tempting idea: “Fuel releases energy because its bonds break.” It sounds plausible because the fuel disappears. But breaking alone costs energy; new interactions complete the explanation.

A downhill-looking energy diagram also cannot predict every spontaneous change. Later chemistry includes particle dispersal and temperature; this diagram isolates the energy account.

Open the reasoning after making a prediction

No. With the same initial and final states, the energy difference stays the same. The transfer can happen faster, so power can increase without increasing total energy.

How much changes, and how quickly?

Imagine collecting gas from a reaction without letting it leak. The collected volume grows rapidly at first, then more slowly, and finally stops changing.

The total tells us how much gas has formed. Its rate tells us how quickly further gas is forming. A large total can coexist with zero present rate.

average reaction rate = change in measured amount / elapsed time

The unit depends on what we measure: moles per second, mass per second, or gas volume per second under fixed pressure and temperature.

On a product-amount–time graph, slope represents production rate. A steeper rising curve means faster production; a horizontal ending means no further net increase.

For a reactant remaining, the graph slopes down. Its negative slope describes disappearance; we often report the positive magnitude as the consumption rate.

Why should rate change? Particles must encounter one another in a suitable arrangement, with a pathway allowing their electron and bond structures to change.

Higher concentration often produces more encounters per second. For a reacting solid, more exposed surface lets more surrounding particles reach it at once.

A catalyst changes the pathway. It does not simply shove particles faster, increase the available reactant amount or raise the energy released per completed reaction.

No visible change can hide two ongoing processes

Put water in a closed container with some space above it. Molecules escape the liquid surface while vapour molecules also return to it.

At first, escape can exceed return. As vapour accumulates, returning molecules become more frequent. Eventually the two rates can match at fixed temperature.

The amounts then remain steady even though individual molecules keep crossing. This is dynamic equilibrium: balanced opposing processes, not stopped microscopic activity.

net change per second = forward rate − reverse rate

Equal rates give zero net change. Equal rates do not require equal amounts on the two sides, just as equal traffic flows need not mean equal populations.

Some chemical reactions also proceed in both directions. In a closed system at fixed conditions, reactant and product amounts can settle while both reactions continue.

Changing temperature, pressure or composition can alter the balance. The system may move towards a different equilibrium; it does not consciously resist disturbance.

A catalyst speeds both directions towards equilibrium. It does not change the equilibrium composition at fixed temperature by providing a lower route between the same states.

This idea connects chemistry to balanced forces, but the analogy has limits. Force balance concerns acceleration; dynamic equilibrium concerns matching rates of conversion or transfer.

Change the arrangement without changing the molecules

Ice melts, liquid water flows, and water vapour spreads. In each ordinary state change, the molecules remain H₂O. Their arrangement and motion change.

In a solid, particles fluctuate around relatively stable positions. Many solids have regular crystal patterns; others, such as glass, lack a long-range repeating pattern.

In a liquid, particles remain close but can rearrange and pass neighbours. In a gas, particles are much farther apart under ordinary conditions.

Heating does not make each molecule swell into a larger version. Expansion mainly changes typical separation and arrangement, although detailed material behaviour varies.

Liquids flow without being easy to compress because particles can rearrange while already close together. Flow and compressibility are different properties.

For a pure substance changing state at fixed pressure, energy can enter while temperature stays approximately constant. The particle arrangement changes instead of temperature rising.

energy transferred during a state change = mass × specific latent heat

Specific latent heat, L, means energy required per kilogram for that particular state change. Its unit J/kg makes mL an energy in joules.

More mass means more particles must change arrangement. Double mass at the same transition conditions and the required energy doubles.

During boiling, water molecules separate into vapour without routinely splitting into hydrogen and oxygen. Overcoming between-molecule attractions differs from breaking within-molecule bonds.

Freezing transfers energy out as a more ordered structure forms. Ice floats because its open hydrogen-bonded structure makes its density lower than liquid water's.

Most substances do not share water's precise density pattern. This is a consequence of a particular structure, not a universal rule that solids must float.

Sublimation is direct solid-to-gas change; its reverse is deposition. A liquid stage is not compulsory, and ordinary physical changes still preserve chemical identity.

Freeze-drying uses low pressure so ice can leave frozen material as vapour. Energy is still needed even though the material does not become bulk liquid first.

Tempting idea: “A flat heating graph means no energy enters.” It feels natural because temperature is unchanged. The missing destination is the changing interaction arrangement.

Pressure comes from many changes of momentum

Imagine gas particles bouncing from the inside of a container. Each wall encounter changes a particle's momentum and gives the wall an opposite push.

Many rapid pushes produce a steady average force. Dividing by wall area gives gas pressure. No single particle has the whole gas's pressure.

At fixed particle number and temperature, squeezing the gas into less volume makes wall encounters more frequent. Pressure rises.

For a dilute gas approximated as ideal, halving volume doubles pressure under those fixed conditions. The product pV stays constant: Boyle's relationship.

At fixed volume and particle number, higher temperature means greater average translational kinetic energy. More energetic collisions increase pressure.

The proportional relationship uses absolute temperature T in kelvin, K. Celsius zero is a chosen reference, not zero particle kinetic energy in this model.

At constant pressure instead, warming a freely expanding gas increases volume. The same heating can therefore change pressure or volume depending on what is constrained.

Add particles while holding volume and temperature fixed: more collisions increase pressure. These observations combine into the ideal-gas model.

pV = nRT

p is pressure, V volume, n amount in moles and T absolute temperature. R is a universal conversion constant connecting these measurements for an ideal gas.

Its units, J/(mol K), make nRT an energy. The left side matches: Pa m³ = N m = J. Matching units are a consistency check.

Do not infer that pV is the total internal energy of every gas merely because it has energy units. Different physical quantities can share units.

The ideal model neglects particle volume and interactions except collisions. Dense gases and conditions near condensation need a more detailed description.

Extrapolating to zero kelvin does not establish that every microscopic motion stops. The ideal-gas model ceases to describe real substances before that limit.

Dissolving changes neighbours, not existence

Stir sugar into water. The crystals disappear from view, but the sweet material remains distributed as molecules throughout the liquid.

A solution is a mixture uniform at the scale of ordinary samples. The solvent is the dissolving medium; the dissolved substance is the solute.

For salt, water molecules surround ions pulled from the crystal. Their partly charged regions interact with the ions: oxygen's side faces positive ions preferentially.

For sugar, water interacts with intact sugar molecules. Dissolving sugar does not generally turn it into a supply of separate positive and negative ions.

Both processes replace some original interactions with new ones. Whether much dissolves depends on the full interaction and dispersal balance, not one attraction alone.

Even without stirring, random particle motion can spread dissolved material. This is diffusion: a spreading process that does not require a directed bulk flow.

Imagine a boundary between crowded and less crowded regions. Particles cross both ways, but more start on the crowded side, producing a net spreading tendency.

When the distribution becomes uniform, crossings continue without a sustained net transfer in one direction. Visible steadiness does not imply microscopic stillness.

Stirring moves fluid in bulk and brings differently composed regions closer together. Diffusion then completes mixing over shorter distances; the two mechanisms cooperate.

Mobile ions allow many salt solutions to conduct. An ordinary pure sugar solution lacks that comparable supply of solute ions, so it conducts much less.

Concentration tells us the solute amount in each portion of solution. Specify which amount measure and which volume you use.

mass concentration = dissolved solute mass / solution volume

Its units might be g/L. Amount concentration uses mol/L instead, counting particle portions rather than grams. These are different measurements of the same mixture.

Add solvent while holding solute amount fixed: concentration decreases. Adding both in the same ratio can enlarge the sample without changing concentration.

Solubility asks a different question: how much can dissolve at equilibrium under stated conditions? Temperature, solvent and substance matter; gas solubility also depends on pressure.

At saturation, extra solid can remain while particles still dissolve and rejoin it. The balance is dynamic. Stirring speeds approach but does not simply raise equilibrium solubility.

A colloid contains dispersed particles larger than ordinary dissolved molecules, often scattering light. Milk is one example; appearance alone does not make it a true solution.

Strength and concentration answer different questions

An acid can transfer a proton, a hydrogen nucleus, to another substance. A base can accept that proton. The two roles describe an interaction.

In water, transferred protons associate with water molecules. H₃O⁺, the hydronium ion, is a useful simple picture; H⁺(aq) is common shorthand for hydrated hydrogen ions.

A soluble base producing an alkaline aqueous solution is called an alkali. Some bases contain OH⁻ ions; others accept protons without containing OH⁻ initially.

Consider two separate choices. How much acid is present per litre? And what fraction transfers protons to water under the stated conditions?

Concentration answers the first. Acid strength concerns the tendency to donate protons; at school level we often compare the extent of ionisation in water.

A strong acid such as hydrochloric acid ionises essentially completely in ordinary dilute aqueous solutions. A weak acid such as ethanoic acid establishes an incomplete ionisation equilibrium.

HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻

HA represents an acid; A⁻ is what remains after it donates a proton. The double arrow means both forward and reverse changes are possible.

For a weak acid, changing concentration also changes the fraction ionised. That fraction is not a fixed label independent of conditions.

A concentrated weak acid and a dilute strong acid are therefore entirely possible. Their names do not, by themselves, tell us which has the lower pH.

Tempting idea: “Weak means harmless.” Everyday language encourages that mistake. Chemical hazard depends on identity, amount, concentration and other properties, not this label alone.

Aqueous solutions can differ enormously in hydrogen-ion concentration. Writing every tiny concentration directly is awkward, so pH uses a scale of repeated factors.

For dilute solutions, pH is approximately the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen-ion concentration relative to 1 mol/L. The comparison inside the logarithm is dimensionless.

pH ≈ −log₁₀([H₃O⁺] / (1 mol/L))

Square brackets here mean concentration. log₁₀ asks which power of ten gives the ratio. The minus sign reverses the scale so greater concentration means lower pH.

You need not calculate logarithms to read the relationship. A one-unit pH decrease means roughly ten times the hydrogen-ion concentration; two units mean a hundred times.

Strictly, pH uses activity, an effective concentration accounting for interactions. The dilute-solution approximation is enough for this first model.

Water itself contains both H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Neutrality means equal amounts of these ions in the relevant concentration sense for the simple dilute model.

Near room temperature, neutral pure water has pH about 7. Neutral pH changes with temperature; the familiar 0–14 range is not a universal boundary.

An indicator changes chemical form, and therefore colour, over a particular pH range. Different indicators have different transition ranges and colours.

Neutralisation needs amounts as well as concentrations

Hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions can combine to form water. In a simple strong-acid/strong-alkali mixture, the amounts determine which remains in excess.

H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O

Other ions remain in solution as the dissolved salt. “A neutral salt formula” means zero total charge; its solution is not necessarily neutral in acidity.

In titration, measured portions of one solution are added to another. The steep part of a suitable pH–added-volume graph can reveal the reaction's amount balance.

The equivalence point means the reacting amounts match the equation. Its pH is near 7 for a dilute strong-acid/strong-base case near room temperature, not universally.

Never average two pH numbers to predict a mixture. pH is a logarithmic description, and the ions can react when mixed.

Follow electrons as well as atoms

An iron strip in a suitable copper-ion solution can become coated with copper while some iron enters the solution. Atoms have exchanged electrons.

Iron atoms lose electrons and become positive ions. Copper ions gain electrons and become neutral copper atoms in the deposited metal.

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

Each line is a half-equation: it follows one side of the electron account. Add them and the two electrons cancel because one process supplies what the other receives.

Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu

Oxidation means loss of electrons; reduction means gain. The names extend beyond oxygen reactions. The paired process is called redox.

Charge remains conserved: the overall +2 charge on the left matches +2 on the right. No electron is created simply because a metal becomes charged.

A reactivity series organises common tendencies of metals to undergo such changes under stated conditions. A metal higher in the usual series can often displace lower metals from suitable solutions.

This is a useful pattern, not an instruction to ignore water reactions, oxide coatings, concentrations or other competing processes.

Ores contain useful metal compounds mixed with other material. Extracting a metal often means reducing its positive ions back to the metallic form.

Some oxides can be reduced by carbon or carbon monoxide at suitable temperatures. More demanding reductions may need electrical energy, as in aluminium production.

Choosing a route therefore combines reaction tendency, conditions, energy and separation. An equation alone does not specify an industrial process.

Carbon commonly forms four covalent bonds in simple neutral organic molecules. It can connect to other carbon atoms, making chains, branches and rings.

Carbon's ability to form varied stable structures helps explain the enormous range of organic substances. Not every carbon compound is classed as organic.

Shape matters in chemistry just as direction matters in vectors. Branching can alter contact between molecules and therefore affect boiling behaviour.

“Per” compares equal portions

Imagine distributing the same load over a larger area. Each square metre supports less force. Division measures that concentration of force.

pressure = force / area

The denominator (the quantity below the division line) tells you the size of the portion being compared. The numerator tells you how much that portion contains.

Density uses mass per volume. Concentration uses dissolved amount per solution volume. Current uses charge per time. Similar division structures answer different physical questions.

Do not cancel their meanings because their equations look similar. Density is not concentration, and neither is current; units preserve those distinctions.

At fixed area, doubling force doubles pressure. At fixed force, doubling area halves pressure. Changing both together can leave pressure unchanged.

If both force and area double, their ratio stays constant. This is why a wider water column does not produce greater pressure at the same depth.

Multiplication reverses the comparison. If every square metre carries p newtons, A square metres carry pA newtons in total.

force = pressure × area

A proportional relationship has a fixed ratio. If y is proportional to x, doubling x doubles y only while the other relevant conditions stay unchanged.

An inverse relationship has a fixed product. If y is proportional to 1/x, doubling x halves y under the specified conditions.

Zero deserves care. Zero elapsed time cannot be put into distance/time to calculate a journey average. Division by zero does not describe a measurable finite average.

Units act as a meaning check. N/m² multiplied by m² gives N. Charge per second multiplied by seconds gives charge.

Passing this check does not prove an equation correct. It does rule out many wrong equations before calculation begins.

A graph can show change or accumulated effect

A graph does not have a fixed physical meaning. Its axes decide what moving right, moving upward, slope and area mean.

On a position–time graph, right means later time and up means more positive position. Slope compares position change with elapsed time: velocity.

On a velocity–time graph, the same slope operation compares velocity change with elapsed time. It now means acceleration.

slope = change in vertical quantity / change in horizontal quantity

A horizontal line means the vertical quantity remains constant. Constant position means rest; constant velocity can mean steady motion. Read the axis before interpreting the line.

A downward slope means the vertical quantity decreases as the horizontal quantity increases. It does not universally mean that an object is slowing.

Now imagine a steady current. Each second transfers I coulombs. Over t seconds, the transferred charge is It.

On a current–time graph this is a rectangle: height I multiplied by width t. Its area represents charge, not a geometrical patch of wire.

If current changes, divide time into short intervals. Each strip contributes approximately its current multiplied by its duration. Add the contributions.

The same accumulated-effect idea gives displacement from velocity–time area, impulse from force–time area, and energy from power–time area.

Area is useful when multiplying the axes' quantities produces the quantity we need. The units expose why it works.

A triangle appears when the vertical quantity rises steadily from zero. Its average height is half its final height, so its area is half base times height.

This explains the one-half in steady-acceleration displacement and spring energy. The factor comes from how the effect builds, not from a rule to memorise.

Why a shrinking interval becomes useful

A car's average velocity over a minute can hide braking during the final seconds. Shorten the interval around that moment and the average becomes more local.

We already know how to compare two positions: subtract them, then divide by the elapsed time. No new rule is needed yet.

average velocity over a small interval = Δx / Δt

Δx means change in position; Δt means the corresponding change in time. The units remain metres per second, however short the interval becomes.

Now imagine intervals shrinking around one instant. For smooth motion, their average velocities approach a particular value. That approached value describes instantaneous velocity.

A limit is the value approached as we make a chosen change increasingly small or increasingly large. Here, the time interval approaches zero.

We never calculate velocity by dividing zero displacement by zero time. We examine ratios for non-zero intervals and ask which value they approach.

On a position–time graph, two points define a short joining line. As they approach one another, that line approaches the local direction of the curve.

That local direction is represented by a tangent: a straight line matching the curve's direction at the chosen point. Its slope represents instantaneous velocity.

Later, the notation dx/dt names this limiting rate. The letter d signals the local-change idea here; it is not an extra quantity multiplying x or t.

The same reasoning gives instantaneous acceleration from a velocity graph and instantaneous reaction rate from a product-amount graph. What changes depends on the axes.

The reverse task also uses shrinking intervals. To accumulate a changing current, divide time into short pieces, estimate each charge transfer IΔt, then add them.

Smaller pieces improve the approximation for a well-behaved current. The limiting sum gives graph area. Later mathematics calls this integration.

Real measurements have finite resolution and noise. Taking an interval too small can worsen an experimental estimate, even when the ideal mathematical limit exists.

A sudden corner in an idealised graph may have no single tangent. Limits are precise questions about a model, not promises that every drawn curve behaves smoothly.

Why doubling size rarely doubles everything

Imagine enlarging a cube so every edge becomes twice as long. Each face becomes twice as wide and twice as high.

Repeated multiplication needs a different scale

Imagine amounts of one, ten, a hundred and a thousand. The numerical gaps grow, but each change uses the same multiplier: ten.

A logarithmic scale (a scale of repeated factors) gives equal steps to equal multipliers. It compresses a huge range without hiding the ratios.

The direction is reversed: lower pH corresponds to more hydrogen ions per volume. The pH number is not itself a concentration.

Do not interchange the scales. One pH step and ten decibels each represent a factor of ten in different quantities, with different conventions.

Repeated fractions provide another useful pattern. Suppose a radioactive sample loses half its undecayed nuclei in each equal half-life interval.

After successive intervals, the remaining fractions are one-half, one-quarter, one-eighth and one-sixteenth. Equal times remove equal fractions, not equal numbers.

The amount lost each interval becomes smaller because fewer undecayed nuclei remain. The half-life stays the same for that isotope under ordinary conditions.

This is a population rule. It does not tell us the exact moment when any one nucleus will decay. Many unpredictable events can produce a predictable overall pattern.
