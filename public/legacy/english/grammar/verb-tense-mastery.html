    Section 2

    The master mental model

    Concept — four dials, one choice

    Every tense decision is the product of four interacting dimensions:

    1 · Time location — where does the situation sit relative to now (or another vantage point)?
    2 · Situation shape — is it a state, an open-ended doing, a job with a finish line, an instant flip? (Section 4)
    3 · Reference point — from which moment is the speaker viewing it?
    4 · Speaker focus — what do you want the listener to attend to: the fact, the process, the result, the plan?

    Tense is the camera setting; time is the scene. The same scene — a train leaving — can be shot as a snapshot (left), a rolling clip (was leaving), or a look-back from later (had left). The scene never chooses the camera. You do.

    The reasoning chain, which every worked answer in this book follows:

    The chain

    Context → time reference → situation shape → reference point → speaker focus → form → meaning produced.

    Notice what is not in the chain: “spot a signal word → paste a memorised tense.” Signal words are witnesses you question, not judges you obey (Section 12 shows exactly when they mislead).

    Section 3

    Time, tense, aspect, modality — four different things

    School grammar sells “twelve tenses.” English actually builds temporal meaning from four separate strands, and keeping them apart is what makes everything later click:

    StrandWhat it doesMachineryExamples
    Tenselocates a situation relative to a temporal viewpointverb marking: only present and pastwalks / walked, is / was
    Aspectpresents the internal shape or relevance of the situationprogressive be + -ing; perfect have + participleis walking, has walked
    Modalityadds prediction, intention, obligation, possibilitymodals: will, would, may, must, could…will walk, must have walked
    Future referencetalks about later timeseveral constructions — no future tense marking existswill go, is going to go, is leaving, leaves at 7

    Concept — the honest definitions (used throughout)

    Tense locates a situation relative to a temporal viewpoint, usually through present or past marking on the verb.
    Aspect shows how the speaker presents the internal temporal shape — or the later relevance — of that situation.
    These stack. is being written = present tense + progressive aspect + passive voice. will have written = modal will + perfect aspect. One verb phrase, several strands, each doing one job.

    Caution — a claim this book must immediately qualify

    “Tense locates real-world time” is a first approximation, not a law. Past forms can signal remoteness from reality rather than past time: If I knew, I would tell you · I wish I had more time · It’s time we left. Section 14 makes this precise. File the exception now so the rule never hardens into dogma.

    Anatomy of the verb phrase

    Every English verb phrase is assembled on one fixed production line. Learn the line once and no construction will ever look exotic again:

    Concept — the assembly order

    (modal) + (have -en) + (be -ing) + (be -en) + VERB
    modality · perfect · progressive · passive · main verb

    writes → has written → has been writing → is being written → will have been written. The full stack — will have been being repaired — is grammatical but so heavy that English almost always rephrases it (Section 16).

    The three faces of a verb still matter: base (go), past (went), participle (gone). The golden distinction: went works alone; gone needs a helper. Mixing them produces ✗ has went and ✗ I seen it — form errors, the one category where a single hard answer really exists.

    Precision notes — glossary of terms as this book uses them
    TermMeaning here
    tensegrammatical location of a situation relative to a viewpoint, via present/past marking
    aspect (viewpoint)the speaker’s framing: whole-and-external (simple), from-inside (progressive), linked-back-from-later (perfect)
    aspect (situation / lexical)the inherent temporal contour of the verb phrase itself — Section 4
    modalitymeanings such as prediction, intention, obligation, possibility, carried by modal verbs
    reference pointthe moment the situation is viewed from — stated in the sentence or supplied by surrounding discourse
    auxiliarya helping verb (be, have, do, the modals) teaming with a main verb
    past participlethe third form (gone, written, seen) — after have, or after be in passives
    construalthe speaker’s chosen framing of a situation — the reason one event can wear several forms

    Section 4

    The two layers of aspect — the deepest lever in the book

    Here is the single distinction that explains the stative puzzle, the limits of “simple = photo,” and why the same tense behaves differently with different verbs. Aspect operates on two layers at once:

    Concept — layer 1: situation shape (built into the verb phrase)

    Situations come in five inherent contours. Plain names first, technical names for reference:

    Concept — layer 2: viewpoint (chosen by the speaker)

    Simple presents a situation from outside, without placing the viewer inside its unfolding.
    Progressive presents it from within its development.
    Perfect relates a prior situation to a later reference point.
    Perfect progressive relates the unfolding or duration of a prior situation to that point.

    Grammatical meaning is shape × viewpoint. That is why She was running (activity, from inside) is ordinary; She was reaching the summit (instant flip, from inside) can only mean the run-up to the flip; and She was knowing the answer fails — a flat line has no inside to stand in.

    Caution — retiring an old anchor honestly

    The first edition taught “Simple = the photo. Continuous = the film.” Keep it as a starter analogy only. A photo suggests one bounded event — but simple forms also carry habits (She cycles to school), states (She knows), general truths (Ice melts at 0 °C), schedules (The train leaves at 7), performatives (I apologise), and live commentary (Singh passes to Patel). None of those is a photograph of one event. The durable idea is the one above: simple = viewed from outside, no interior claimed.

    Coercion — the bridge between the layers

    Concept — one mechanism, not a pile of exceptions

    When a viewpoint meets a shape it doesn’t naturally fit, English doesn’t always refuse — it coerces the situation into a compatible shape:

    · state → temporary state: I’m living in Hyderabad this year
    · state → intense current episode: I’m loving this course ≈ informal
    · identity → behaviour: You’re being silly (acting silly now, not being a silly person)
    · instant flip → run-up: The train was arriving
    · blip → repetition: He was knocking on the door (again and again)

    So progressive compatibility depends on how the speaker construes the situation in context — not on whether the verb “describes visible action.” This one rule replaces every stative-verb list you have ever memorised. Section 11 drills it.

    Section 6 · Present time

    Simple present vs present progressive

    Concept

    Simple present — a situation viewed from outside, presented as holding true: habits (Ravi plays chess), states (She knows the answer), general truths (Ice melts at 0 °C), official schedules (The train leaves at 7 — future use, Section 9), performatives (I apologise), and live commentary (Singh passes to Patel).
    Present progressive — the situation viewed from inside its unfolding: right now (Listen — someone is playing the piano), around now (I’m reading a great novel), temporary (She’s living with her grandparents), changing (Prices are rising), fixed arrangements (I’m meeting my teacher tomorrow — Section 9), and, with always, affectionate or irritated repetition (He’s always losing his keys).

    Contrast — permanent vs temporary

    Stable

    Nina lives with her grandparents.

    Her normal arrangement; no end in view.

    Temporary slice

    Nina is living with her grandparents.

    A current phase — while the flat is repainted, while her parents travel. The progressive coerces the state into a bounded stretch (Section 4).

      Explain the difference in meaning: “Nina lives with her grandparents.” / “Nina is living with her grandparents.”

      Show answer

      ✓ Both grammatical — stable arrangement vs temporary phase · TEMP-PERM

      Why

      Lives presents the arrangement as her normal state, no boundary in view. Is living coerces the state into a bounded current slice and implies impermanence — the listener expects a reason or an end date.

      Fix it and say why: “I am knowing the answer.”

      Show answer

      ✗ ungrammatical → I know the answer. · STATIVE

      Why

      Know is a flat-line state with no internal development to stand inside, and — unlike live or love — no ordinary construal rescues it: knowing cannot be framed as a temporary activity you are midway through. This is one of the few statives that resist coercion almost completely.

      2. rehearses
      3. has been rehearsing

    Section 7 · Perfect aspect

    The viewing platform — one shape, three positions, five readings

    Concept — the platform

    Every perfect construction relates an earlier situation to a later reference point — a platform the speaker stands on:

    Platform = now → present perfect: She has packed (so we can leave now).
    Platform = a past moment → past perfect: She had packed when the taxi came.
    Platform = a future moment → future perfect: She will have packed by noon.

    Do not define the perfect as “a completed action.” Completion alone never earns a perfect — the simple past is full of completed actions. What earns it is the link: the earlier situation still counts at the platform.

    Concept — the platform is the unifying model; the specific relationship varies

    What “still counts” means differs by reading. The five readings of the present perfect:

    ReadingExampleWhat the link is
    ResultI’ve lost my keys.a present state caused by the event — the keys are lost now
    ExperienceI’ve been to Rome. · The Earth has experienced five mass extinctions.the event sits in the record of a life or period that reaches now — no immediate practical result required
    ContinuationI’ve lived here for ten years.the situation began earlier and still holds
    TallyYou’ve had three coffees today.a count inside an open period — the number can still rise
    RecencyShe has just left.freshness — the event practically touches now

    Not every perfect involves a consciously felt “look back over the shoulder,” and “present relevance” is often supplied by context rather than baked into the form. Keep the platform as the picture; keep the five readings as the vocabulary.

    Concept — the mechanical check learners can actually apply

    The definite-time rule: the present perfect refuses adverbials that name a finished, definite past time; the simple past demands them.

    ✗ I have seen that film last Saturday · ✓ I saw that film last Saturday · ✓ I have seen that film

    Ask one question: is the time expression a closed box (yesterday, last week, in 2019, at 3 p.m.) or an open container that still includes now (today, this week, since 2019, ever, so far)? Closed box → simple past. Open container → present perfect available. This check is mechanical, which makes it usable under exam pressure — unlike the feeling of “relevance,” which is not.

    Precision notes — where the definite-time rule bends

    Since-phrases name a definite starting point yet pair with the perfect — because they define an open container stretching from that point to now. This morning is an open container until about noon, then quietly becomes a closed box: I’ve drunk two coffees this morning (said at 11:00) vs I drank two coffees this morning (said at 16:00). And American English relaxes the rule with just / already / yet — I already ate — a variety difference ≈, not an error (Section 18).

    Contrast — perfect simple vs perfect progressive (works at every platform)

    Result · completion · quantity

    She has painted the fence. Look — it’s green. She has written three essays. The count is the point.

    Duration · process · fresh evidence

    She has been painting the fence. There’s paint on her hands; whether it’s finished isn’t claimed. She has been writing all morning. The effort is the point.

    Statives take the simple perfect for continuation: I have known her since school, not ✗ have been knowing — layer 1 (Section 4) still rules.

    Section 8 · Past time

    The storyteller’s engine and the flashback

    Concept — the four past viewpoints

    Simple past — the engine of narrative: whole events, in order, tied to a finished time. She opened the door, saw the mess, and sighed.
    Past progressive — the scene from inside: background, atmosphere, and the stretch an event interrupts. Rain was falling. I was reading when the lights went out.
    Past perfect — the flashback: an earlier situation viewed from a later past platform.
    Past perfect progressive — the long run-up to that platform. The ground was wet: it had been raining all night.

    Concept — the corrected rule for the past perfect

    First-edition rule (retire it): “past perfect needs the earlier of two stated events.” Too strong.

    The real rule: past perfect needs an earlier situation and a later past reference point — and that reference point may be stated in the sentence or supplied by the surrounding discourse. It can be an event, a moment, or even a state.

    I looked around the empty room. Everyone had left. — The second sentence names only one event, yet it is perfect English: the platform is the moment of looking, carried over from the previous sentence.

    Contrast — when is a stage, not a script

    Interruption

    When the phone rang, I was washing up. The ringing lands inside the stretch.

    Sequence

    When the phone rang, I answered it. Ring, then answer: two whole events in order.

    And a third reading: When I arrived, the film had started — the earlier event viewed from the arrival platform. Same connector, three time-shapes: the tenses are the actors; when is only the stage.

    Caution — over-marking is the advanced learner’s disease

    Once learners discover the past perfect they start spending it everywhere. If the order of events is already obvious — after, before, or plain narrative sequence — the simple past usually reads better: After he finished the test, he slept for an hour is at least as natural as after he had finished. And a chain of dated facts wants no perfect at all: The film started at 6 and ended at 8. Reserve had for when the look-back does work: surprising order, or an earlier cause explaining a later scene.

    Section 9 · Future time

    Nine tools, no future tense — and defaults, not laws

    Concept — the toolbox

    English has no future tense marking; it projects forward with constructions. The choice signals the source and status of your knowledge:

    ToolCore useExample
    will + basepredictions, promises, offers, decisions made at the moment of speakingThe doorbell! I’ll get it.
    be going toprior intentions; predictions from present signsI’m going to learn the drums. Look at those clouds — it’s going to rain.
    present progressivefixed personal arrangements — the diary is writtenI’m meeting Dr Sen at ten tomorrow.
    simple presenttimetables and institutional schedulesThe exam starts at 9:30. Term ends on the 20th.
    will be + -ingin progress at a future moment; expected course; polite enquiryAt 8 I’ll be travelling. Will you be using the printer?
    will have (been) …done — or running — before a future platformBy June, she’ll have finished / will have been studying here for two years.
    be about to / on the point ofimmediacy — the edge of the eventQuiet — the film is about to start.
    be due to / be set to / be likely toscheduled, slated, or probable futures (news and workplace register)The train is due to arrive at six. Rates are set to rise.
    be to + baseofficial arrangement or destiny (formal)The president is to visit in May. He was to become a legend.

    Contrast — the two big decisions

    will vs going to

    “We’ve run out of bread.” — “Oh, I’ll get some.” Decision born this second. · “I’m going to get bread on the way home” — the plan predates the conversation.

    Whose calendar is it?

    A person’s diary → progressive: I’m meeting the dentist at five. An institution’s timetable → simple present: The exam starts at 9:30.

    Caution — these are defaults, not semantic laws

    Real usage overlaps far more than the neat table admits. I’ll meet Sam tomorrow can report a settled plan; I’m going to meet Sam at ten can be a concrete arrangement; It’s going to be difficult needs no visible evidence; The train will leave at seven can be an authoritative schedule statement. That is why this book grades future answers as ✓ default / ◐ construal / ○ needs context — and why any exercise claiming exactly one “natural” future form should be treated with suspicion, including in other books.

    Section 10 · States and construal

    Progressive compatibility is about framing, not verb lists

    Concept — the rule that replaces the list

    Progressive compatibility depends on how the speaker construes the situation in context — not on whether the verb “describes visible action.”

    The old classroom test — “can you do it on purpose for five minutes, then stop?” — measures deliberateness, and deliberateness is the wrong dimension: sleep is not deliberate but happily progressive (she’s sleeping); wait shows almost no visible action yet is an activity (we’re waiting). What matters is whether the situation, as framed here, has an inside to stand in (Section 4).

    Chameleon verbsdepends on the meaning in playsee, think, have, taste, be — different senses, different classes (table below)

    Section 11 · Signal words and adverbial scope

    Witnesses, not judges

    Concept

    Time expressions are evidence. Good detectives weigh evidence; they never let one witness dictate the verdict. Every marker below usually suggests a form — and every one of them can be overruled by context:

    WitnessUsually points toWhen it misleads
    yesterday, last week, in 2019simple past (closed box)reliable for the clause it modifies — but check which clause it modifies (scope, below)
    forperfect (duration to a platform)the stretch may be sealed in the past: I lived there for ten years (Q12)
    sinceperfect (open container from a start point)as a conjunction it can mean because: Since you ask, no. — no tense force at all
    when—compatible with interruption, sequence, and anteriority (Section 8); it never fixes order by itself

    this morning / todayopen container → perfect availablethe container closes as the day moves on: I’ve had two coffees this morning (at 11:00) → I had two coffees this morning (at 16:00)

    Adverbial scope — which verb does the time phrase touch?

    Concept

    A time expression modifies one clause, not the whole sentence by default — and sometimes the sentence lets it attach to either, creating real ambiguity:

    “Yesterday I heard that she resigned.” — Did the hearing happen yesterday, the resigning, or both? Fronted yesterday most naturally scopes over the hearing; the resigning is timed only by inference.

    “I’m still working on the essay I started yesterday.” — yesterday governs only the relative clause (started); the main clause sits calmly in the present.

    Caution

    Half of all “signal word” errors are really scope errors: the learner sees yesterday anywhere in the sentence and pastes past tense everywhere. Ask: which verb is this witness testifying about?

    Standing in the past, looking forward

    Section 13 · Time clauses, conditionals, and remoteness

    Where past forms stop meaning past time

    Concept — rule 1: the no-will zone

    Inside future-referring clauses opened by when, after, before, until, as soon as, once, while, if, unless — use the present, not will:

    ✓ I’ll call you when I arrive. · ✗ when I will arrive
    ✓ We’ll leave as soon as the rain stops. · and for completion-before: I’ll lend it to you when I’ve finished it — present perfect, same zone.

    The main clause carries the future; the subordinate clause borrows its time and stays in present clothing.

    Concept — rule 2: remoteness, the second life of the past tense

    Past forms mark distance — and English exploits three kinds:

    Distance in time: She left yesterday.
    Distance from reality: If I knew, I’d tell you · I wish I had more time · It’s time we left · Suppose she came tomorrow? — past forms, present or future time, unreal or tentative meaning.
    Social distance (politeness): I wondered if you could help me — the “past” backs respectfully away from the hearer.

    One mechanism, three uses. This is why “tense = time” had to be filed as an approximation back in Section 3.

    Section 14 · Reported speech

    Backshift — and when not to

    Concept — the shift

    Reporting from a past platform slides forms one step back: am → was, have done → had done, did → had done (often optional), will → would, can → could, may → might. “I’m tired,” she said → She said she was tired.

    Backshift is a courtesy to the past platform, not a law. When the reported content still holds — or is a standing truth — the present may stay: She said the library closes at eight. · He told me he is Canadian.

    Precision notes — the double-access reading

    Keeping the present inside a past report — He told me he is Canadian — makes a double claim: true at the telling and true now, on the speaker’s own authority. Backshifted he said he was Canadian commits you only to what was said then. That is why journalism backshifts contested claims (the minister said the figures were accurate) and keeps the present for what the writer also endorses. The choice is an act of vouching.

    Contrast — questions and commands

    Reported questions

    Statement order, no do, no question mark: “Where do you live?” → He asked where I lived. · “Did you finish?” → She asked if I had finished.

    Reported commands

    told/asked + object + to-infinitive: “Wait outside.” → She told us to wait outside. Negative: told us not to wait.

    4. Q68 Diagnose

      What exactly is wrong here — time, aspect, or machinery? “The letters are been delivered at noon every day.”

      Show answer

      ✗ form → are delivered · FORM

      Why

      The time choice (routine → present simple) was right; the machinery misfired: been belongs after have, never directly after are. Passive present simple is just be + participle. Naming the broken layer is the repair.

    Section 16 · Modal perfects

    Reasoning backwards in time

    Concept

    modal + have + participle aims a present judgement at a past event — deduction, possibility, criticism, relief, regret:

    FormMeaningExample
    must haveconfident deduction about the pastThe lights are off — they must have left.
    can’t / couldn’t haveconfident negative deductionShe can’t have taken it; she was with me.
    may / might / could haveopen past possibilityHe might have missed the bus.
    should haveright thing, not done (criticism/regret)You should have called me.
    could havepossible, not done (missed chance)We could have won that game.
    would havecounterfactual outcomeI would have helped, if you’d asked.
    needn’t havedone, but unnecessaryYou needn’t have brought a gift.

    Contrast — the pair everyone confuses

    needn’t have done

    You did it; it wasn’t necessary. You needn’t have waited — I had my key. (You waited.)

    didn’t need to do

    It wasn’t necessary; whether you did it is open — usually you didn’t. I didn’t need to wait, so I went home.

    Practice — Modal perfects

    1. Q69 MCQ

      The streets are completely dry. It ___ last night after all.

      1. can’t have rained
      2. must have rained
      3. shouldn’t have rained
      Show answer

      ✓ (a) can’t have rained · MODAL-TIME

      Why

      Present evidence (dry streets) → confident negative deduction about the past. (b) reasons the wrong way from the evidence; (c) would scold the weather for raining — a criticism, not a deduction.

    2. Q70 Fill the blank

      I ___ (bring) my umbrella — it didn’t rain at all and I carried it around all day.

      Show answer

      ✓ needn’t have brought · MODAL-TIME

      Why

      The carrying happened; hindsight declares it unnecessary — exactly the needn’t have slot. Didn’t need to bring would clash with “carried it around all day.”

    3. Q71 Compare two

      “You should have told me.” / “You could have told me.” What does each accuse?

      Show answer

      Duty missed vs opportunity missed · MODAL-TIME

      Why

      should have: telling me was the right thing, and you failed it — moral weight. could have: telling me was possible and you chose not to — exasperation at a wasted option. Both look back from now at a road not taken; they differ in what the road was paved with.

    4. Q72 Fill the blank

      If you had told me earlier, I ___ (arrange) everything in time.

      Show answer

      ✓ would have arranged · MODAL-TIME

      Why

      Unreal past condition → counterfactual outcome: would have + participle. This is Section 13’s remoteness at maximum distance — off the real line and back in time — with the modal carrying the consequence.

    5. Q73 Grade every option

      Rhea isn’t answering her phone. She ___ .

      1. might have gone to bed
      2. must have gone to bed
      3. had gone to bed
      Show answer

      (a) ✓ · (b) ◐ · (c) ✗ here · MODAL-TIME

      Why

      One unanswered call supports an open possibility (a). (b) needs stronger evidence — it’s 2 a.m., her light is off — to earn its confidence. (c) is a plain past perfect: it needs a past platform, and this sentence stands in the present. Standalone, it isn’t a deduction at all.

    Section 17 · Habitual past

    used to, would, and the irritated progressive

    Concept — four instruments for old habits

    used to + base — discontinued states and actions: I used to live near the sea. I used to swim daily.
    would + base — actions only, and only inside an established past frame; nostalgic-narrative flavour: Every summer we’d cycle to the lake, and Grandpa would tell the same story.
    simple past + frequency — the neutral workhorse: We often walked to school.
    past progressive + always — coloured repetition, usually fond or irritated: He was always losing his keys.

    Caution — the would trap

    ✗ I would live in Nagpur as a child — live is a state, and habitual would refuses states: say I used to live in Nagpur. And opening a story cold with “We would go to the beach” leaves the listener asking when? — establish the frame first (When we stayed with our aunt, we would…).

    Practice — Habitual past

    1. Q74 Grade every option

      “I ___ a big dog when I was small.”

      1. used to have
      2. would have
      3. had
      Show answer

      (a) ✓ · (b) ✗ here · (c) ✓ · HABIT

      Why

      Owning is a state: used to and the plain past both serve; habitual would rejects it (and this string collides with conditional would have, doubling the confusion). The state/action test is the gatekeeper.

    2. Q75 Fill the blanks

      Every Diwali we ___ (travel) to my grandmother’s house, and she ___ (make) sweets for a whole week beforehand.

      Show answer

      ✓ would travel (…) would make — or used to / simple past throughout · HABIT

      Why

      Every Diwali raises the frame, so narrative would can carry both repeated actions with its storybook glow. All three habitual instruments are grammatical here; would is the most evocative once the frame exists.

    3. Q76 What changed?

      “He always lost his keys.” / “He was always losing his keys.”

      Show answer

      Report vs eye-roll · HABIT

      Why

      The simple past states the frequency as fact. The progressive with always paints the habit as an ongoing atmosphere you lived inside — and carries attitude: exasperation or affection. Grammar encoding a sigh.

    4. Q77 Correction · justify

      “I use to play cricket on Sundays.” (about the past)

      Show answer

      ✗ form → used to play · FORM

      Why

      The habitual marker is used to with the past -d — the d merges with t in speech, which is why the spelling error is so common. (After did, the base surfaces: Did you use to play?)

    Section 18 · Dialect, register, variation

    One system, many Englishes

    Concept — the ≈ verdict’s home

    Differences between standard varieties are systematic patterns, not errors. The skill is matching variety to audience:

    PatternBritish-leaningAmerican-leaning
    recent past with just/already/yetI’ve just eaten. Have you finished yet?I just ate. Did you finish yet?
    participle of gethas gothas gotten (obtained/become); has got survives for possession
    collective subjectsThe team are playing well.The team is playing well.
    shallShall we begin? (offers/suggestions)rare; Should we begin?

    Register cuts across geography: I’m loving it, narrative present, and gonna are informal everywhere; exam and formal writing everywhere prefer the conservative options. When in doubt in Indian academic settings, British-leaning exam conventions are the safe default.

    Practice — Variation

    1. Q78 Judge it

      An American friend texts: “I already saw that movie.” Error?

      Show answer

      ≈ variety — standard AmE · DIALECT

      Why

      American English pairs already/just/yet freely with the simple past; British and exam English keep the perfect (I’ve already seen it). Same system, different default settings — translate for your audience, don’t “correct” the friend.

    2. Q79 Choose for the context

      Formal school report — pick and justify: “The class ___ (make) excellent progress since January.”

      Show answer

      ✓ has made · DIALECT

      Why

      Since January opens the container → perfect; a formal report takes the conservative register. (BrE would also accept plural have made with a collective read; the singular is safest across audiences.)

    3. Q80 Translate the register

      Rewrite this text-message future for a formal notice: “Heads up — we’re gonna start the meeting at 3, it’ll prob run late.”

      Show answer

      ✓ “Please note: the meeting will begin at 3 p.m. and is likely to run late.” · REGISTER

      Why

      gonna → will (or is scheduled to), prob → is likely to: the future tools have register ranks, and Section 9’s be likely to / be due to family is the formal shelf. Meaning preserved; clothing changed.

    Section 19 · Narrative layering

    The three-camera shoot

    Concept

    A well-told past story runs three cameras at once:
    Background camera — past progressive: scene, weather, atmosphere.
    Action camera — simple past: the events, in order — the story’s spine.
    Flashback camera — past perfect: what was already true or done before the scene.

    Practice — Narrative layering

    1. Q81 Label the cameras

      Name the layer of each verb: “The station clock was striking six when Meera ran onto the platform. The train had left.”

      Show answer

      background · action · flashback · NARR-LAYER

      Why

      Was striking sets the scene; ran is the spine event; had left is what was already true at that platform — three cameras in twenty words, and the tragedy lives entirely in the third.

    2. Q82 Complete the scene

      Fill all three, one per camera: “Snow ___ (fall) softly. Arun ___ (open) the gate — someone ___ (shovel) the path already.”

      Show answer

      ✓ was falling · opened · had shovelled ≈ shoveled AmE · NARR-LAYER

      Why

      Atmosphere in progressive, event in simple past, prior work in past perfect — the shovelling’s platform is Arun’s opening, supplied by the sentence before: Section 8’s implicit reference point working inside a story.

    3. Q83 Edit the flat draft

      Improve by re-layering (keep the facts): “I walked home. It rained. I forgot my umbrella at school.”

      Show answer

      ✓ “As I walked home, it was raining — and I had forgotten my umbrella at school.” · NARR-LAYER

      Why

      The rain becomes weather-in-progress around the walk; the forgetting moves before the scene where it belongs. Three sealed boxes become one inhabited moment. (Other layerings are fine if each verb sits on a deliberate camera.)

    4. Q84 Spot the deliberate shift

      A memoirist writes: “We buried him on a Tuesday. He loved Tuesdays. He always said the week finally got honest on a Tuesday.” Why simple past for the loving — and would had loved be wrong?

      Show answer

      Both grammatical — different griefs · NARR-LAYER

      Why

      Loved keeps the man inside the story’s time, alive in his habits beside the funeral. Had loved would file his loving as background completed before the burial — more distant, more final. Neither is an error; the choice is the eulogy. Tense at this level is not correctness but meaning-craft.

    Section 20 · The editing and ambiguity laboratory

    Where single right answers honestly end

    Concept

    Real text work is not “pick the tense” but “what does each tense fix, what stays open, and what would pin it down?” This lab practises exactly that — including on a task the first edition got wrong by pretending it had one answer.

    1. Q85 Repair the message

      Fix every tense fault in this workplace message and keep the meaning: “Hi sir, I have submitted the report yesterday. I am working on the slides since Monday and I will send them when I will finish.”

      Show answer

      ✓ “Hi sir, I submitted the report yesterday. I have been working on the slides since Monday, and I’ll send them when I finish.” · DEF-ADV TIME-CLAUSE

      Why

      Three separate systems failed: definite-time rule (yesterday → simple past, Section 7); open-container duration (since Monday → perfect progressive, not present progressive); the no-will zone (when I finish, Section 13). Diagnosing per-system beats “fixing the tenses” blindfolded.

    2. Q86 Preserve the meaningful shift

      A reviewer “corrects” this to all-past. What is lost? “Kalpana Chawla grew up in Karnal. Her journey shows students everywhere that the sky is not the limit.”

      Show answer

      The shift is the point — biography past + legacy present · GEN-TRUTH

      Why

      Life events sit in the past; the inspiration is claimed as true now, so the present tense is doing deliberate work. Flattening to showed buries the legacy with the biography. Editing rule: before unifying tenses, ask whether the writer meant the seam.

    3. Q87 Find the genuine error

      One shift here is meaningful, one is broken. Which is which? “The experiment shows that plants grow faster in blue light. We measured each stem daily. The results suggested that light colour was mattering a great deal.”

      Show answer

      shows/measured = meaningful · ✗ was mattering → mattered / matters · STATIVE

      Why

      Report grammar legitimately splits: standing findings in the present, method steps in the past. The breakage is aspect: matter is a flat-line state and refuses the progressive. (Suggested vs suggest is a register choice; both defensible.)

    4. Q88 Ambiguity analysis — the rebuilt classic

      These four sentences arrived shuffled. Do not hunt for “the” order — instead: (1) state what each tense fixes about the timeline, (2) give the orderings that remain readable, (3) name one added anchor that would force a unique order.

      1. By then, the rain had stopped.
      2. We packed the tents while the others were cooking breakfast.
      3. We had arrived at the campsite the night before, soaked to the skin.
      4. At sunrise, I unzipped the tent and looked out.
      Show answer

      Fixed: (c) earliest; (a) is a state at a reference point, not a datable event. Readable orders: c–d–a–b and c–d–b–a · REF-POINT NARR-LAYER

      Why

      What each tense fixes: (c)’s past perfect + the night before nails the arrival before everything told at sunrise. (d)’s simple past + at sunrise plants the morning platform. (b)’s simple-past-inside-progressive is a morning action wrapped in a cooking backdrop — after (d), but its exact slot relative to (a) is loose. (a)’s by then is a pointer with no clock of its own: it reports the rain already over at whatever moment the neighbouring sentence supplies, so it can sit after (d) (“I looked out; by then the rain had stopped”) or after (b) as a closing observation. That is why the first edition’s single-answer version was unanswerable: the grammar genuinely underdetermines one slot. Forcing anchor: rewrite (a) as “By the time we started packing, the rain had stopped” — now then has an owner and c–d–a–b is the only reading.

      Expert note · The upgraded skill isn’t sorting sentences; it is knowing which relations tense pins down (anteriority, platforms, backdrops) and which it leaves to discourse — and how to add an anchor when you need to close the gap in your own writing.

    2. Q90 Grade every option

      “The ceremony ___ at nine, so don’t be late.” (printed invitation in hand)

      2. is beginning

      Show answer

      (a) ✓ · (b) ◐ · (c) ✓ · FUT-SOURCE

      Why

    3. Q91 Correction

      “She is knowing the answer since yesterday.”

      Show answer

      ✗ → She has known the answer since yesterday. · STATIVE OPEN-CLOSED

      Why

      Two faults, one clause: know refuses the progressive, and since yesterday opens a container reaching now → continuative perfect, kept simple because the verb is a state.

    4. Q92 Convert

      Report: “Will you help me tomorrow?” she asked her brother.

      Show answer

      ✓ She asked her brother if/whether he would help her the next day. · BACKSHIFT

      Why

      Yes/no question → if/whether + statement order; will → would; tomorrow re-anchors to the next day because the reporting platform moved.

    5. Q93 Fill

      Her bag is gone but her scooter’s here — she ___ (take, can’t) it far.

      Show answer

      ✓ can’t have taken · MODAL-TIME

      Why

      Show answer

      ✗ → …until the nurse calls your name. · TIME-CLAUSE

      ✓ will have been repainted · PASSIVE

      Why

    8. Q96 Compare two

      “What did you do when the alarm rang?” / “What were you doing when the alarm rang?”

      Show answer

      Response vs backdrop · WHEN-FLEX

      Why

      The first asks what you did next — your reaction. The second asks what the alarm interrupted. Investigators ask both, in that order, for exactly this reason.

    4. Q100 Explain

      Two polite versions of one request — what is the grammar doing? “I want to ask something.” → “I wanted to ask something.” → “I was wanting to ask something.”

      Show answer

      Stacked remoteness · REMOTE

      Why

    5. Q101 Complete text — the repaired science log

      Fill all four blanks: “Yesterday we ___ (test) how paper planes fly. Before each throw, we ___ (fold) the wings at the same angle. The plane with folded wingtips ___ (fly) the farthest. From this, we can conclude that small flaps ___ (help) planes glide.”

      ✓ tested · folded · flew · help · GEN-TRUTH

      Why

      Method and results anchored by yesterday → simple past throughout; the conclusion is wrapped in “we can conclude that…”, which frames a standing truth → present help.     6. Q102 Scope check

      Is this sentence about a past study habit or a present one? “The routine I followed last year still works: I revise each topic twice.”

      Show answer

      Present habit; last year scopes only the relative clause · ADV-SCOPE

      Why

      Followed lives with last year inside the relative clause; works and revise stand on the now-platform. One sentence, two time zones, zero contradiction — scope keeps the books.

    7. Q103 Judge it

      Chennai colleague, informal chat: “I have sent you the file just now.”

      Show answer

      ≈ variety — common in Indian English; BrE/AmE defaults differ · DIALECT

      Why

      Perfect + just now is widespread and comprehensible in Indian English. British English prefers I’ve just sent it; American, I just sent it. For international formal writing choose one of those defaults; among colleagues, communication succeeded — grade varieties by audience, not by one textbook.

    8. Q104 Capstone — grade the quartet

      Your friend moved to Pune in 2021 and still lives there. Grade each report:

      1. She has lived in Pune since 2021.
      2. She has been living in Pune since 2021.
      3. She lived in Pune since 2021.
      4. She is living in Pune since 2021.
      Show answer

      (a) ✓ · (b) ✓ (temporary flavour) · (c) ✗ · (d) ✗ · OPEN-CLOSED

      Why

      (a) continuative perfect, the plain claim; (b) adds lived-in texture and a hint the arrangement may not be forever. (c) seals the stretch in a closed box while since 2021 holds the container open — a genuine contradiction, the ✗ kind no context repairs; (d) same clash from the present progressive, which cannot host a since-container.
