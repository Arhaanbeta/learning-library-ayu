Four habits that make understanding dependable

A journey and its arrow

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Walk along one edge of a playground, then turn along the next. Your route has two pieces. Your change of place needs one arrow.

Distance adds the lengths of the route. Displacement is the straight arrow from the original start to the final finish.

Read the arrow in two parts: its length represents the size of the displacement; its arrowhead shows the direction.

Place successive displacement arrows head to tail. The combined arrow joins the first tail to the last head. Returning to the start gives a zero arrow.

Displacement is a vector: it has size and direction and combines by this arrow rule. Distance is a scalar: it needs a size and unit.

Signs can stand in for arrows on one line

Choose right as positive. Then +3 m means three metres right; −3 m means three metres left. Neither means “more” or “less real”.

displacement = final position − initial position

Both positions use the same origin. Moving that origin changes both position numbers equally, so their difference stays unchanged.

Tempting idea: A long journey must give a large displacement.

Why it seems reasonable: Both quantities use metres, so they sound interchangeable.

What actually happens: A complete lap has positive distance and zero displacement.

Why: One records the route; the other records the change of place.

Two people take different routes between the same start and finish. What must agree?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Their displacement arrows agree. Their distances and journey times may differ. Endpoints determine displacement; the route determines distance.

One arrow, two simple pieces

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Imagine giving someone directions to a point across a field. “Go this far east, then this far north” locates the same endpoint as one diagonal arrow.

Those eastward and northward pieces are components. They describe one displacement in two chosen directions; they are not extra journeys the object must actually make.

Call the horizontal component x and the vertical component y. The right triangle connects them to the diagonal's length r.

r² = x² + y²

Here x, y and r are lengths in metres. This is Pythagoras applied to the drawing, not a new physics law.

Components may be negative; a length cannot. Reversing the horizontal part changes the endpoint and direction, even if the diagonal's length stays the same.

To add several arrows, add all their horizontal components and all their vertical components. Rebuild the final arrow from those two totals.

How do we show a change of arrow?

Draw the old and new arrows from the same tail. The arrow from the old head to the new head is the change.

change = new vector − old vector

This subtraction will explain a turning car: equal speed arrows can have different directions, so their difference need not be zero.

Where sine, cosine and unit vectors come from

Keep an arrow's direction fixed and make it twice as long. Its right triangle doubles too. Each component therefore stays the same fraction of the arrow.

For an angle θ measured from the positive horizontal direction, the horizontal fraction is called cosine. The vertical fraction is called sine.

horizontal component = arrow length × cos θ

vertical component = arrow length × sin θ

At zero angle the whole arrow is horizontal: cosine is 1 and sine is 0. At a quarter-turn, those roles reverse.

These names record triangle geometry. You do not need a table of values to predict whether a component grows, shrinks or vanishes.

A unit vector is an arrow of length one with no unit attached: it carries direction alone. Multiplying by a quantity supplies its size and units.

The symbols î and ĵ often mean unit arrows along the chosen horizontal and vertical axes. Then xî + yĵ says “combine these two components”.

In three dimensions, add a third perpendicular component z. Applying Pythagoras twice gives r² = x² + y² + z².

Turning the axes changes the component numbers, not the physical arrow. Choosing axes along a slope often makes the physical explanation simpler.

Components along perpendicular axes never exceed the arrow's length. Along nearly parallel axes, large opposing pieces can cancel into a small resultant.

The nearly horizontal supporting strings in a later force diagram will show why that distinction matters physically.

Rotate an arrow without changing its length. Has the vector changed?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Yes. Direction is part of the quantity. A speed may stay constant while the velocity vector changes; circular motion will make this distinction essential.

Speed, velocity and viewpoint

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

A bus travels along a road. A seated passenger is still relative to the bus but moving relative to a tree. “Moving” needs a stated viewpoint.

A reference frame is the set of surroundings we measure positions against. For everyday road journeys, the ground is usually a convenient choice.

Speed tells how quickly distance is covered. Velocity tells how quickly position changes, including direction. Returning to the start separates them immediately.

average speed = total distance / total time

average velocity = displacement / total time

Both have units m/s. Average speed cannot be negative. Along a chosen line, negative average velocity means the overall displacement points in the negative direction.

A speedometer estimates speed over a short interval. A whole-journey average cannot reveal each stop or burst of speed. These are different questions about the journey.

On this position–time graph, moving right means later time. Moving upward means a more positive position; the vertical axis is not the road’s physical height.

The slope is position change divided by time: velocity. A steeper rising line means greater positive velocity. A horizontal line means the position is unchanged.

A falling line means motion towards more negative positions. It need not mean slowing down. Equal slopes mean equal velocities, even at different positions.

Area under this graph is not distance. A graph’s meaning comes from its axes. We will use area with a velocity–time graph instead.

Walking forwards along a steadily moving bus adds your motion to the bus’s motion, as seen from the road. Walking backwards subtracts it.

The passengers and roadside observer describe different velocities for the same person.

Half the journey time at one speed and half at another permits a simple average of those speeds. Equal distances do not: the slower part occupies more time.

Always return to total distance divided by total time.

Later, instantaneous velocity describes the value approached as we shorten the measuring interval around a moment. The mathematics lesson explains why this is not division by zero.

Where limits come from: measuring “right now”

A single photograph gives position, not velocity. Two photographs give a change of position over a time interval. That ratio is an average.

What if the trolley speeds up within the interval? Use photographs closer together. The shorter interval usually gives a better description of its motion near that instant.

For a smooth motion, these short-interval averages can approach one definite value. That approached value defines instantaneous velocity in the mathematical model.

On a position graph, the line joining two points gives average velocity. Such a joining line is a secant.

Bring the second point closer. The secant's slope approaches the slope of the locally straight direction of the curve: the tangent.

A limit is the value approached by the estimates. We never calculate “distance travelled in zero time divided by zero time”.

To see the mathematics gently, suppose x = At². Here x is position, t is time and A is a constant with units m/s².

At time t + h, position is A(t + h)². Subtract the position at t. The letter h represents a small, non-zero time interval.

position change = A[(t + h)² − t²] = A(2th + h²)

The old t² part cancels because we want only the change. Dividing by the elapsed time h gives the interval's average velocity.

average velocity = A(2t + h)

Cancellation is legal because h is not zero. As h approaches zero, the extra Ah term approaches zero, leaving the local velocity 2At.

Its units are (m/s²) × s = m/s. The result describes velocity; the original constant A was not itself the velocity.

The same approached value must result from nearby earlier and later times. At an ideal sharp corner, the two slopes disagree and one instantaneous velocity is undefined.

The notation v = dx/dt means this local rate, called a derivative. Read it as “how position changes locally with time”, not ordinary zero-over-zero division.

Real instruments use finite intervals and uncertain measurements. Extremely short intervals can amplify measurement noise. An ideal limit describes the model, not a perfect physical stopwatch.

Why rates can multiply and powers produce coefficients

A wheel advances a known distance per revolution. The wheel turns a known number of revolutions per second. Multiplying gives distance per second.

The intermediate “revolution” cancels in the units. This is the physical idea behind the chain rule: one change happens through another.

For local rates, dx/dt = (dx/dθ)(dθ/dt). Here θ is wheel angle; distance per angle multiplied by angle per time gives velocity.

If a law changes with position, its change with time depends both on the position sensitivity and how quickly the object passes through positions.

The square example also reveals a derivative pattern. Expanding (t + h)² produces two terms containing exactly one h, which is why the coefficient 2 survives.

For a cube, (t + h)³ produces three such terms. After dividing by h, the terms still containing h vanish in the limit.

This is the beginning of the power rule. Its coefficients come from counting the contributions that survive a small change; they are not arbitrary decorations.

If two quantities both vary, both contribute to their product's change. Ignoring one contribution would falsely predict that changing either factor alone has no effect.

Advanced differentiation rules organise these small-change accounts. The course uses their physical meaning before requiring any catalogue of symbolic techniques.

Two cyclists meet while moving in opposite directions. Which quantities can match at that instant, and which need not?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Their positions match in the chosen frame. Their speeds can match, but their velocities have opposite directions. Their accelerations require separate information.

At the top of a smooth position–time curve, its tangent is horizontal. Does this require its slope to remain zero afterwards?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. A horizontal tangent describes velocity at that instant. The curve may immediately slope downwards; acceleration describes that continuing change of slope.

A velocity can change in two ways

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Two trolleys have the same velocity now. One continues steadily; the other gains speed. Their next positions differ because their velocities change differently.

We need a quantity describing how quickly the velocity changes. That quantity is acceleration. It compares a velocity difference with the time taken.

average acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) / elapsed time

Its unit m/s² means “metres-per-second of velocity change during each second”. Both divisions matter: position changes, and the rate of that change changes.

Choose right as positive. A leftward acceleration reduces a rightward velocity, but increases the speed of an object already moving left.

Compare the directions of velocity and acceleration. Matching directions increase speed. Opposite directions reduce speed until the object stops and may reverse.

At fixed velocity change, twice the time means half the average acceleration. At fixed time, twice the vector change means twice the acceleration.

Turning also changes velocity

A car follows a bend at steady speed. Its velocity arrow turns, so its velocity changes. Acceleration need not point along the motion.

Later, we will separate acceleration into a part that changes speed and a perpendicular part that changes direction.

Tempting idea: Zero velocity means zero acceleration.

Why it seems reasonable: A still photograph shows no visible motion to change.

What actually happens: A ball thrown upwards has zero velocity at the top, while its acceleration remains downwards.

Why: Gravity continues changing velocity through zero; an instant of rest is not a lasting balance.

A trolley moves left while its acceleration points right. What happens immediately, and what could happen later?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

It first slows. If the acceleration persists, it reaches zero velocity and then speeds up to the right. Acceleration keeps its direction throughout.

A graph can rebuild a journey

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Mark a trolley's position once every second. Equal gaps mean equal average speeds over those intervals. Increasing gaps mean faster motion over successive intervals.

A graph makes such comparisons continuous. Always read its axis labels before interpreting a line's height, slope or area.

On a position–time graph, right means later and up means more positive position. Slope is velocity: a steeper rise means greater positive velocity.

On a velocity–time graph, height is velocity and slope is acceleration. A horizontal line means unchanged velocity, including steady motion if its height is non-zero.

On an acceleration–time graph, a horizontal line means unchanged acceleration. It does not mean unchanged velocity unless that line lies at zero.

Why area can mean displacement

For constant velocity, displacement is velocity multiplied by time. On the velocity graph, that product is a rectangle's area.

Split changing motion into short time strips. Each strip contributes approximately “velocity here × time width”. Add the strips to rebuild the displacement.

Above the time axis, strips add positive displacement. Below it, they add negative displacement. Adding all strip sizes instead gives distance travelled.

Area has meaning through units: (m/s) × s = m. Area under a position graph has metre-seconds, so it is not distance.

Build the motion equations from one straight line

Suppose acceleration a stays constant. Velocity gains the same amount in each equal time interval, so the velocity–time graph is straight.

Let u be initial velocity, v final velocity, t elapsed time and s displacement. Choose one positive direction for all signed quantities.

v = u + at

The product at is the velocity gained. Its units, (m/s²) × s, are m/s. If a is zero, velocity remains u.

The area consists of a rectangle of height u and a triangle whose extra height is at. The triangle has half a rectangle's area.

s = ut + ½at²

The first part is the displacement unchanged velocity would give. The second is the added displacement produced by acceleration over the interval.

The t² has a physical origin: more time lets velocity change more and lets that changed velocity act for longer.

The whole straight-line area's average height is (u + v)/2. Therefore s = ½(u + v)t. This endpoint average needs constant acceleration.

Use v − u = at to eliminate time: 2as = (u + v)(v − u). Expanding the brackets gives another description of the same motion.

v² = u² + 2as

Every term has units m²/s². Squaring removes direction information, so this equation alone cannot decide whether the final velocity points left or right.

If a varies, the graph bends. The straight-line formulas then need replacement by the actual slopes and accumulated strips.

The second reason limits appear: adding tiny contributions

Wide strips miss some of the changing height under a curved graph. Narrower strips follow it more closely.

For a smooth physical model, the sums approach a definite value as the strip widths shrink. This limiting sum is an integral.

displacement = ∫ v dt

The long S-shaped sign means “sum the contributions”. Here v is local velocity and dt identifies a tiny interval of time within that limiting construction.

This is not multiplication by zero. It is the limit of sums made from non-zero intervals. The resulting total can be finite.

Why does accumulation undo taking a slope? Extend the time interval a little. The extra area is approximately current velocity multiplied by that extra time.

Divide by the extra time: the area's rate of growth is velocity. Rate and accumulation are connected descriptions, not unrelated mathematical tricks.

An acceleration–time area similarly gives velocity change. A force–time area gives impulse; a power–time area gives energy. The axes specify what is accumulated.

Starting from rest with constant acceleration, double the time. What happens to velocity and displacement?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Velocity doubles. Displacement quadruples: the velocity–time triangle becomes twice as high and twice as wide. Hold the acceleration fixed for this comparison.

Two motions can share one clock

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Picture two balls at the same height. Release one without a push; launch the other horizontally at the same instant.

For now, ignore air resistance and use uniform downward gravity. Both start with zero vertical velocity and receive the same downward acceleration.

At each matching time they have fallen equally far. The launched ball also travels sideways. Its sideways motion does not supply vertical support.

This is projectile motion: after release, gravity is the only force in our model. Describe its horizontal and vertical changes separately, using the same time.

Let uₓ and uᵧ be the initial horizontal and upward velocity components. Choose the launch point as the origin, with upward positive.

x = uₓt

y = uᵧt − ½gt²

Here g is the magnitude of downward acceleration, in m/s². The letters x and y represent positions in metres; t is elapsed time in seconds.

Gravity supplies no horizontal acceleration here. Horizontal position changes steadily. Vertical motion combines the initial upward motion with an increasing downward departure.

Every term is a length. The minus sign records gravity's direction. At fixed t, changing uₓ changes x without changing y in this model.

For an upward-slanting launch, vertical velocity eventually reaches zero. Horizontal velocity need not. At the top, acceleration still points downwards.

Tempting idea: A faster sideways launch stays in the air longer.

Why it seems reasonable: A longer curved route looks as though it must require more time.

What actually happens: From the same height with the same initial vertical velocity, the landing times match.

Why: Gravity changes vertical motion equally; extra horizontal speed covers more sideways distance during that time.

Range, height and the boundary of what can be reached

Launch with positive upward component uᵧ and land at the same height. The rise and fall are symmetric when gravity is uniform and drag absent.

The upward velocity loses g metres-per-second each second. Time to the top is uᵧ/g; the return to launch height takes twice that time.

flight time T = 2uᵧ/g

horizontal range R = uₓT = 2uₓuᵧ/g

The range depends on both components: horizontal speed and the vertical speed that supplies time aloft. A nearly vertical launch has little horizontal reach.

At fixed launch speed u, Pythagoras fixes uₓ² + uᵧ² = u². The product uₓuᵧ is largest when those two positive components are equal.

Why? The square (uₓ − uᵧ)² cannot be negative. Expanding it gives u² − 2uₓuᵧ ≥ 0. Equality occurs when the components match.

Equal perpendicular components mean a 45° launch. This maximises range only under the stated same-height, fixed-speed, uniform-gravity, no-drag conditions.

Swapping the two components leaves their product unchanged. The two trajectories then have equal range but different heights and flight times.

Landing lower gives additional falling time. Landing on a hillside changes the endpoint condition. Use x(t) and y(t) again; the 45° conclusion need not survive.

At any chosen horizontal distance, compare all possible launch directions at fixed speed. Only a limited height is reachable there.

The tops of those possibilities form an envelope, a boundary of reachable points. That is the idea behind the source's “parabola of safety”.

Drag changes the forces. For ordinary quadratic air drag, its components depend on the total speed, so horizontal and vertical motion become coupled.

Components still describe the motion. What fails is assuming their acceleration laws remain independent and constant.

Motion depends on who measures it

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

A passenger tosses a ball vertically inside a smoothly moving train. It returns to the hand. A platform observer sees the ball travelling forwards too.

The ball already shared the train's forward velocity before release. Ignoring air effects, no horizontal interaction removes that velocity during flight.

Both accounts describe the same event. A reference frame supplies the positions and directions against which an observer measures motion.

velocity relative to observer = object velocity − observer velocity

Subtract the arrows, not just their lengths. If both share the same velocity, their relative velocity is zero even while both move relative to the ground.

For frames moving at constant relative velocity with non-rotating axes, both observers measure the same acceleration. Subtracting a constant velocity does not change velocity differences.

A river makes the addition visible

Point a boat straight across flowing water. Its motion relative to water combines with the water's motion relative to the bank.

boat relative to bank = boat relative to water + water relative to bank

Steering partly upstream can cancel downstream drift, but leaves a smaller across-river component at fixed boat speed through water.

If the current is faster than the boat's speed through water, no heading can cancel it completely. An impossible component is a physical limit, not an algebra failure.

Common acceleration and closest approach

Two freely flying balls experience the same uniform gravitational acceleration. Subtract their accelerations: their relative acceleration is zero.

Their individual paths can curve while their separation changes at constant relative velocity. Common motion disappears from a comparison.

Aiming one ball towards a target that begins falling at release uses this idea: both acquire the same extra downward displacement before meeting.

The meeting still requires enough time before either reaches the ground. Shared acceleration does not remove the initial geometry or physical boundaries.

Now imagine two boats moving at constant velocities. Watch from one boat. The other follows a straight relative path.

The closest point on that line lies along a perpendicular from you. At that instant, relative motion is sideways to the separation, not towards or away.

If that point lies behind the boat's current position, the nearest future position is already the present one. “Closest” must specify the time interval considered.

These comparisons use Newtonian motion, with speeds small compared with light speed. Later relativity refines how different observers compare space and time.

Rain falls vertically relative to the ground. Why does it seem to slant towards a runner?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Subtract the runner's forward velocity from the rain's downward velocity. The relative arrow has a backward component. The runner changed viewpoint; the rain need not have changed direction.

What changes motion?

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Push a trolley, then let go. It slows on a rough floor. Everyday experience suggests that motion needs a continuing push. Now imagine reducing the friction.

The trolley keeps moving longer. Remove all opposing interactions in the ideal model and there is no reason for its velocity to change.

Newton’s first law: with zero net external force, an object keeps the same velocity. That includes remaining still. Inertia names this persistence of motion.

Several objects may push or pull on the trolley. Their combined effect, added with directions, is the net force. A force is an interaction, measured in newtons, N.

This is a force diagram: isolate one object and draw only forces acting on it. Name their sources. An arrow showing motion would be a different kind of arrow.

How strongly will the velocity change?

For the same mass, a greater net force produces greater acceleration. For the same net force, a greater mass produces less acceleration.

acceleration = net force / mass, or Fnet = ma

Here m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in m/s². One newton changes a 1 kg mass’s velocity by 1 m/s each second.

Double net force while mass stays fixed: acceleration doubles. Double mass while net force stays fixed: acceleration halves. Reverse net force: acceleration reverses.

Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity. Balanced forces can act on a moving object.

This form describes fixed-mass Newtonian motion in an inertial frame: a non-rotating viewpoint where force-free objects move at constant velocity.

As the bus accelerates, your feet move with the floor while the rest of you initially keeps its earlier velocity. You lean back relative to the bus.

From the roadside, no mysterious backward force is needed; the bus is moving ahead beneath you.

A book rests on a table. Earth pulls it down; the table pushes it up. Equal forces on this one book give no acceleration.

Next we must distinguish this balance from an interaction pair.

A table supports a book because neighbouring atoms resist being compressed into a different arrangement. Electrical interactions and quantum behaviour supply the microscopic contact-force mechanism.

Why a table can push back

A book slightly deforms the table and itself. Their atoms are displaced from their preferred arrangements, creating a restoring contact force.

The deformation may be too small to see. Its effect is measurable: the table pushes upward on the book.

Electrical interactions among nuclei and electrons, together with quantum restrictions on electron arrangements, underlie ordinary solid contact. Atoms are not simply hard miniature billiard balls.

The useful school-level model is an elastic response: pressing changes an arrangement; the changed arrangement resists further compression.

This is an organic connection to bonding. The chemical composition and atomic structure help determine stiffness; the force diagram describes the resulting physical push.

When a material breaks or permanently deforms, its arrangement changes differently. An ideal rigid-body or simple spring model then reaches its limit.

Two objects have the same acceleration under different net forces. What does that suggest about their masses within this model?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Force divided by mass must match. The object experiencing greater force must have proportionally greater mass; matching acceleration does not imply matching force.

Weight and falling together

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Press your palm against a wall. Your hand pushes the wall, and the wall pushes your hand. Both forces belong to one interaction and occur together.

Newton’s third law: the forces two objects exert on each other are equal in size and opposite in direction. They act on different objects.

The wall’s force on your hand belongs on your hand’s force diagram. Your hand’s force on the wall belongs on the wall’s diagram. They do not cancel there.

The book’s upward support and downward weight can balance, but they are not a third-law pair: both act on the book and have different partners.

Mass, weight and support are different

Mass measures inertia and is measured in kg. Weight is Earth’s gravitational pull, measured in N. Near Earth’s surface, weight is W = mg.

Here g is the downward acceleration of free fall, about 9.8 m/s², also expressible as 9.8 N/kg. “Newtons per kilogram” says how much pull each kilogram experiences.

Double mass at the same location: weight doubles. Move where g is smaller: weight decreases while mass stays the same.

Gravity gives a heavier object more force but it also has proportionally more inertia. Ignoring air resistance, a = mg/m = g: both effects grow together.

Support force is a surface’s push, perpendicular to it; “normal force” is its technical name. A scale senses support. A freely falling scale reads zero although gravity acts.

Release both at the same height and time, with no initial vertical velocity. Ignoring air resistance, their downward motion matches. The thrown ball also travels sideways.

The two motions combine; the sideways launch does not delay its fall.

A ball thrown upward has zero vertical velocity at its highest point. Gravity is still changing that velocity downward.

Before the top it slows the rise; afterwards it speeds the fall. The interaction never switched off.

Outside an approximately spherical body, gravitational strength decreases with distance squared. Doubling distance from its centre quarters the field, with source mass held fixed.

This explains why altitude changes weight without changing mass. Height above the ground differs from distance measured from Earth’s centre.

Uniform downward acceleration lets us describe sideways and vertical motion separately, then combine their displacement arrows. This becomes projectile motion without requiring new force laws.

A lift and an accelerating observer

Stand on a scale inside a lift. The scale measures its upward support force N on you, not your mass directly.

With upward positive and local g unchanged, your vertical force equation is N − mg = ma. Therefore N = m(g + a).

Upward acceleration requires N greater than mg. Downward acceleration requires less. Steady upward motion has a = 0, so the reading returns to mg.

In free fall, a = −g and support becomes zero. Weight remains mg. You feel weightless because the supporting interaction disappears, not because gravity disappears.

Now watch a loose object from a train accelerating forwards. Relative to the train it accelerates backwards even if no horizontal force acts on it.

Relative acceleration is object acceleration minus frame acceleration A. Rearranging the ground-frame equation gives:

relative acceleration a′ = a − A

m a′ = Freal − mA

The term −mA is called an inertial force or pseudo-force. It corrects an accelerating coordinate description; it does not identify an extra interacting object.

Its direction opposes the frame's acceleration and its unit is a newton. In the ground frame, use actual interactions and ground-frame acceleration without that correction.

A turning frame needs rotational corrections too. The outward centrifugal term describes a rotating viewpoint; a stationary observer instead describes the required inward acceleration.

A freely sliding object can also trace a curved path relative to a rotating platform. The platform turns under its path. The associated correction is called Coriolis.

These effects are measurable differences between coordinate descriptions. Do not combine a rotating-frame correction with an acceleration taken from the stationary frame.

From falling to orbiting

Near Earth's surface, height changes are usually small compared with Earth's radius. Gravitational field strength g then changes so little that treating it as constant works well.

Farther away, that approximation fails. Newton's gravitational model for point masses, or non-overlapping spherically symmetric bodies, uses their centre-to-centre distance r.

gravitational force magnitude F = GMm/r²

M and m are the two masses. G is the universal gravitational constant, with units N m²/kg². It sets the strength of this interaction.

At fixed distance and one mass, doubling the other mass doubles the force. At fixed masses, doubling r reduces the force to one-quarter.

The inverse-square shape fits the growing area of spheres around a source. This geometric picture helps interpret the observed law; geometry alone does not prove gravity's law.

Dividing force on the small mass by that mass gives g = GM/r². The test object's mass cancels; the source mass and distance remain.

For a circular orbit, gravity must supply the required mv²/r. “Gravity” and “centripetal force” are two descriptions of the same interaction's role here.

Astronauts and spacecraft fall together. Small differences in gravity across a large region produce tidal effects, so free fall does not erase every gravitational effect everywhere.

Why a movable pulley trades force for distance

Imagine a load hanging from a pulley supported by two vertical sections of one string. Each section pulls upward with tension T.

To keep the total string length fixed, the free end must move 2h. The free end travels twice as far as the load.

This is a constraint: a restriction from geometry. It is why the velocities need not match even though the string is inextensible.

Friction responds to slipping

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Push a crate gently. It stays still. Push harder; it may still stay still. The floor's sideways force changes to balance each push.

This static friction prevents relative slipping at the contact. It adjusts up to a limit; it is not automatically at that limit.

static-friction magnitude ≤ μₛN

N is the perpendicular support force. The symbol μₛ, “mu subscript s”, is a dimensionless measure of the maximum grip for this pair of surfaces.

The sign ≤ means “no greater than”. Doubling N doubles the model's maximum available grip, not necessarily the friction actually required.

Once slipping occurs, a common dry-contact approximation is sliding-friction magnitude ≈ μₖN. Here μₖ describes sliding and often differs from μₛ.

Microscopic contact regions deform and adhere. Friction emerges from their interactions. Apparent roughness alone does not predict it; these simple coefficient models have limited ranges.

Friction can speed something up

Put a book on a trolley that starts moving forwards. The book initially tends to slip backwards relative to the trolley. Friction on it points forwards.

The same idea explains walking. A foot pushes the ground backwards; the ground's friction pushes the person forwards.

Tempting idea: Friction always opposes an object's motion.

Why it seems reasonable: A sliding book usually slows because of friction.

What actually happens: Friction opposes relative slipping or its tendency at the contact.

Why: The two contacting surfaces may themselves be moving; friction can accelerate an object in the ground frame.

A slope is a test of components and assumptions

Tilt a board holding a block. Gravity remains vertical. The board's support remains perpendicular to its surface. Neither arrow should rotate merely to suit the algebra.

Choose one axis down the slope and another perpendicular to it. Gravity then has a down-slope part and an into-surface part.

At slope angle θ, those parts have magnitudes mg sin θ and mg cos θ. Check a flat board: the down-slope part must vanish.

For a stationary board, maintained contact and no other perpendicular force, N = mg cos θ. This equality follows from the perpendicular acceleration being zero.

To remain at rest, the block needs up-slope friction mg sin θ. Compare that requirement with the available maximum μₛmg cos θ.

The limiting slope satisfies sin θ / cos θ = μₛ. The ratio is called tan θ, so the familiar expression tan θ = μₛ records this balance.

This is a single-block model. Real sand heaps also depend on particle shapes, moisture and collective rearrangements; they are not exact copies of one sliding block.

If the block is already sliding uphill, sliding friction points downhill. Gravity and friction then both slow the upward motion.

If a rope pulls the block, recheck every force and the slipping tendency. The slope alone does not determine friction's direction.

For stacked blocks, first suppose they move together. Find the friction needed to accelerate the upper block; compare it with its maximum grip.

Imagine a ball following a horizontal circle. At each point, its velocity points along the tangent: the direction it would immediately follow if released.

To remain on the circle, the ball must keep changing that direction. Compare two nearby velocity arrows using the head-to-head difference already learned.

As the time interval shrinks, that change points inward. At constant speed, acceleration points towards the centre even though the velocity points sideways to it.

The faster the ball travels, the faster its direction must turn. A smaller circle also bends the motion more sharply at the same speed.

inward acceleration a = v²/r

Here v is speed in m/s and r is radius in metres. The units (m²/s²)/m give m/s², as acceleration requires.

At fixed radius, twice the speed requires four times the inward acceleration. At fixed speed, twice the radius requires half as much.

Newton's law therefore requires a net inward force mv²/r. Centripetal names this inward role; it does not name an additional interaction.

String tension can supply it. Tyre friction can supply it. Gravity supplies it for an ideal circular orbit. Draw the actual forces and find their inward sum.

Tempting idea: Add a separate centripetal force to the force diagram.

Why it seems reasonable: Circular motion seems to need a new force with its own name.

What actually happens: “Centripetal” describes what the net inward force must do.

Why: Adding it again would count the same inward interaction twice.

Why speed is squared

During a tiny interval, the radius turns through a small angle. The tangent velocity turns through exactly that same angle.

The two radius arrows form one thin triangle. The two velocity arrows form another. Their matching angles make the triangles similar.

size of velocity change / v ≈ small distance travelled / r

For the short interval Δt, the small travelled distance is approximately vΔt. Substitute this into the right side and multiply by v.

size of velocity change ≈ v²Δt/r

Divide by Δt and shrink the interval. The acceleration magnitude approaches v²/r. One v describes travel speed; the other converts turning into velocity change.

At fixed v, a very large r gives a nearly straight path and very small inward acceleration. At v = 0 this circular-motion requirement is zero.

Taking r towards zero at fixed non-zero v would require unbounded acceleration. A real string, tyre or structure cannot supply unlimited force.

If speed also changes, add a tangential acceleration along or opposite the velocity. It changes speed; the inward component changes direction.

The two perpendicular components combine as vectors. Neither one alone describes the full acceleration of a speeding-up turn.

Falling faster changes the air's response

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Drop an object through air. Gravity pulls down from the beginning, while the air's upward drag grows as the object falls faster.

Choose downward as positive. The net downward force is weight minus drag. It is this difference, not weight alone, that determines acceleration.

downward acceleration = (weight − drag) / mass

Initially, weight exceeds drag, so downward speed increases. Growing drag reduces the net force and therefore reduces acceleration.

Eventually drag can equal weight. Net force and acceleration then become zero, while downward speed remains non-zero. This steady falling speed is terminal speed.

Open a parachute. Its greater effective area suddenly increases drag at the existing speed. The net force becomes upward while the motion is still downward.

The falling object slows. As speed decreases, drag decreases until it again matches weight, now at a lower terminal speed.

For many everyday fast flows, drag is approximately proportional to speed squared when shape, orientation and air density stay fixed. This is a model, not universal behaviour.

Doubling speed can then require roughly four times the opposing force. A changed posture or parachute changes the relationship itself, so hold conditions explicit.

At terminal speed, kinetic energy stops increasing. Yet gravity still transfers energy through the continuing downward displacement; air motion and warming receive it.

Balanced force therefore does not imply zero energy transfer. Steady motion and zero net work on the falling object's kinetic energy can coexist with large transfers.

In orbit, air resistance may be very small, but gravity remains. An orbiting object continually falls while moving sideways, so its path keeps curving around Earth.

Astronauts float relative to their spacecraft because both fall together. Floating is evidence of little support force, not evidence that gravity has disappeared.

Why the last part of the speed increase becomes smaller

Release a falling object. Initially drag is small, so weight produces a substantial downward acceleration. As speed rises, drag grows and the net force shrinks.

This is a negative feedback: the change creates an effect that reduces further change. It approaches a force balance rather than generating endless acceleration.

For a slow small object in an appropriate viscous-flow regime, drag magnitude can be approximated as bv. The constant b has units N s/m.

With downward positive, m a = mg − bv. The terminal speed vₜ satisfies bvₜ = mg, so vₜ = mg/b.

Replace mg by bvₜ. Then a = (b/m)(vₜ − v): acceleration is proportional to the remaining gap below terminal speed.

As the gap shrinks, its rate of shrinking decreases. Equal time intervals reduce the remaining gap by the same fraction in this ideal linear model.

That pattern is called exponential approach. It also appears in simple cooling models. The shared structure is “change rate proportional to the remaining difference”.

Many falling objects instead experience approximately quadratic drag, proportional to v². The balance idea survives, but the detailed curve and terminal-speed formula change.

At terminal speed, kinetic energy stays constant while gravitational energy continues transferring to the surroundings. Force balance does not require an end to energy transfer.

A falling object has reached terminal speed. Does it lose gravitational potential energy, gain kinetic energy, or warm its surroundings? Which claims can hold together?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

It continues losing gravitational potential energy and transfers energy to air and surroundings. Its kinetic energy stays constant because its speed is constant.

A push lasts: momentum and impulse

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Stopping a moving trolley requires an interaction sustained for a time. Compare equal velocities: the heavier trolley needs more force, more time, or both.

We combine mass and velocity into momentum, a vector that helps describe how motion is exchanged in interactions.

momentum p = mv

Its unit kg m/s means mass multiplied by velocity. At fixed velocity, doubling mass doubles momentum. At fixed mass, reversing velocity reverses momentum. Rest gives zero momentum.

Now let a steady net force act for time Δt. The symbol Δ means “change” or “interval”. From Fnet = ma and a = Δv/Δt:

Fnet Δt = m Δv = Δp

The left side is impulse: force multiplied by the time it acts. Its unit N s equals kg m/s. The right side is the momentum change of a fixed mass.

If the force varies, use its average over the interval. The area under a force–time graph gives impulse: each strip contributes force × a little time.

For the same stopping change, increasing stopping time reduces average force. Moving your hands back while catching a ball applies this idea.

Interactions exchange momentum

When two skaters push apart, equal opposite forces act for the same time. They gain equal opposite momentum changes. Their combined momentum stays unchanged.

This conservation holds for a chosen system with zero net external impulse. Individual momenta may change greatly. Internal forces exchange momentum within the boundary.

The rocket pushes exhaust backwards; the exhaust pushes the rocket forwards. For rocket plus exhaust, momentum can balance without air to push against.

The rocket alone loses mass, so we must account for the momentum carried away.

If external impulse is negligible, total momentum is conserved whether objects bounce or stick.

Their kinetic energy, the energy of motion, need not be conserved: deformation, sound and warming can receive energy. Equal opposite momenta can cancel; positive kinetic energies cannot.

Gas pressure will reuse this idea: each collision transfers momentum, while countless collisions give a nearly steady average force on the wall.

Why the system boundary controls conservation

Catch a ball while standing on the ground. The ball's momentum changes. The ball-plus-you system can also exchange momentum with Earth through your feet.

Enlarging the boundary changes which interactions count as external. It does not change the physical event.

For a fixed collection of matter, total momentum change equals net external impulse. Zero net external impulse means unchanged total momentum over that interval.

An individual external force can be non-zero while external forces cancel. Conversely, a short impact does not automatically make every external impulse negligible.

Compare the impulse being ignored with the important momentum changes. Neglecting gravity during a brief strong collision can be a good approximation, but needs that comparison.

For two skaters initially at rest pushing apart, total momentum stays zero if external horizontal impulse is negligible. The lighter skater then has the greater speed.

Equal opposite momentum changes follow because their equal opposite forces act for the same interval. The action and reaction do not act on one skater.

Spread a specified momentum change over longer time: average force decreases. A smaller average alone does not guarantee a smaller peak without information about the pulse shape.

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

A whole object's motion has a centre

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Toss a lopsided dumbbell gently in your imagination. Its ends tumble along complicated paths, but one special point follows a smooth projectile path.

That point is the centre of mass: the average position of the mass, giving heavier portions proportionally more influence.

For two equal masses, it lies halfway between them. Make one mass larger and the point moves towards it. Equal position weighting would miss this change.

xcm = (m₁x₁ + m₂x₂)/(m₁ + m₂)

The numerator combines mass × position. Dividing by total mass leaves metres. Positions may be negative if they lie on the negative side of the origin.

You can picture three equal mass portions: two at one end, one at the other. Their position average is the same as treating the first end as twice as massive.

In uniform gravity, this point is also where the body's weight can be represented for turning calculations. A ring's centre of mass lies in empty space.

Why this point is useful for motion

Internal pushes exchange momentum between parts. When their momentum changes are added, those internal contributions cancel in pairs.

total momentum = total mass × centre-of-mass velocity

For a fixed collection of matter, only net external force changes that total momentum. Its centre of mass therefore accelerates as total external force divided by total mass.

An exploding projectile's fragments can separate dramatically while their centre of mass continues the original path, if gravity is their only net external interaction.

Symmetry, missing pieces and why the axis matters

For a uniform symmetric object, pair each piece of mass with its mirror partner. Their average lies on the symmetry line or plane.

This can locate the centre without algebra. Symmetry of shape alone is insufficient if one side contains denser material.

Divide a continuous body into small pieces. Add each piece's mass times position, then divide by total mass. Smaller pieces make the approximation more accurate.

The advanced integral is this same mass-weighted sum carried to a limit. No new physical definition is being introduced.

If an off-centre piece is removed, subtract both its mass and its mass-times-position contribution. The remaining centre shifts away from the removed material.

The subtracted mass is an accounting operation, not a claim that negative matter exists. Reattaching the missing piece must reconstruct the original centre.

Viewed from the centre of mass, total momentum is zero. Two bodies then have equal opposite momenta, although their speeds differ if their masses differ.

Later, this viewpoint makes collisions easier: separate the group's overall travel from motion of its parts relative to each other.

Two people on an otherwise isolated freely floating platform exchange places. Can internal movements accelerate the whole system's centre of mass?

No. Their individual positions change, and the platform may move. But internal interactions only redistribute momentum; the combined centre of mass keeps its original constant velocity.

Force acting through a distance

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Push a trolley along the floor. Your force changes its motion while the trolley moves. Physics calls this mode of energy transfer work.

Energy is tracked through motion, stored arrangements and internal changes. It can transfer between systems. Work names one such transfer, not a separate substance.

For a constant force acting along an object’s displacement, more force or more distance means more transferred energy.

work W = Fd

Here F is force in newtons and d is displacement along the force in metres. The unit is N m, called a joule, J.

At fixed force, doubling distance doubles work. At fixed distance, doubling force doubles work. The equation applies directly when force and displacement point the same way.

An opposing force does negative work: it removes kinetic energy from the object. A perpendicular force does zero work on its motion along that displacement.

For a slanting force, only its part along the displacement contributes. A perpendicular component can change direction without changing speed at that instant.

This is one reason force needs an arrow.

Following the energy account

If an object starts from rest and gains speed, it gains kinetic energy: energy associated with motion. Net work on an object changes its kinetic energy.

net work = change in kinetic energy

“Net” matters again. Your push may do positive work while friction does negative work. Add the work of all forces to find the kinetic energy change.

“Holding a heavy bag still must count as work.” Your muscles tire, so this seems obvious.

But the bag has no displacement: you do no mechanical work on it while holding it still. Chemical processes within your muscles still use energy and warm your body.

In ideal steady circular motion, the inward force is perpendicular to the instantaneous motion. It changes velocity’s direction without changing speed or kinetic energy.

Forces can change direction without transferring energy to that motion.

Force acting through time changes momentum; force acting through distance transfers energy. The units N s and N m distinguish impulse from work.

This particle-style work–kinetic-energy description isolates translational motion. Deformable bodies and rotation require additional parts of the energy account.

Why one vector product measures alignment

Pull a suitcase with an inclined strap while it moves horizontally. Split your force into horizontal and vertical components.

Only the horizontal component acts through the suitcase's horizontal displacement. The vertical component may change its support force, but contributes no work along that displacement.

If θ is the angle between constant force F and displacement d, the aligned component is F cos θ. Both F and d here mean magnitudes.

work W = (F cos θ)d

The shorthand F · d is called a dot product. It multiplies one arrow's length by the other arrow's signed projection along it.

Aligned arrows give positive work. Perpendicular arrows give zero. An opposing component gives negative work. The result is a signed scalar, not a force arrow.

If force changes along the path, add the short pieces of force-along-motion times displacement. A force–displacement graph's signed area gives this work in one dimension.

An upward graph height means positive chosen force component; right means increasing position. A graph's negative slope does not itself mean negative work.

Negative work comes from force opposing the displacement. A decreasing force can remain positive and still supply positive work.

With a curved path, re-evaluate the local displacement direction. A single final displacement arrow generally cannot replace the whole route for variable forces.

Imagine identical trolleys brought to rest by the same steady braking force. One starts twice as fast. Predict the stopping distance before looking at an equation.

Same force and same mass give the same deceleration. The faster trolley has twice as much speed to lose, so it takes twice as long to stop.

During each stop, speed falls steadily to zero. The faster trolley’s average speed is also twice as large.

Twice the average speed for twice the time gives four times the distance.

The vertical axis is speed; the horizontal axis is time. Area means distance because speed × time has units of metres. Each stopping graph is a triangle.

The larger triangle is twice as high and twice as wide. Its area is four times as large. The same braking force therefore does four times as much stopping work.

Compress the reasoning into an equation

Let m be mass, v starting speed, F the positive braking-force magnitude and t stopping time. The fixed-mass impulse relation gives Ft = mv, hence t = mv/F.

The triangle gives d = ½vt. Substituting the stopping time is allowed because it describes this same stop: d = mv²/(2F).

The magnitude of braking work is Fd. The F cancels, leaving the energy removed:

kinetic energy K = ½mv²

Here v is speed, so K is never negative. The unit kg m²/s² equals a joule. Zero speed gives zero kinetic energy in that reference frame.

At fixed speed, doubling mass doubles K. At fixed mass, doubling speed quadruples K. Halving speed leaves one-quarter of the kinetic energy.

Steady braking made the graph easy to read. For ordinary speeds, ½mv² describes translational kinetic energy regardless of how the object was accelerated.

We have used a simple process to discover a general relationship.

Storing energy in an arrangement

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Lift a book slowly. You push upward while it moves upward, transferring energy. When released, it can regain motion. What changed was the book–Earth arrangement.

The stored energy is gravitational potential energy. Near Earth, weight mg is approximately constant. Lifting through height h against that force requires work mgh.

increase in gravitational potential energy = mgh

Here m is mass, g gravitational field strength and h the rise in height. The units kg × N/kg × m give N m, or joules.

At fixed g and height, double mass: double the increase. At fixed mass and g, double height: double it. A sideways move at the same height makes no gravitational change.

We choose a reference height where gravitational potential energy is zero. Heights below it can have negative values. The physically useful quantity is the energy difference.

A spring stores energy differently

Stretch a spring gently. The farther you stretch it, the stronger it pulls back. For an ideal spring within its elastic range, pulling-force magnitude F = kx.

Here x is extension from the relaxed length, and k is stiffness in N/m. Double x at fixed k: double the force.

The spring’s restoring force points back towards its relaxed position.

On a force–extension graph, each strip represents force × a little extension: work. The total area under the straight line is a triangle.

elastic energy = ½ × x × kx = ½kx²

The one-half appears because force rises from zero, rather than staying at kx throughout. Double extension within this model: stored energy quadruples. At zero extension it is zero.

A marble in a bowl loses gravitational potential energy as it rolls towards the bottom. At rest there, a small displacement creates a restoring tendency: stable equilibrium.

On an upside-down bowl, a small displacement carries it farther away: unstable equilibrium. Energy and force describe the same arrangement from different viewpoints.

A spring stretched too far may stay deformed. Then F = kx and ½kx² are no longer reliable. A model’s boundary matters as much as its equation.

What a potential-energy graph tells you

Picture an ideal spring with a mass. At equilibrium its interaction energy is lowest. Stretching or compressing it raises that energy.

Plot potential energy U vertically against displacement x horizontally. This graph is an energy account, not a picture of a real hill beneath the mass.

If U decreases when x increases, the conservative interaction can supply positive work for rightward motion. Its force therefore points right.

If U increases to the right, that force points left. A steeper local energy change per metre means a stronger force.

force along x = − local slope of U against x

The units J/m equal N. The minus sign links positive work to a decrease in the system's potential energy.

In advanced notation, Fₓ = −dU/dx. This is the derivative idea already used for local velocity, now applied to energy changing with position.

A flat tangent means zero force from that interaction. A local minimum is stable: small displacements on either side produce forces back towards it.

A local maximum is unstable. A flat region is neutral in this coordinate. Zero slope alone does not tell you which case you have.

If total mechanical energy E remains constant, K = E − U. The vertical gap between the E line and U curve represents kinetic energy.

Where U exceeds E, classical motion under this isolated mechanical model is impossible because K cannot be negative. Ordinary crossings with E = U mark turning points.

At an equilibrium with exactly matching total energy, the body may instead remain at rest. A point of zero speed is not automatically a turn.

Changing the chosen zero shifts U and E together. It changes neither their gap nor the force from the slope.

The same stable minimum inside matter

Two atoms at a stable bonded separation have lower interaction energy than nearby compressed or separated arrangements. The preferred spacing is an energy minimum.

Move them slightly closer and the restoring force tends to separate them. Move them slightly farther apart and it tends to bring them back.

Near the minimum, the curve resembles a spring's shallow bowl. This explains why small atomic vibrations and small elastic deformations can behave approximately like springs.

Pulling bonded atoms apart requires energy. Bond breaking does not itself release stored energy; it moves the arrangement up towards a separated state.

A reaction may release energy overall when new interactions form at a lower total energy than the initial arrangement. Compare the complete beginning and ending systems.

Why some energy stores depend on arrangement alone

Raise a book between two shelves along different routes. In uniform gravity, only the total upward height change contributes to gravitational work.

Upward and downward detours cancel in that work account. Returning to the same position gives zero net gravitational work.

A conservative force permits this path-independent work account. The corresponding potential energy can therefore belong to the configuration, rather than to its history.

Sliding friction against a stationary rough floor behaves differently. An outward and return trip both transfer organised motion into internal energy.

Returning to the start does not undo that transfer. A potential-energy value depending only on the box's final position cannot record it.

Do not extend this stationary-floor example into “friction always does negative work”. A moving conveyor can do positive work on a slipping object.

For distant gravitational motion, the convenient zero is infinite separation. Newton's model then gives U = −GMm/r for the two-body arrangement.

G, M, m and r have the same meanings as in the gravitational-force law. Its units are joules; the value approaches zero from below as separation grows.

Negative U marks the reference choice and the energy needed to separate an attractive pair. It does not mean a negative amount of substance or motion.

For small height changes near Earth's surface, this varying-distance account reduces approximately to ΔU = mgΔh.

If everyone chooses a different zero for gravitational potential energy, could they still agree on the energy released during the same fall?

Energy, power and efficiency

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

A pendulum speeds up while descending and slows while rising. Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy exchange. With negligible friction, their sum stays constant.

Real pendulums gradually stop. Air and the pivot receive energy as internal motion and sound. Mechanical energy — kinetic plus potential — decreases, while total energy remains accounted for.

For an isolated system, no energy enters or leaves, so total energy stays constant. For a system with transfers across its boundary, include those transfers in the account.

The same energy can be transferred at different rates

Two motors lift the same load to the same height. Their useful work matches. The one completing the lift sooner has greater average power.

average power P = energy transferred / time taken

One watt, W, is one joule per second. At fixed energy, halving the time doubles power. At fixed time, transferring twice the energy doubles it.

If power stays constant, energy transferred = Pt. A power–time graph’s area gives energy: add the contributions from successive intervals when power varies.

Be careful: W as a unit means watt; W as a symbol earlier meant work. The surrounding words and units tell you which is intended.

Useful for which purpose?

A lamp transfers energy into visible light and thermal energy. If our purpose is lighting, only some output is useful. The rest still exists.

efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input

Multiply by 100% to express the fraction as a percentage. The ratio has no units because both energies use the same unit. The useful purpose must be stated.

“Friction destroys energy.” Motion disappearing makes this believable. But rough surfaces deform and excite microscopic motion: they warm.

Energy is conserved; energy concentrated in organised motion becomes dispersed and harder to recover fully as useful work.

Two objects stick together in an isolated collision. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy may decrease. Ask where that energy went before calling it “lost”.

The thermal lessons examine the microscopic part of that account.

Chemical energy depends on the arrangement of atoms and electrons together with possible reaction partners. Breaking bonds costs energy; forming new interactions can release more.

Useful output depends on purpose. Warming from a lamp is unwanted for lighting, while an electric heater is designed to warm its surroundings.

Where the energy account goes next

Nuclear processes change nuclei rather than only electron arrangements. Some fission and fusion reactions release energy because their final products have lower total rest mass.

Later physics relates rest energy E and mass m through E = mc²; c is light speed in vacuum. Released energy corresponds to decreased system mass.

The principle remains an account of the complete system. Nuclear changes require a different microscopic model from chemical rearrangements, and are only a forward connection here.

A motor lifts the same load twice, taking half the time on the second lift. What can you infer about useful energy, power and efficiency?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Useful energy matches and average useful power doubles. Efficiency cannot be inferred without knowing total input energy and losses.

What can survive an impact?

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Picture two trolleys meeting on a nearly frictionless track. During contact they deform and push on each other. Those pushes are equal and opposite.

For the same contact interval, their momentum changes are equal and opposite. If external impulse is negligible, the two-trolley system keeps its total momentum.

But deformation may leave energy in microscopic motion, sound or a changed internal arrangement. Total kinetic energy need not return to its original value.

An elastic collision preserves total kinetic energy as well as momentum under this isolation assumption. In an inelastic collision, kinetic energy changes.

If the trolleys stick, the collision is perfectly inelastic. They share a final velocity, but need not stop. Non-zero initial total momentum requires continuing motion.

before: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = after: (m₁ + m₂)v

Here u₁ and u₂ are signed initial velocities; v is their shared final velocity. Every term has units kg m/s.

The shared velocity is a mass-weighted average. Momentum explains it without requiring the detailed contact force at every instant.

Tempting idea: Momentum conservation means kinetic energy is conserved too.

Why it seems reasonable: Both quantities describe motion and often appear in the same event.

What actually happens: Equal opposite momenta can sum to zero while both objects have positive kinetic energy.

Why: Momentum includes direction; kinetic energy uses speed squared. Their conservation conditions answer different questions.

What “bounciness” measures

Before contact, two bodies approach each other. Afterwards, they may separate. Compare the relative speeds along the line of impact, not their separate ground speeds.

coefficient of restitution e = separation speed / approach speed

This dimensionless ratio describes recovery from the impact. For ordinary passive impacts, e lies between 0 and 1; e = 1 represents full normal-speed recovery.

If e = 0, there is no relative separation speed along that line immediately afterwards. In a one-dimensional sticking model, both bodies share one velocity.

A real value can depend on material, impact speed and condition. It is not a universal number belonging permanently to a substance.

For a vertical bounce on a stationary massive floor, with negligible drag, rebound height / drop height = e². The square comes from kinetic energy's v².

Equal masses in a one-dimensional elastic collision exchange velocities. A very heavy stationary target changes speed little while a light incoming body can reverse.

Internal stored energy can also be released during an impact. Then kinetic energy can increase; conservation of total energy still holds.

One event can need two different accounts

Imagine a soft ball lodging in a hanging catcher. During the brief impact, horizontal external impulse may be negligible, making horizontal momentum conservation useful.

Kinetic energy decreases during the lodging. Do not carry a mechanical-energy conservation assumption across that stage.

During the subsequent swing, negligible friction allows kinetic energy to become gravitational potential energy. The strings exert external forces, so momentum need not stay constant.

The model changes because the dominant interactions and interval changed. “Use conservation” is incomplete until the conserved quantity, system and conditions are named.

In two dimensions, conserve both momentum components. For smooth elastic equal spheres, one initially at rest, non-zero outgoing velocities are perpendicular after an oblique collision.

Why? The final velocity vectors sum to the initial one, while their squared lengths sum to its squared length. That is the right-triangle condition.

This conclusion needs those assumptions. Spin, tangential impulses, unequal masses or an inelastic impact can change the angle.

What if the system gains or loses matter?

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Sand falls vertically onto a moving conveyor belt. Before landing it has no horizontal velocity. After settling, it travels horizontally with the belt.

The belt must continually give incoming sand horizontal momentum. Sand pushes backwards on the belt, so maintaining belt speed requires an extra driving force.

required extra force = mass arriving per second × horizontal velocity gained

If q kilograms arrive each second and gain speed v, the ideal steady requirement is F = qv. Units (kg/s) × (m/s) give newtons.

Doubling q at fixed v doubles that force. Once an individual grain matches the belt speed, this acceleration ends; newly arriving grains keep the demand going.

A rocket sends momentum outwards

A rocket accelerates exhaust backwards relative to itself. Through that interaction, the exhaust pushes the rocket forwards. No surrounding air is needed as a reaction partner.

ideal thrust magnitude = exhaust speed relative to rocket × mass ejected per second

This simple expression assumes steady exhaust and leaves out an additional nozzle pressure term that real engine calculations may require.

The remaining rocket loses mass. A constant thrust can therefore produce increasing acceleration, while gravity and drag may also contribute to its net force.

Tempting idea: Changing mass makes Newton's laws stop working.

Why it seems reasonable: Substituting a changing mass into familiar formulas can give contradictory answers.

What actually happens: The laws still apply; the selected system now exchanges matter and momentum.

Why: Track a fixed collection, or include the momentum carried across an open boundary.

A boundary must account for what crosses it

For a fixed collection of particles, external force equals the rate of change of their total momentum. This is the system form of Newton's second law.

For a changing collection, some momentum change occurs because particles enter or leave. It cannot all be attributed to external force on the current contents.

Over a short interval, select the rocket plus the exhaust it is about to eject. Compare this same matter before and after separation.

Internal rocket–gas forces cancel in the combined momentum account. The rocket's forward change balances the gas's change, with any external impulse included.

This is why simply writing external force = d(mv)/dt for the remaining rocket can fail. It omits momentum crossing the boundary.

Similarly, rain entering a freely rolling truck must gain the truck's horizontal motion. Contact forces accelerate the rain and slow the truck.

The incoming rain's horizontal momentum may be zero before contact. That does not mean no horizontal interaction occurs while it joins the truck.

For the belt, driving power is Fv = qv². The arriving sand's kinetic-energy gain per second is only ½qv².

The remainder becomes internal energy during slipping in this idealised loading process. Equal momentum accounting does not imply identical energy transfers.

A falling chain piling on a table similarly adds an impact force: arriving links must lose momentum. Exact forces depend on how the chain falls and stops.

Why the rocket equation contains a logarithm

In a short interval, eject a small mass δm at fixed backward speed u relative to the rocket. Let its current mass be m.

Ignoring external forces, momentum accounting gives an approximate forward gain δv ≈ u(δm/m). The fraction of the current mass expelled is what matters.

As m decreases, the same small expelled mass gives a larger speed gain. Add those small gains over the changing mass.

The sum becomes a function called the natural logarithm, written ln. It converts multiplied mass ratios into added speed gains.

Δv = u ln(minitial/mfinal)

The logarithm's input is a ratio, so it has no units. Multiplying by exhaust speed u gives a speed change.

Equal fractional mass reductions produce equal ideal speed gains. Two successive halvings multiply the total mass ratio by four and add two equal gains.

A mass ratio of 1 gives zero gain. Keeping more final mass at fixed initial mass reduces the gain. Increasing u improves it.

This is the ideal free-space relation with fixed relative exhaust speed. Gravity, drag and engine details alter a real flight's velocity gain.

You need not evaluate a logarithm to understand why expelled mass fraction, rather than propellant mass alone, controls the pattern.

Forces can turn objects

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Push a door near its hinge, then near its handle. The same sideways push turns it more effectively at the handle. Distance from the turning axis matters.

The relevant distance is the shortest, perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force’s line of action. We call it the lever arm.

turning effect = force × perpendicular lever arm

The turning effect is called moment of a force, or torque. Its unit N m records a force acting with a lever arm. It describes turning, not energy.

With force fixed, doubling the lever arm doubles torque. With the lever arm fixed, doubling force doubles torque. A force directed through the pivot has zero lever arm.

Choose clockwise as positive, or anticlockwise; either works if used consistently. Opposite torques can balance even when the individual forces have different sizes.

Where should you support an object?

Balance a ruler on a finger. Shift some mass towards one end and the balancing point shifts towards it. More mass there means more gravitational pull there.

The centre of mass is the average location of an object’s mass, weighted towards its heavier parts. In uniform gravity, its weight acts as if concentrated there.

A uniform ruler’s centre lies halfway along. An uneven object’s need not. The centre of mass can even lie in empty space, as for a ring.

For static balance of an upright object on a level base, without additional ties or adhesion, the vertical line through this centre must fall within the support area.

Beyond the edge, weight produces a tipping torque.

Spreading your feet widens the support area. You can lean farther before the line of your weight passes outside it.

Carrying a heavy bag changes the combined centre of mass, so you adjust your posture.

No overall acceleration needs balanced forces. No change in rotation also needs balanced torques. Two equal opposite forces at different places can form a turning pair.

Later, this will explain why a motor coil rotates.

A spinning skater can turn faster by pulling their arms inward when external turning effects are negligible. Their angular momentum, a measure of rotational motion, remains approximately constant.

The changed mass distribution makes the same rotational account correspond to a greater spin rate. Muscles do work, so rotational kinetic energy can increase.

Equal opposite forces need not cancel the turning

Push one side of a steering wheel forwards and the other backwards. Equal opposite forces can have zero resultant force yet produce a turning effect.

Their lines of action are separated. Each contributes torque in the same turning sense. Such a pair is called a couple.

With force magnitude F and perpendicular separation d between their lines, the couple's torque magnitude is Fd. Both forces must act on the chosen body.

Zero net force means no centre-of-mass acceleration. Zero net torque means no change of angular momentum. One condition does not replace the other.

For a force applied distance r from a pivot, only its component perpendicular to that radius creates turning about the pivot.

torque magnitude = rF sin θ = force × perpendicular lever arm

θ is the angle between the radius-to-application-point arrow and the force. The sine selects the perpendicular part; the lever-arm method avoids trigonometry altogether.

The compact cross product, written r × F, records this turning magnitude and the axis direction given by the right-hand convention.

Its geometric magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two arrows. Parallel arrows enclose zero area and give zero torque about that point.

Work instead uses alignment along displacement. Torque and work can share dimensions while describing different things; torque is written N m and work in joules.

For constant torque about a fixed axis, rotational work is τΔθ, with angle in radians. Force acts through an arc distance rΔθ, connecting the two descriptions.

What the more advanced vector geometry is doing

The dot product measures alignment. The cross product measures perpendicular spread and orientation. These two physical jobs explain why physics uses two different vector products.

Combine three displacement arrows to make a slanted box. Two arrows define the base; the perpendicular part of the third defines its height.

The scalar triple product is signed base area multiplied by height. It is zero when the arrows all lie in one plane, making a flat box.

A determinant is one efficient algebraic way to compute that volume from components. It is not an additional physical mechanism you must learn first.

A vector triple product combines perpendicular constructions twice. Later algebra rewrites it using projections onto two directions; it does not introduce a new kind of force.

For present mechanics, draw the alignment, lever arm, axis or box first. The formal identities become useful only when those geometric jobs become cumbersome.

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Yes. Acting at different places, their forces cancel but their torques can reinforce. The object choice and force locations matter.

One turning angle, many travelling distances

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Put two marks on a rotating disc, one near the centre and one near the rim. Both complete each turn together, but the outer mark travels farther.

One angle can describe the whole rigid disc's turning. “Rigid” means distances between its parts stay effectively fixed; real objects deform slightly.

For a mark at radius r, divide the arc distance s by r. This ratio measures the swept angle in radians.

angle θ = s/r, so s = rθ

One radian sweeps an arc as long as the radius. A full circumference is 2πr, so one complete turn is 2π radians.

Metres divide by metres, leaving a dimensionless angle. The name radian reminds us which angle measure belongs in s = rθ.

The rates of turning

Angular velocity ω, “omega”, describes angle change per second. All parts of a rigid disc share it about a fixed axis.

tangential speed v = rω

This follows by dividing s = rθ by the same time interval. At fixed ω, twice the radius means twice the speed.

Angular acceleration α, “alpha”, describes how quickly ω changes, in rad/s². For fixed r, tangential acceleration is rα.

Even if α is zero, a rim point still has inward acceleration rω² when it rotates. Unchanged turning rate does not mean unchanged velocity direction.

Choose a positive turning sense and keep it. Opposite signs of ω and α mean the rotation is slowing, just as in straight-line motion.

Familiar graphs, new axis labels

On an angle–time graph, slope gives angular velocity. On an angular-velocity–time graph, slope gives angular acceleration and signed area gives angle turned.

With constant α, the straight-line argument gives ω = ω₀ + αt and Δθ = ω₀t + ½αt². Here ω₀ is the initial angular velocity.

These are the old slope-and-area relationships applied to angle. They are invalid across an interval where α varies unless rebuilt from its actual graph.

An accumulated angle can exceed one turn. Ten full turns mean 20π radians of positive angular displacement, even if the disc looks similarly oriented afterwards.

Reversals require signed areas for net angle, and summed area sizes for total angle travelled. Net change and total journey remain different.

In space, angular velocity is represented by an axis arrow. Curl your right-hand fingers with the rotation; your thumb gives the conventional positive axis direction.

Finite rotations about different axes need more care. Turning a book first about one axis and then another can differ from reversing the order.

Ordinary vector addition is order-independent. Therefore a finite orientation change is not generally an ordinary vector, despite having an angle and an axis.

Belts, gears and shafts share different things

Two wheels linked by a non-slipping belt share the belt's tangential speed at contact. Thus r₁ω₁ = r₂ω₂ in magnitude.

The smaller wheel must turn faster to cover the same belt distance each second. The radii, not the wheel masses, set this kinematic ratio.

Two gears in direct external contact also share tangential contact speed, but rotate in opposite senses. Otherwise their teeth would interpenetrate or separate at contact.

Two wheels rigidly fixed to one shaft instead share angular velocity. Different rim speeds are then possible because their radii differ.

Ask which motion the connection physically enforces. “Connected” does not automatically mean equal speed of every kind.

Where the mass sits matters

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Imagine twisting a light rod with equal weights attached. Move the weights farther from the middle without changing their masses. The same twist changes its turning more slowly.

Mass alone misses the change. The relevant quantity must also describe distance from the chosen axis.

At angular speed ω, a small mass m at perpendicular distance r has speed rω. Its kinetic energy is therefore ½m(rω)².

Rearrange this as ½(mr²)ω². Each piece contributes its own mr². The whole rigid body shares the same ω, so those contributions can be added.

moment of inertia I = sum of each piece's mr²

rotational kinetic energy K = ½Iω²

“Moment of inertia” means the body's rotational inertia about that axis. Its unit kg m² records both mass and squared distance.

Double one piece's mass at fixed radius: double its contribution. Double its radius: quadruple the contribution. A point exactly on the axis contributes zero.

For a rigid body about a fixed axis, its net torque changes its angular velocity according to the rotational counterpart of F = ma.

net torque τ = Iα

The symbol τ, “tau”, means torque; α means angular acceleration. Units kg m²/s² equal N m. Use the torque component about the chosen axis.

At fixed I, twice the net torque gives twice the angular acceleration. At fixed torque, greater I means a slower change of rotation.

Zero torque does not require zero rotation. An ideal wheel can keep turning steadily, just as zero net force permits steady straight-line motion.

Reconstruct the axis rules instead of memorising a list

A thin hoop has all its mass at radius R, so I = MR² about its central perpendicular axis. M is total mass.

A uniform solid disc of the same mass and radius places some mass closer in. It therefore has smaller I; summing its thin rings gives ½MR².

Changing the axis changes the distances. A uniform door has less rotational inertia about a vertical line through its middle than about its hinge line.

Moving an axis parallel to itself by distance d from the centre-of-mass axis gives:

I = Icm + Md²

Why the extra term? Each shifted squared distance produces an old squared-distance term, a d² term and a cross-term.

The cross-terms cancel because mass-weighted position relative to the centre of mass sums to zero. The remaining added contribution is total mass multiplied by d².

This parallel-axis rule needs parallel axes and a centre-of-mass reference axis. It does not allow arbitrary jumps between two displaced axes.

For a thin flat sheet in the x–y plane, each piece lies at squared distance x² + y² from the perpendicular z axis.

Adding mr² gives Iz = Ix + Iy. This perpendicular-axis rule needs a flat sheet and three axes meeting at the same point.

A radius of gyration k simply replaces a distributed body by an equivalent ring: I = Mk². It summarises the distribution; it need not be a physical edge.

For a composite object, add contributions about the same axis. For a hole, subtract the missing contribution about that axis. No negative physical mass is implied.

Pulling the arms in: another conserved account

A skater spinning with arms extended pulls them inward. External torque may be small while the mass distribution changes appreciably.

For this simple rotation about a symmetry axis, angular momentum is L = Iω. Negligible external torque keeps L approximately constant.

Reducing I therefore increases ω. Its unit kg m²/s records rotational inertia multiplied by turning rate. Direction follows the rotation-axis convention.

The faster spinning has more rotational kinetic energy. Where did it come from? The skater's muscles did work while pulling the arms inward.

Angular momentum conservation is not a promise of constant kinetic energy. Internal work can change energy distribution without external torque.

The general law relates net external torque to angular-momentum change. L = Iω as a simple same-direction vector relation needs suitable axes; arbitrary tumbling is more complex.

A wheel both travels and turns

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Picture a wheel rolling along a stationary road without slipping. Its centre moves forwards, while its rim turns around that moving centre.

Add those two velocity descriptions at each point. This is the vector addition already used for motion inside a train.

At the bottom, rotation relative to the centre points backwards. It cancels the centre's forward velocity. The contact point is instantaneously at rest relative to the road.

At the top, the two velocities point forwards and add. The top point moves at twice the centre's speed.

no slipping on a stationary surface: vcm = Rω

R is wheel radius and ω is angular speed. This is a contact condition, not a rule for every rotating wheel. A skidding wheel can violate it.

The contact point changes as rolling continues. One piece of rubber is not fixed to the road throughout the journey.

Where does the energy go down a slope?

A freely rolling body descending a slope gains both translational kinetic energy and rotational kinetic energy.

K = ½Mvcm² + ½Icmω²

At a given downward height change, more energy going into rotation leaves less for the centre's speed. Mass distribution therefore affects a rolling race.

A hoop uses more rotational energy than a solid disc of equal mass and radius at the same rolling speed. Under comparable ideal conditions, the disc accelerates faster.

How friction can create turning without dissipating energy

Release a rigid disc on a rough slope from rest. Gravity acts through its centre, giving no torque about that centre in a uniform field.

Static friction points up the slope and supplies the torque that increases its rotation. It also reduces the centre's downward acceleration compared with frictionless sliding.

For ideal rolling on a stationary rigid surface, the instantaneous contact point has zero velocity. The static-friction force therefore does zero instantaneous work there.

This does not make friction unimportant. It enforces the relationship between rotation and translation while gravitational potential energy supplies their combined increase.

Real wheels deform. Deformation, material losses and air resistance can dissipate mechanical energy, so actual rolling resistance is not zero.

For a freely rolling object on a fixed slope at angle θ, define b = Icm/(MR²). This dimensionless ratio describes how far out the mass lies.

down-slope acceleration = g sin θ / (1 + b)

The numerator is the frictionless sliding acceleration. The extra factor 1 + b accounts for the need to increase rotation as well as translation.

This result assumes adequate static friction and no additional applied torque. It does not describe a powered wheel or one that slips.

Does rolling without slipping always require a non-zero friction force?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. An ideal rigid wheel already rolling steadily on a level surface can continue with zero horizontal force and zero friction.

Friction supplies a needed contact constraint when the situation demands it.

From concentrated force to floating

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Press a book against your palm, first flat and then edge-on, with the same force. The edge concentrates that force into a smaller area.

average pressure p = perpendicular force F / area A

Pressure is force per area. One pascal, Pa, means one newton per square metre. At fixed force, doubling area halves average pressure.

Pressure is not an extra force beside the contact force. Multiplying a uniform pressure by area reconstructs its perpendicular force on that area.

Why the amount of matter per space matters

Two equally sized blocks can have different masses. Mass density compares mass with the volume containing it: ρ = m/V.

The symbol ρ, “rho”, has units kg/m³. Double mass in the same volume and density doubles. Double both mass and volume and density stays unchanged.

Weight density is different: weight per volume, ρg, in N/m³. It changes with gravitational field strength even when mass density stays effectively unchanged.

A fluid's pressure increases downwards

In a stationary fluid under gravity, a lower layer must support the fluid above it. Greater depth therefore means greater pressure.

Pressure pushes on each surface perpendicularly. A submerged object's bottom generally receives a stronger upward pressure force than the downward force on its top.

The combined pressure force is buoyancy. It equals the weight of the displaced fluid in a uniform gravitational field under the usual hydrostatic conditions.

A floating object's buoyant force balances its weight. It adjusts how much volume is submerged until enough fluid is displaced.

Derive depth pressure and explain buoyancy

Imagine a vertical fluid column of area A and depth h. Its volume is Ah and its mass is ρAh.

Its weight is ρAhg. Bottom pressure must provide the extra upward force needed to support that weight beyond the pressure force on top.

pressure increase × A = ρAhg

pressure increase = ρgh

Area cancels because a wider column contains proportionally more weight and provides proportionally more supporting area. Pressure at a given depth does not depend on container width.

The units (kg/m³) × (m/s²) × m give N/m². This needs a stationary fluid with approximately constant density and g.

Total pressure is surface pressure plus ρgh. Depth zero means zero increase, not necessarily zero total pressure.

Replace an imagined volume of stationary fluid with an object of the same shape. The surrounding pressure forces were supporting the removed fluid's weight.

The same surrounding pressure pattern therefore gives the inserted object that upward force. This explains displaced-fluid weight without memorising a separate force rule.

For complete immersion, doubling displaced volume at fixed fluid density and g doubles buoyancy. Changing the object's mass alone does not change that buoyant force.

An object can nevertheless accelerate because its own weight changed. Compare the two forces; buoyancy alone does not decide whether it rises or sinks.

In a connected confined fluid, an added static pressure can be transmitted through the fluid. Different piston areas then experience forces pA of different sizes.

A larger piston moves less distance for the same displaced volume. Hydraulic force multiplication, like a pulley, trades distance for force while preserving the ideal work account.

Visible pressure from invisible momentum transfers

Imagine gas particles striking a container wall. Their velocity component towards the wall reverses on rebounding, so their momentum changes.

The wall exerts force on the particles; they exert force on the wall. Many collisions per second make a steady average force and pressure.

At fixed particle number and temperature, a smaller volume produces more frequent wall impacts per area. In the ideal-gas model, pressure varies inversely with volume.

At fixed volume and particle number, higher absolute temperature means greater average random translational kinetic energy. Faster particles transfer momentum more strongly and strike more frequently.

The combined pattern is pV proportional to particle number multiplied by kelvin temperature. Its standard microscopic form is:

pV = NkBT

Here N is particle count, T is absolute temperature in kelvin and kB is Boltzmann's constant, with units J/K. N here is not a support force.

Pressure times volume has units (N/m²) × m³ = N m = J. The right side has matching energy units.

Keep N and T fixed: doubling V halves p. Keep N and V fixed: doubling kelvin temperature doubles p. Doubling Celsius temperature does not mean the same thing.

This dilute-gas model treats particle size and attractions as negligible for the pressure account. It becomes less reliable near condensation or at high density.

Heat, temperature and internal energy

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Rub your hands together. Their organised motion slows while they warm. Mechanical work increases energy in microscopic motions and interactions.

That microscopic energy is internal energy. It differs from the kinetic energy of your whole hand moving across the room.

Temperature describes thermal state and determines the direction of spontaneous net energy transfer through thermal contact. It is not a count of all stored energy.

A bath of warm water can contain more internal energy than a tiny hotter droplet. Amount of material and particle arrangements matter as well as temperature.

Bring a warm spoon into cooler water. Energy transfers through their thermal interaction. Heat names this transfer caused by temperature difference, measured in joules.

Internal energy belongs to the system; heat describes energy crossing its boundary. Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or kelvin, not joules.

Microscopic motion and a macroscopic temperature

For an ideal gas, average random translational kinetic energy per particle is proportional to absolute temperature. Individual particles still have different speeds.

In a solid, particles vibrate around typical positions. In liquids and solids, interactions and arrangements contribute importantly to internal energy.

At equal temperature, there is no net heat flow between bodies in thermal contact. Microscopic exchanges can continue in both directions while balancing on average.

Tempting idea: Metal feels colder, so it must have a lower temperature than nearby wood.

Why it seems reasonable: Your hand is sensitive to cooling and feels a clear difference.

What actually happens: Both can have the same room temperature while metal removes energy from your hand faster.

Why: The sensation depends on transfer rate as well as temperature.

Why a temperature ratio needs kelvin

The zero of the Celsius scale is tied to a water-related reference, not to zero average thermal energy in a classical gas model.

Shifting that reference changes a temperature ratio. A “twice as hot” statement based on Celsius numbers therefore cannot specify twice the ideal-gas particle kinetic energy.

Kelvin is the absolute scale used for that relationship: T in kelvin equals the Celsius reading plus 273.15.

A one-kelvin change is the same size as a one-degree-Celsius change. This is why mcΔT works with either change unit, but p proportional to T needs kelvin.

Absolute zero is the lower limit of thermodynamic temperature for ordinary equilibrium systems of this kind. It does not mean every form of microscopic motion vanishes.

Quantum physics permits zero-point motion. The simple “temperature tracks particle motion” picture is useful, but it must not become “temperature measures all energy”.

Three routes for thermal energy

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Put one end of a metal spoon in hot soup. The handle warms without soup travelling through the spoon. Energy can move through material without bulk flow.

Conduction passes energy through interactions among neighbouring particles. In metals, mobile electrons also carry energy through the material. The particles themselves are never completely motionless.

With other conditions fixed, a larger temperature difference usually gives faster conduction. A thicker layer slows transfer; a larger area gives more paths for it.

Air conducts poorly. Trapping it in small pockets restricts circulation too, helping insulation work. Conductivity describes transfer; it differs from a material’s capacity to store energy.

A fluid can carry its internal energy with it

Heat water from below. Over ordinary temperatures, warming makes it expand, so the same mass occupies more volume: its density decreases.

Denser surrounding water produces a greater upward buoyant force than the warmed region’s weight. The warmed water rises; cooler water moves into its place.

This circulation transfers energy by convection. It connects microscopic expansion to density, pressure, buoyancy and visible motion. The same force ideas explain the thermal effect.

“Heat rises” is a misleading shortcut. Buoyant warm fluid may rise under gravity. Conduction and radiation can transfer energy in any direction. Pumps can also force fluid circulation.

Radiation crosses empty space

Sunlight reaches Earth across space. Thermal radiation carries energy as electromagnetic waves; it needs no material medium. You will meet these waves again in optics.

All ordinary objects emit and absorb radiation. Hotter objects generally emit more intensely. Net exchange with surroundings depends on both temperatures and surface properties.

Dark, matt surfaces often absorb and emit thermal radiation effectively. Polished metallic surfaces often reflect it. Visible colour alone does not determine every infrared property.

The vacuum gap greatly reduces conduction and prevents convection across the gap. Reflective walls reduce radiative transfer. A stopper reduces transfer through the opening.

The contents still cool eventually because the insulation is not perfect.

Put an ice cube above a warm object without contact. Can the object lose energy to it? Yes, through net radiation. No rule requires thermal energy to travel upwards.

Insulation changes the rate of transfer; it is not itself an energy source. A thick coat slows heat loss from a warm body already producing thermal energy.

A temperature difference spread across a distance

Compare two slabs of the same material and area, held between the same hot and cold temperatures. A thicker slab spreads that difference over more distance.

The local temperature change per metre is gentler. In steady one-dimensional conduction, that smaller temperature gradient gives a lower transfer rate.

conduction power = kA × temperature difference / thickness

Here k is thermal conductivity, A is cross-sectional area and thickness is the distance across the slab. This k is unrelated to spring stiffness.

The units of k are W/(m K). Multiply by area in m² and temperature gradient in K/m to obtain watts.

Double area at fixed thickness and temperatures: double the power. Double thickness while keeping those other conditions fixed: halve it.

This assumes a uniform material, steady temperatures and suitable constant conductivity. It does not describe every transient warming process with one fixed number.

The structure resembles an electric potential gradient producing an electric field. A gradient describes spatial change; the physical response law must still be established separately.

Why smaller pieces exchange energy faster per kilogram

Imagine scaling a solid cube so each edge doubles. Each face area becomes four times larger, while its volume and mass become eight times larger at unchanged density.

Surface grows with length squared; volume with length cubed. The larger cube has less surface per kilogram through which to exchange energy.

Cutting one cube into eight equal smaller cubes preserves its total mass and volume. Separating the pieces exposes new surfaces.

At comparable temperature differences and surface-transfer conditions, more exposed area permits faster energy transfer. The energy needed for the same temperature change still depends on mass.

This helps explain why small food pieces cool differently from one large piece. Interior conduction, shape and airflow can also matter, so area alone is not everything.

The same area-to-volume comparison appears in evaporation and diffusion. The geometry repeats; each physical process supplies its own transfer mechanism.

Warming is different from melting

Part I · Motion, matter and energy

Equal masses of water and metal receiving equal energy can warm by different amounts. We need to compare the energy required per kilogram per degree.

That comparison is specific heat capacity, c. Its unit is J/(kg K), equivalently J/(kg °C) for a temperature change.

energy transferred Q = mcΔT

m is mass and ΔT is temperature change. Multiplication rebuilds the total from “energy for each kilogram and each degree”.

This model assumes no change of state and approximately constant c. For fixed energy and mass, a larger c means a smaller temperature rise.

On the graph, right means more energy supplied and up means higher temperature. For the same mass, a gentler rising slope means larger heat capacity.

The flat part asks a different question

During melting at fixed pressure, solid and liquid can coexist while supplied energy changes the particles' arrangements and interactions. Temperature can remain steady.

Vaporisation similarly requires energy to separate particles against attractions. Boiling water does not normally split its molecules into hydrogen and oxygen.

Specific latent heat, L, is energy needed per kilogram for a specified phase change under specified conditions, in J/kg.

energy for the phase change Q = mL

Double the mass undergoing the same change: double the energy. The corresponding reverse change releases energy into the surroundings.

If we count energy into a chosen system as positive, cooling or freezing can involve negative Q. The quoted positive L is the magnitude per kilogram.

Why evaporation and boiling are different

At a liquid surface, some molecules can escape even below the boiling temperature. Their energy and interactions determine whether they leave rather than return.

When evaporation removes energy faster than other transfers replace it, the remaining liquid cools. Evaporation is not restricted to the highest-energy molecules alone.

Boiling requires vapour bubbles to grow within the liquid. Their vapour pressure must be sufficient against surrounding pressure, with small-bubble surface effects also relevant.

Lower external pressure lets water boil at a lower temperature. A boiling temperature therefore needs its pressure condition; it is not an unconditional property of a substance.

In a closed container, evaporation and condensation can reach equal rates. The macroscopic amounts then stay steady even though molecules continue crossing the boundary.

This is another dynamic equilibrium: equal opposing rates, not stopped microscopic activity. The same reasoning separated force balance from absence of interactions.

Why returning motion overshoots

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Attach a mass to a horizontal spring and pull it slightly from equilibrium. On release, the spring pulls it back, giving it velocity towards the middle.

At the middle the spring force becomes zero, but the mass already has velocity. It crosses the middle; the spring then pulls it backwards.

Restoring force brings it towards equilibrium. Inertia carries it through equilibrium. Together they produce repeated back-and-forth motion: an oscillation.

At an extreme, velocity is zero but restoring force is largest. At the middle, speed is largest but the spring's restoring force is zero.

For an ideal spring, choose displacement x from equilibrium as positive to the right. The restoring force is F = −kx.

The minus sign points the force back towards equilibrium. Combining this with F = ma gives:

acceleration a = −(k/m)x

k is spring stiffness in N/m; m is mass. The coefficient k/m has units 1/s², so multiplying by x gives m/s².

At fixed m and x, a stiffer spring returns the mass more strongly. At fixed spring and displacement, greater mass responds with smaller acceleration.

This acceleration proportional to the opposite displacement defines simple harmonic motion. It is a model for small, nearly linear restoring responses.

Real oscillations lose energy through damping. The amplitude, the largest departure from equilibrium, then decreases unless a source supplies replacement energy.

What sets the time for one cycle?

Doubling an ideal spring's amplitude gives twice the travel distance, but also twice the restoring acceleration at corresponding positions.

The motion scales in size while keeping the same cycle time. That is a special property of the linear restoring law, not of every oscillator.

The time scale must grow with inertia and shrink with stiffness. The combination √(m/k) has units seconds; it sets the natural time scale.

ideal spring period T = 2π√(m/k)

T is the time for one complete cycle. The factor 2π comes from the full-cycle geometry of sinusoidal motion; dimensional reasoning alone cannot supply it.

One way to see that geometry is to track the horizontal shadow of a point moving uniformly around a circle. Its shadow oscillates between two extremes.

The shadow's acceleration points back towards the middle and is proportional to its displacement. This connects circular motion to the simple-harmonic model.

A small-angle pendulum has T ≈ 2π√(L/g), where L is its pivot-to-bob length. Its mass cancels because weight and inertia increase together.

At large swing angles, the restoring geometry is no longer linear enough for that period approximation. State the small-angle assumption before using it.

Small vibrations near a stable interaction-energy minimum also have nearly linear restoring forces. This links springs to elastic solids and molecular vibrations.

A spring's mass passes through equilibrium. Is this where it stops because the net restoring force is zero?

An oscillation passed from place to place

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Displace a hanging mass on a spring and release it. The spring pulls it back. At the middle, its velocity carries it onwards before the spring slows it.

A restoring force and inertia together produce repeated motion, called an oscillation. Without damping, energy moves between kinetic and stored forms. Damping transfers energy to the surroundings.

Now connect many neighbouring particles. Disturb one: its interaction with the next passes the disturbance along. A wave is a travelling pattern of disturbance.

Each point of a rope moves mainly up and down while the wave travels sideways. The movement of the pattern differs from the movement of the material.

In this simple model, energy travels along the rope without the rope travelling along with it. Real waves may also produce secondary flows; the basic distinction remains useful.

Two directions to compare

In a transverse wave, disturbance is perpendicular to travel: the rope moves up and down while the pattern moves horizontally.

In a longitudinal wave, disturbance is parallel to travel. Push and pull one end of a spring: crowded and spread-out regions travel along it.

Sound in air is longitudinal. Air particles oscillate backwards and forwards as compression patterns travel. Water surface waves are more complicated: particles generally move in loops.

Does every wave need a substance?

Rope and sound waves need a medium, the material carrying the disturbance. Its inertia and restoring interactions together determine how quickly the disturbance propagates.

Electromagnetic waves, including light, can travel through a vacuum. Their changing electric and magnetic fields carry energy; no material chain of particles is required.

“If the wave reaches the far end, the original particles must reach it.” Watching a moving crest encourages this. Mark one rope point: it moves locally as the crest passes.

Interactions pass the disturbance on; the marked material does not travel with the crest.

For one oscillator, force restores motion towards equilibrium while inertia carries it through. Couple neighbouring oscillators and the disturbance can spread.

Waves grow naturally from the force and energy ideas already developed.

Earthquakes produce several wave types. Compressional P waves can travel through solids and liquids; shear S waves need a medium that supports sideways restoring forces.

A liquid does not support a static shear distortion like a solid. This difference helps explain why ordinary S waves do not pass through Earth’s liquid outer core.

Two ropes have different restoring interactions but the same mass per length. Must a disturbance travel at the same speed?

Wavelength, period and frequency

Photograph a repeating rope wave. The distance from one crest to the next is a spacing in space. Watching one marked point gives a repeating pattern in time.

Wavelength, λ (lambda), is distance between successive matching points, in metres. Period, T, is the time for one complete cycle, in seconds.

Frequency, f, counts cycles per second. Its unit hertz, Hz, means one cycle per second. Taking twice as long per cycle means half as many cycles per second.

f = 1/T

Amplitude, A, is the largest displacement from equilibrium. For a rope, it is measured in metres. Peak-to-trough height is twice the amplitude.

The snapshot panel compares positions at one instant. Moving right means farther along the rope. The time-trace panel follows one point; moving right means later time.

Their similar shapes can hide their different meanings. A horizontal gap between crests gives λ in the first and T in the second.

Build the speed relationship

During one cycle, the repeating pattern advances one wavelength. Distance travelled by the pattern divided by time gives its speed:

wave speed v = λ/T = fλ

Read fλ as “cycles each second × metres in each cycle”. The result is metres per second. The v here is pattern speed, not particle speed.

For a given rope tension and mass per length, small waves travel at approximately fixed speed. Driving twice as frequently then produces half the wavelength.

Do not hold v, f and λ independently fixed. If f doubles while v remains fixed, λ must halve. The equation constrains how the quantities fit together.

A larger amplitude means a larger disturbance, not necessarily a higher frequency or greater wave speed.

Within the small-wave model and unchanged medium, you can make taller waves without making their crests arrive more frequently.

What sets the speed?

Compressing matter produces a restoring response. Greater stiffness tends to pass a disturbance on more rapidly; greater inertia tends to resist rapid changes.

The balance of these properties determines sound speed. “Particles are closer together” is not a complete explanation. Sound often travels faster in solids, but density alone cannot predict it.

For small sound waves in unchanged air, making the source louder or raising its pitch does not significantly change travel speed. Frequency changes wavelength instead.

A sound pulse travels to a wall, reflects, and returns. If the wall is distance d away, the sound travels 2d. The measured delay covers both journeys.

distance to reflector d = vt/2

Here v is sound speed in the medium and t the round-trip time. Metres per second multiplied by seconds gives metres; the two divides out the return journey.

With sound speed fixed, twice the delay means twice the distance. If the reflector is very close, the return may blend into the original sound instead of sounding separate.

The method needs the relevant sound speed; the speed in air cannot simply be reused in water.

No steady wind from speaker to ear is needed.

A tuning fork vibrates rapidly, producing repeating pressure changes. The number of cycles per second largely determines pitch: higher frequency is usually heard as a higher note.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection

Rough surfaces also reflect, but their differently tilted patches send rays in different directions. A smooth mirror preserves an orderly pattern of reflected directions.

Your eye interprets incoming light as travelling straight. Extend reflected rays backwards on paper: they seem to meet behind a plane mirror.

That apparent source is a virtual image. Light does not actually meet behind the mirror. The image is the same size and distance behind as the object is in front.

A convex mirror, bulging outward, spreads rays. They appear to come from a smaller upright virtual image behind the mirror, giving a wider field of view.

A real image forms where rays actually meet and can be caught on a screen. A virtual image forms where their backward extensions meet.

“Virtual” means apparent ray origin; it does not mean the image cannot be seen.

Refraction and lenses

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

A wavefront enters glass at an angle. The part entering first slows first, turning the front. Light’s direction bends towards the normal. This is refraction.

Refractive index, n, compares vacuum speed c with light speed v in the material:

n = c/v

The speeds’ units cancel. Greater n means slower light in that material. This is an optical property, not simply mass density. Frequency stays unchanged at a stationary boundary.

Entering a higher-index medium obliquely bends light towards the normal; entering a lower-index medium bends it away. At normal incidence, speed changes without a direction change.

Since v = fλ, lower speed at unchanged frequency means shorter wavelength. Refraction therefore connects a ray’s direction with a wave’s spacing.

Two surfaces can work together

A glass convex lens in air is thicker in the middle. Refraction at its surfaces bends parallel incoming rays towards a focus: it is converging.

A concave lens is thinner in the middle and spreads incoming parallel rays: it is diverging. Their backward extensions meet at a virtual focus.

Focal length is the distance from a thin lens’s centre to its focus. Shorter focal length means stronger convergence or divergence for parallel incident rays.

A converging lens can make a real inverted image on a camera sensor or projector screen.

Place an object within its focal length and it acts as a magnifier, producing an upright virtual image.

The eye’s cornea and lens together focus light on the retina. Changing the lens’s shape adjusts focus as object distance changes.

In a short-sighted eye, distant light focuses in front of the retina; a diverging lens reduces convergence.

In a long-sighted eye, the available convergence is insufficient in relevant viewing conditions; a converging lens helps focus onto the retina.

Light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it exits water. Your eye traces those rays backwards in straight lines, locating an apparent source nearer the surface.

The water did not move the bottom; it changed the ray paths.

The simple lens diagrams use thin-lens and near-axis approximations. Real lenses can blur images through aberrations; their detailed shape and material also matter.

A ray enters water along the normal. Can its wavelength change while its direction remains unchanged?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Yes. At a stationary boundary frequency stays fixed while speed and wavelength change. Symmetrical, perpendicular entry gives no sideways turning.

White light can contain many visible wavelengths. A prism separates them because its refractive index depends on wavelength. This separation is dispersion.

For ordinary glass in visible light, violet bends more than red. We see a spread of colours because their outgoing directions differ.

A primary rainbow combines refraction, one internal reflection, and refraction again inside raindrops. Different colours reach your eye from different directions: red forms the outer edge.

A secondary rainbow involves two internal reflections and reverses the colour order. The drops redirect and separate existing sunlight; they do not manufacture the colours.

An object’s colour needs a light source

A red surface reflects some red wavelengths more strongly than others. Under pure green illumination, an ideal red-only reflector appears dark because no red light arrives to reflect.

Real surfaces reflect ranges of wavelengths, so actual colours depend on the material, illumination and observer. Absorbed light transfers energy to the material.

Air molecules scatter shorter visible wavelengths more strongly. This helps make the sky blue. A low Sun’s direct light crosses more atmosphere, losing more short-wavelength light from that path.

When the outward ray disappears

Light travelling from higher to lower refractive index bends away from the normal. Increase the incident angle: eventually the refracted ray runs along the boundary.

That incident angle is the critical angle. Beyond it, an ideal transparent boundary returns the light into the original medium: total internal reflection.

Both conditions matter: travel towards lower refractive index, and incidence beyond the critical angle, measured from the normal. A steep angle measured from the surface can be misleading.

A fibre’s core has higher refractive index than its surrounding cladding. Suitable rays repeatedly undergo total internal reflection, carrying signals along it.

Real fibres still have losses; “total” describes reflection at the ideal boundary, not perfect transmission through any length.

A converging mirror concentrates sunlight: similar power falls on less area, so intensity increases. The mirror redirects energy; it creates none. Pressure uses similar “per area” reasoning.

A fibre has the correct refractive indices. Is that alone enough to guarantee total internal reflection for every entering ray?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. Each relevant incidence angle must also exceed the critical angle. The index ordering is necessary but not sufficient.

Shine red and green light onto the same white surface. Light of both kinds reaches your eyes together, and the overlap can appear yellow.

This is additive colour mixing: combine arriving light. A screen uses red, green and blue subpixels in varying intensities to produce many colour sensations.

Three suitable primaries can produce the sensation of white without containing every visible wavelength. The eye's responses do not uniquely identify a complete spectrum.

Matter selects the light it exchanges

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

White light passing through a prism spreads into colours. Light from a particular excited gas can instead contain narrow bright lines at selected wavelengths.

The lines are evidence that atoms do not accept every possible electron-energy change. Their electrons can occupy particular allowed energy states.

An atom can absorb a suitable portion of light energy and reach a higher state. It can later emit light while moving to a lower state.

A photon (one quantum, or portion, of light energy) carries energy related to frequency. Higher frequency means greater energy per photon.

photon energy = h × frequency

The constant h sets the size of the relation; its unit J s converts cycles per second into joules. The relationship is experimental physics, not ordinary geometry.

The energy difference between allowed states selects the emitted or absorbed frequency. Different atomic structures therefore produce different spectral patterns.

Increasing brightness can mean more photons arriving each second. It need not mean each photon has higher frequency. Intensity and colour remain different quantities.

Molecules also exchange energy through vibrations and rotations. Many infrared absorption bands involve changes in molecular vibrations rather than visible electronic transitions.

This is why light can reveal chemical composition. Spectroscopy (identifying matter through the light it absorbs or emits) connects waves, energy and electron structure.

An atom can absorb or emit visible light even though its size is much smaller than visible wavelengths. Detection and resolving shape are different tasks.

An ordinary light microscope cannot resolve an atom's internal structure. Shorter-wavelength probes or surface-sensitive techniques are needed for atomic-scale detail.

Do not picture an electron circling like a planet to generate each colour. A shell diagram summarises allowed arrangements; the deeper model is quantum physics.

A lamp becomes brighter without changing its colour. Has every photon become more energetic? What measurement would distinguish the possibilities?

Charge, interaction and energy per charge

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Rub a balloon against hair. Electrons may transfer between them. An object gaining electrons becomes negatively charged; losing them leaves a positive imbalance.

Charge is measured in coulombs, C. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. An overall neutral object contains positive and negative charges whose totals balance.

Charge is conserved: transfer changes where it is, not the total of an isolated system. In this example, protons remain bound inside atomic nuclei.

The balloon can attract a neutral wall because charges in the wall shift slightly. Opposite charge is drawn nearer; the nearer attraction can exceed the more distant repulsion.

A field separates source from test object

Earth produces a gravitational field; a mass placed there feels a force. Electric charges similarly produce an electric field; another charge responds to it.

electric field strength E = force on a positive test charge / its charge

Its unit N/C means force per coulomb. Field direction is the force direction on a positive charge. A negative charge experiences the opposite force direction.

The test charge must be small enough not to change the source arrangement appreciably. At fixed field, doubling the test charge doubles force. Field and force are not interchangeable.

Voltage is an energy comparison

Moving charge between two places can change electric potential energy. Potential difference, or voltage, is that energy change per charge, measured in J/C, called volts.

potential difference = change in electric potential energy / charge

One volt means one joule per coulomb. Double the transferred charge at the same voltage difference: double the associated energy, not the voltage.

Like gravitational height, electric potential needs a reference; potential differences are what matter for transfers. Electric potential energy belongs to the interacting charge arrangement.

Charge: how much electric quantity? Field: what force would each positive coulomb experience here? Potential difference: how much energy changes per coulomb between these places?

A battery uses chemical processes to maintain a potential difference between its terminals.

Humid conditions often let surface charge leak away more readily through adsorbed moisture. Static charging depends on material and surroundings, not just how vigorously you rub.

A static electric field points towards decreasing electric potential. A steeper potential change across distance means a stronger field in that direction.

This is another gradient: how much a quantity changes from place to place. In a uniform field along a line, field size equals voltage change divided by distance.

The units V/m equal N/C. A volt is a joule per coulomb, and a joule is a newton metre, so the two field descriptions agree.

Force per portion and energy per portion

Compare two masses at the same place near Earth. Each kilogram experiences approximately g newtons downwards. Gravitational field separates the place's influence from the test mass.

Electric field does a similar job per coulomb: F = qE. Here q is signed charge and E is the local electric-field vector.

A negative q reverses the force direction. Doubling q at fixed source arrangement doubles force without doubling the field itself.

Now compare two positions. Electric potential V is potential energy per test charge: U = qV, with the chosen reference understood.

In a static field, a positive charge moving towards lower V loses electric potential energy. A negative charge can lose energy by moving towards higher V.

Over a small displacement Δx, potential changes by ΔV. The conservative force relation gives Eₓ ≈ −ΔV/Δx, approaching −dV/dx locally.

Its units V/m equal N/C because one joule is one newton metre. The mathematics connects an energy-per-charge description to a force-per-charge description.

Zero potential at a point need not mean zero field. A zero-height point on a graph can still have a slope. Zero gradient is what removes this field component.

This electrostatic potential picture assumes a static electric situation. Changing magnetic fields can produce circulating electric fields requiring a more general account.

No. The same electric field produces opposite force directions on opposite charges. Field direction is defined using a positive test charge.

Current, voltage and resistance

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Choose a cross-section of a wire and count the charge passing it. More charge in the same time means a larger current.

average current I = charge Q / time t

One ampere, A, means one coulomb per second. Twice the charge in the same time gives twice the average current. The same charge taking twice as long gives half.

In a metal, mobile electrons drift through the material. Conventional current points in the direction positive charge would move; electron drift is opposite.

Closing a circuit establishes an electric field through it. Electrons already throughout the wire respond; a lamp need not await electrons travelling all the way from the battery.

Voltage and current answer different questions

The electrical disturbance spreads through the circuit at finite speed. Its propagation is much faster than the slow average drift of the electrons.

Voltage V measures energy transferred per charge across a component. Current I measures charge passing per second.

A battery can have a terminal voltage while an open circuit has zero current.

In a resistor, electrons interact with the material, transferring energy to it. This impedes drift. Resistance compares the voltage across a component with its current.

resistance R = V/I; equivalently V = IR

The unit ohm, Ω, means volt per ampere. Larger R means less current for the same applied voltage. At fixed voltage, doubling resistance halves current.

Ohm’s law makes an additional physical claim: for an ohmic conductor under fixed conditions, especially temperature, current is proportional to voltage, so R stays constant.

On this I–V graph, voltage increases rightwards and current upwards. A straight line through the origin shows that proportionality. Its slope is 1/R; a steeper line means lower resistance.

“Every component has one fixed resistance.” A simple resistor encourages this model. But a hot filament changes resistance, and a diode or LED has a strongly non-proportional I–V relationship.

One measured V/I ratio does not predict its behaviour at every voltage.

A circulating flow helps picture charge per second; a pump helps picture energy supplied. But voltage is energy per charge, not literal pressure, and electrons are not consumed like fuel.

Keep the quantities distinct.

Longer paths and more parallel pathways

Compare two uniform wires of the same material, cross-section and temperature. The longer one contains more successive material through which charge must drift.

It therefore has greater resistance. Double its length while holding material, temperature and cross-section fixed: the simple model doubles resistance.

Make the wire thicker instead. Its larger cross-sectional area offers more parallel routes for charge transport, reducing resistance at fixed length and material.

resistance R = resistivity ρ × length L / area A

Resistivity ρ describes the material under stated conditions. Its unit Ω m makes ρL/A an ohm. It is a different quantity from mass density, despite sharing a symbol.

Double the wire's radius and its area becomes four times larger. At fixed length and resistivity, resistance becomes one-quarter, not one-half.

Mobile electrons carry charge through a metal while ion cores remain near their sites. In a liquid electrolyte, moving positive and negative ions can both carry current.

Solid ionic material may contain charges yet conduct poorly because those ions cannot move freely. Charge presence and charge mobility are different physical requirements.

This is where material structure matters to circuit physics. A separate chemistry chapter is unnecessary to understand why different charge carriers change conductivity.

Could adding more dissolved particles to water leave conductivity almost unchanged? What property of the added particles matters?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Yes, if they are mainly neutral molecules that do not supply many mobile ions. Particle count alone is not a charge-transport model.

Circuits conserve charge and energy

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Join two resistors end-to-end with a battery. In a steady state, charge cannot continually pile up between them. The same current must pass through both.

This is a series circuit: one path. Across each resistor, electrical energy is transferred. Around the loop, total energy supplied per charge matches total energy transferred per charge.

series: I is the same; Vtotal = V₁ + V₂

For resistors, substitute V = IR. Since the same I multiplies both resistances, Rtotal = R₁ + R₂. More series resistance gives less current at fixed supply voltage.

Parallel paths share endpoints

Now connect each resistor across the same two battery terminals. Both span the same potential difference. This is a parallel circuit.

In steady operation, charge entering a junction each second equals charge leaving. Therefore:

parallel: V is the same; Itotal = I₁ + I₂

At fixed terminal voltage, lower-resistance branches carry more current. Adding a branch increases total current, lowering equivalent resistance.

Existing branches retain their voltage if the supply maintains it. Real batteries have internal resistance, so heavy loading can reduce terminal voltage and dim lamps.

The battery supplies energy, not fresh charge

Charge circulates around the complete conducting loop. A component receives energy while passing charge onwards. Current is not gradually “used up” through successive components.

“The first lamp gets more current because it is nearer the battery.” This resembles consuming fuel along a route. But sustained unequal currents would accumulate charge between lamps.

In a steady series circuit, current is the same throughout; energy per charge is shared between the components.

Household appliances use parallel branches so each can receive the supply voltage and be switched independently. An open break in one branch need not stop the others.

A short circuit is different: a very low-resistance path can draw excessive current and activate protection.

The current rule at a junction expresses conservation of charge. The voltage rule around a loop expresses conservation of energy. Circuit rules grow from ideas already used in mechanics.

Different charge carriers can complete different parts of a circuit. A cell’s electrolyte carries ions while its external metal wire carries electrons; sustained charge flow still balances.

A battery changes energy arrangements, not the amount of charge

Inside a working cell, coupled reactions at its electrodes transfer electrons into and out of the external circuit. Ions move internally to complete the charge transport.

The complete chemical arrangement changes to one of lower available energy during discharge. Some of that decrease supplies electrical energy; some becomes internal energy.

This is the chemical connection needed for the physics: atoms and electrons rearrange while charge continues to be conserved.

The emf, symbol ε, is the source's supplied energy per unit charge, measured in volts. Despite its historical name, “electromotive force”, it is not a force in newtons.

A simple cell model also includes internal resistance r. When it supplies current I, an internal voltage drop Ir leaves terminal voltage V = ε − Ir.

At zero external current, the model has almost no internal drop. Drawing more current at fixed ε and r lowers the terminal voltage.

Real cells are more complicated and their conditions change during use. The simple model explains why an ideal fixed-voltage assumption can fail under heavy load.

For parallel resistors across fixed voltage V, total current is V/R₁ + V/R₂. Dividing by V gives 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

The reciprocal appears because parallel branches add current per volt, the ease of conduction, rather than adding resistance along one path.

One parallel branch opens. Under which supply assumption do the other branches keep their previous currents?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Their currents remain if the supply maintains the same terminal voltage and their component conditions remain fixed. Real internal resistance can alter that outcome.

Electrical power: two “per” quantities multiply

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

A battery transfers V joules of energy per coulomb. A current of I amperes carries I coulombs per second. Multiply the two rates of comparison.

power P = IV

The units explain why: (C/s) × (J/C) = J/s. The coulombs cancel, leaving watts. Voltage says energy per charge; current says charge per time.

At fixed current, doubling voltage doubles power. At fixed voltage, doubling current doubles power. For constant power, energy transferred in time t is E = Pt.

An electricity meter totals energy, not instantaneous power. A kilowatt-hour, kWh, is the energy transferred by 1 kW for one hour; it equals 3.6 million joules.

Two forms that need their conditions stated

For a resistor obeying V = IR, replace V in P = IV. This gives P = I²R. At fixed R, twice the current gives four times the heating power.

Alternatively use I = V/R, giving P = V²/R. At fixed V, twice the resistance halves the power. These are compatible statements under different fixed conditions.

Microscopic interactions turn electrical energy into internal energy in the resistor. A heater exploits this; supply wires generally should minimise this unwanted transfer.

Why transmit at high voltage?

To deliver the same power, increasing voltage allows smaller current because P = IV. Smaller current means less heating of the same wires, following I²R.

This comparison holds transmitted power and wire resistance fixed. It does not mean high voltage always makes every device produce less heat.

A bird touching only one wire has nearly the same electric potential at both feet, so little voltage is applied across its body.

Bridging different potentials, such as another wire or Earth, changes the situation. A wire’s potential alone does not specify current through an object.

A lamp passes the same charge onward that enters it in steady operation, yet it transfers energy to light and surroundings.

Which decreases around the resistor: charge flow rate or electric potential energy per charge? The latter; that is the voltage drop.

A resistor is replaced by one with twice the resistance. Why can the heating power increase in one experiment and decrease in another?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

At fixed current, I²R doubles. At fixed voltage, V²/R halves. The apparent contradiction comes from holding different quantities constant.

Imagine a fault connecting a live wire to an appliance's metal case. The case can acquire a voltage relative to surrounding grounded objects.

An earth connection provides a designed low-resistance fault path for appliances requiring it. The resulting large fault current can operate a fuse or circuit breaker.

A fuse melts when excessive current heats its thin element sufficiently. A circuit breaker opens a switch. Both interrupt the circuit rather than absorbing unlimited energy.

A residual-current device, RCD, compares outgoing current with returning current. An imbalance indicates that some current has taken another route; the device disconnects the supply.

These devices detect different problems. An ordinary overcurrent fuse is not a guarantee against every electric shock, and an RCD is not complete protection either.

Some appliances use double insulation (two protective barriers or an equivalent construction), so their safety design does not rely on an earth connection.

The lesson is about circuit behaviour, not home-wiring instructions. Use the guide's circuit models; never create faults, bypass protection or investigate live mains equipment.

An open path and a voltage are different

A battery can maintain a voltage across disconnected terminals with almost no external current. Closing a conducting path permits a current determined by the complete circuit.

An earth wire is not a universal drain for all charge. It is part of a return path in a particular protective arrangement.

Likewise, a bird's two nearby feet on one wire usually span very little voltage. Bridging different potentials creates a different current path.

Charge conservation still applies when a protection device opens. Charges do not vanish; the path supporting sustained current has changed.

Magnetic fields and electromagnets

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

A compass turns near a magnet. The small magnet in the compass responds differently at different places. A magnetic field describes that local influence.

Draw an arrow showing where the compass’s north-seeking end points. Repeat at nearby positions. Joining those directions produces a field-line diagram.

Lines are our representation, not material threads. Their direction shows field direction; closer spacing conventionally represents greater strength. Outside a bar magnet, lines run from north to south.

A bar magnet has two poles. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Cut an ordinary bar magnet and each piece has both a north and a south pole.

Magnetised materials contain many microscopic magnetic contributions aligned enough to reinforce. Cutting the material divides these aligned regions; it does not detach an isolated pole.

Current produces a magnetic field

A compass near a current-carrying wire turns. Around a straight wire, the magnetic field forms circles. Reverse the current and the field direction reverses.

Wind the wire into a coil. The turns’ fields reinforce inside, producing a bar-magnet-like pattern. Such a coil is called a solenoid.

An iron core can strengthen the field by aligning magnetic regions. More current or more turns per length generally strengthens a similar coil, until material limitations matter.

An electromagnet can lift suitable iron or steel objects when energised.

A doorbell uses a related cycle: current attracts an arm, contact breaks, a spring returns the arm, and contact closes again.

The circuit changes its own motion, which changes the circuit: feedback.

Earth’s field helps align a compass approximately north–south. The north-seeking end points towards the northern geographic region, which behaves magnetically like a south pole.

Geographic names and magnetic attraction must be kept distinct.

Electric fields act on charge. Magnetic fields act on moving charges and magnetic objects.

A compass maps field direction, but that arrow is not automatically the force direction on every moving charged particle. Different interactions need their own force rules.

An unmagnetised piece of iron can contain locally aligned magnetic regions pointing in different directions. A net magnetisation appears when their contributions reinforce sufficiently.

Why might cutting a magnet into smaller pieces reduce each piece’s overall effect without producing isolated north and south poles?

Each piece retains microscopic magnetic organisation and two-pole behaviour. Dividing the material changes its size and field pattern without extracting separate poles.

Motors and generators

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

A current-carrying wire placed suitably in a magnetic field experiences a force. The field acts on moving charges; their interaction with the wire transfers the force to it.

Force direction depends on both current and field directions. Reversing either reverses the force; a wire parallel to the field has no magnetic force from this effect.

A motor uses a turning pair

In opposite sides of a coil, currents run opposite ways. A suitably directed magnetic field gives opposite forces on those sides. Because they act at different places, they produce torque.

Remember the door: force at a distance from an axis can turn an object. The coil’s forces can have zero net push while their turning effects reinforce.

A simple DC motor reverses coil current each half-turn using a split-ring contact, a commutator, so the useful torque continues in the same rotational direction.

Electrical input supplies mechanical output and internal-energy increases. A magnetic field helps direct the interaction; it is not a source of unlimited motor energy.

A generator changes the magnetic situation

Move a magnet relative to a coil, or turn a coil in a magnetic field. A changing magnetic field through the coil can produce an induced voltage: electromagnetic induction.

A complete conducting path allows that voltage to drive current. An open coil can have voltage without a sustained current.

Mere motion is insufficient if the magnetic situation does not change appropriately.

Faster change or more coil turns generally gives a larger induced voltage. Reversing the change reverses its direction.

The induced current’s magnetic effect opposes the change producing it: Lenz’s law. A generator supplying a load resists the driving motion. Mechanical work supplies electrical energy, with losses.

If induction helped its own cause without an energy source, the energy account would fail.

In regenerative braking, a moving vehicle drives its motor as a generator. The vehicle’s kinetic energy supplies electrical energy, and the opposing torque slows it.

The motor need not rotate backwards; the direction of energy transfer changes.

A loudspeaker uses force on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field. Changing current moves the attached cone, producing the pressure waves studied in sound.

A changing current in a nearby stationary coil can also induce voltage. Physical movement is one way to change the magnetic situation, not an essential requirement.

Field arrows and force arrows describe different things

An electric-field arrow gives the force direction on a positive stationary test charge. A magnetic-field arrow does not serve that same job for a moving charge.

The magnetic part of the force is perpendicular to both its velocity and the magnetic field. It can turn the motion without directly changing the particle's speed.

For a charge q with speed v in field magnitude B, the force magnitude is |q|vB sin θ. Here θ is the velocity–field angle.

B is measured in tesla, T; 1 T = N/(A m). Parallel motion gives zero magnetic force; perpendicular motion gives the largest magnitude for fixed q, v and B.

Reversing the charge sign or either arrow reverses the force. This is another cross-product geometry, like the perpendicular direction used for torque.

In a motor, the complete circuit and its electric field supply energy. Magnetic interactions transmit forces to the wire and couple the electrical and mechanical motion.

For a straight wire segment of length L perpendicular to a uniform field, the force magnitude is BIL. L means the segment within the field.

Current I is charge per second. More current means more charge transport receiving and transmitting magnetic force, under these fixed geometric conditions.

Why a generator needs changing magnetic linkage

Hold a loop in a uniform magnetic field. How much of the field passes through its opening depends on field strength, loop area and orientation.

The quantity recording this linkage is magnetic flux. For a flat loop in a uniform field, Φ = BA cos θ.

Here A is area, B is field magnitude and θ is measured between the field and the perpendicular to the loop. Flux is measured in T m², called webers.

Turn the loop edge-on to the field: its projected area becomes zero. Face it squarely towards the field: the projected area is greatest.

A changing flux produces an induced emf. For N identical linked turns, its magnitude is N times the rate of flux change.

The more complete signed statement is ε = −N dΦ/dt. The minus sign expresses the opposition described by Lenz's law, using a consistent orientation convention.

The derivative is the familiar local-rate idea. Wb/s equals volts. Unchanging flux gives zero induced emf in this model, even if something is moving.

Changing B, area or orientation can change flux. Moving a closed rigid loop entirely through a spatially uniform steady field need not change it.

Induction therefore grows from change and geometry together. The energy account explains why supplying a load makes a generator harder to drive.

A generator’s circuit is opened while it keeps turning. Which can remain: induced voltage, substantial load current, or the same electrical output power?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Induced voltage can remain. Opening the path suppresses load current and output power, though friction and other losses can remain.

Transformers: changing voltage

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Place two coils around a shared iron core. A changing current in the first changes the magnetic field through both. The second coil receives an induced voltage.

The first is the primary; the second is the secondary. This arrangement is a transformer. The core guides a large part of the magnetic field through both coils.

If each turn links approximately the same changing magnetic field, each receives the same induced voltage per turn. More turns add more of these contributions.

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np

Here V means voltage and N number of turns; subscripts s and p mean secondary and primary. This is the ideal transformer relation for corresponding AC voltage measures.

Twice as many secondary turns gives twice the secondary voltage. Fewer secondary turns gives a smaller voltage. Turns are counts, so both ratios have no units.

Higher voltage does not mean extra energy

For an ideal transformer powering a resistor, input and output powers match. Use effective AC values — values giving the same heating as DC:

VpIp = VsIs

At the same transferred power, stepping voltage up steps current down. Real transformers also transfer some input energy into heating and other losses.

Alternating current, AC, continually changes direction and magnetic field. A steady direct current, DC, gives no sustained induction in the secondary after switching effects end.

Follow one connected energy story

A turbine turns a generator. A transformer raises voltage for lower-current transmission. Wires then waste less power through I²R heating. Other transformers reduce voltage near users.

Phone chargers add switching and control circuits. Induction cooktops use changing fields to drive currents that heat a pan; the pan can warm the cooktop.

Connect a motor to a generator and feed the generator’s output back. Could it keep producing surplus energy?

No: even ideal conversion gives no extra energy, and real losses leave less useful output than input. Following each transfer exposes the mistake.

Interactions change motion or energy; equations connect their sizes, directions and rates, while conservation keeps the complete account consistent.

Photovoltaic solar cells convert absorbed light through electronic processes; they do not require a spinning generator. Different devices can connect the same energy domains by different mechanisms.

High-voltage DC transmission is also possible with suitable conversion electronics. A conventional transformer’s need for changing flux does not mean DC can never transmit power.

An ideal transformer doubles voltage while supplying the same power. What must happen to current, and why would keeping it unchanged violate the account?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

Current halves. Doubling voltage at unchanged current would double power without a corresponding extra input, contrary to the stated ideal power balance.

Part II · Waves, fields and connections

Strong reasoning means knowing what a model predicts, what it leaves undecided and which observation could expose a wrong assumption.

For a new situation, start with a sketch. Name the objects, the reference frame, and the interval you are comparing.

Draw interaction arrows. Mark any contact, fixed-length or no-slipping condition. Ask whether those restrictions can actually be maintained.

Then choose an account: changes of momentum, changes of energy, or forces at an instant. Different accounts often answer different parts of the same question.

Test the result by reversing a direction, doubling one quantity, or setting it to zero. State what stays fixed in each comparison.

The following situations combine ideas across the course. Predict before opening the reasoning; no numerical calculation is needed.

Distinguish a prediction from a guess

A feather and a stone fall differently in air. One explanation says mass alone decides falling acceleration. Another says gravity and air resistance must both be included.

Changing the surrounding air tests the competing explanations. So does changing shape while keeping mass similar. A useful experiment alters what the explanations disagree about.

Measuring only more examples of ordinary drops may repeat the same hidden conditions. Critical reasoning looks for a discriminating change, not just more observations.

Similarly, finding one straight segment in a motion graph does not establish constant acceleration for the entire journey. Inspect the relevant interval and measurement uncertainty.

An equation fitting the units is a useful candidate, not a complete proof. Units cannot supply every numerical factor or establish the force law experimentally.

Agreement between force and energy reasoning is a stronger check when both use the same physical assumptions. Disagreement is a reason to inspect those assumptions and signs.

Follow a bicycle from microscopic stores to braking

Muscles use changes in chemical arrangements to supply mechanical work. That changes how the rider pushes the pedals; it is not an external forward force by itself.

Pedals and chain supply wheel torque. At the driven tyre, the road's static friction can supply forward force, changing the rider-and-bicycle system's momentum.

The wheels rotate while their centres translate. Some kinetic energy belongs to each description. The mass distribution of a wheel influences its rotational share.

At steady speed, driving effects balance resistance. Net acceleration is zero, but energy continues transferring into air and internal energy in the equipment and surroundings.

Braking transfers kinetic energy chiefly into internal energy. A higher starting speed increases that kinetic-energy account quadratically, under the same mass conditions.

Neither momentum nor energy alone answers every braking question. Stopping time, stopping distance, grip and maximum force require their corresponding accounts and constraints.

Follow energy into a cup of water

An electric heater transfers energy at rate IV. Charge continues through the circuit while electric potential energy per charge decreases across the heating element.

Microscopic interactions increase the element's internal energy. Conduction transfers energy to nearby water; convection redistributes warmed fluid through density and buoyancy differences.

The water's temperature rise depends on its mass and specific heat capacity. Once boiling occurs at the prevailing pressure, phase change requires an additional energy account.

Escaping vapour carries energy away. Radiation, conduction and moving air also transfer energy to the room. Electrical input need not all remain in the water.

The molecules rearrange and separate during boiling without normally changing their chemical identity. Particle thinking explains the thermal physics without turning this into a reaction lesson.

An energy transfer that passes through several descriptions

A source maintains a potential difference. Current in a loudspeaker coil experiences magnetic forces; changing current changes the force and moves the attached cone.

The cone alternately compresses and releases nearby air. Local pressure differences accelerate neighbouring air, transmitting a sound wave.

Frequency helps set pitch. Amplitude affects intensity. The room's reflections and the speaker's resonances affect what reaches a listener.

Electrical power is input energy per second; sound intensity is delivered power per area. They are related, but neither is the other without additional information.

Some input energy warms the coil and other parts. The complete energy account connects voltage, current, force, oscillation, pressure and thermal transfer.

No new energy appears when we switch from an electrical description to a mechanical or acoustic one. We changed the model's viewpoint, not the event.

Two frictionless tracks descend between the same starting and finishing heights. Will identical beads released from rest finish with the same speed? Must they arrive together?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

If each bead remains constrained to its stationary track and losses are negligible, equal height drops give equal final speeds.

Their arrival times need not match: speed along the whole route and route length determine time. Energy at the endpoints cannot supply every detail of the journey.

A book starts from rest on a belt already moving steadily. Friction points forwards. Does this violate the idea that friction dissipates mechanical energy?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. Friction does positive work on the book while the moving belt supplies energy. During slipping, some supplied energy also becomes internal energy. Account for book, belt and drive together.

One force's work on one object is not the complete energy account.

A turning car maintains constant speed. Its passenger says, “The forces balance because the speed stays constant.” What observation disproves that reasoning?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

The velocity direction changes. An inward net force is needed even though speed and kinetic energy stay constant. A speedometer alone cannot detect every form of acceleration.

Two graphs have identical-looking falling straight lines. One plots position against time; the other plots velocity against time. Do they describe the same motion?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. The first describes constant negative velocity. The second describes constant negative acceleration; speed may decrease or increase depending on the velocity's sign.

Graph shape acquires physical meaning only through its axes.

A lamp becomes dimmer after another resistor is added. Can this observation alone prove that the new resistor was connected in series?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. Series resistance can reduce current, but adding a parallel load to a real source can also lower terminal voltage through internal resistance.

The observation does not uniquely identify the circuit. Measure or specify the connections and source behaviour.

A spring–mass oscillator and an electrical device both show repeated changes. Does the shared graph shape prove that their microscopic mechanisms are the same?

Make a prediction, then open the reasoning

No. Similar mathematical structure can arise from different physical interactions. First identify what stores energy, what transfers it and what provides a restoring response in each system.

A mathematical analogy helps only where those roles genuinely match.

Scientific distinctions retained throughout

Weight persists in free fall. Rough contact can exert zero friction. A force resultant describes translation; its application point also matters for rotation.

Momentum conservation needs negligible net external impulse. Total energy conservation must not be confused with conservation of kinetic or mechanical energy.
