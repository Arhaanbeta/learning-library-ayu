Slide a book across a table and let go. It slows down and stops. Nobody pulled it back, so why did it stop? A force must have acted on it, and that force is **friction**.

<KeyIdea>
**Friction** is a contact force between two surfaces. It always acts **against** the way they slide (or are trying to slide) over each other.
</KeyIdea>

## Where friction comes from

Even surfaces that feel smooth are bumpy when you zoom in. Under a microscope, a polished table looks like a mountain range. When two surfaces touch, their tiny bumps catch on each other and resist sliding.

## What makes friction bigger or smaller

Two things matter most:

1. **The surfaces.** Rough surfaces, like sandpaper or a carpet, give more friction. Smooth ones, like ice or polished wood, give less.
2. **How hard the surfaces press together.** A heavy box is harder to slide than an empty one, because it presses down harder on the floor.

<Tip>
Getting something to **start** sliding usually takes more force than keeping it sliding. That is why a heavy box suddenly "gives" and then moves more easily.
</Tip>

## Friction with air and water

When something moves through air or water, the air or water rubs against it and pushes back. In air we call this **air resistance**; in general it is called **drag**. It gets bigger the faster you go, which is why cycling into the wind at speed is such hard work.

## Helpful friction, unhelpful friction

Friction is often **useful**:

- the grip of your shoes lets you walk without slipping;
- brakes press pads against a wheel, so friction slows the bike down;
- a pencil leaves a mark because friction rubs graphite off onto the paper.

Sometimes it gets **in the way**:

- it wears things out, such as shoe soles and tyres;
- it turns movement into heat, wasting energy in machines.

To reduce friction we use a **lubricant**: a slippery layer, such as oil or grease, that keeps the surfaces' bumps apart.

<Warning>
Rub your hands together fast: they get warm. Friction always turns some movement into heat. In machines, that heat can be a real problem, which is why engines need oil.
</Warning>

<Remember>
Friction acts **against sliding**. It is bigger for **rougher** surfaces and when surfaces **press together harder**. Air resistance (drag) is friction with air, and it grows with speed.
</Remember>

## In the real world

<RealWorld title="Ice skating">
Skates glide because ice gives very little friction: its surface has a thin, watery layer that makes sliding easy. Great for skating, dangerous for walking.
</RealWorld>

<RealWorld title="Bike brakes">
Squeeze the brake lever and rubber pads grip the wheel rim. The friction between them turns the bike's movement into heat, and the bike slows down. Touch the rim after a long downhill and you will feel it is warm.
</RealWorld>

<RealWorld title="Tyre treads in the rain">
The grooves in a tyre push rainwater out of the way so the rubber can still grip the road. Without them, a car could slide on a thin layer of water.
</RealWorld>
