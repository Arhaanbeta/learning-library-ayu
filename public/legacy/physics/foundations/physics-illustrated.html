Part I · The big picture

A small set of ideas explains a lot

A glass slips from your hand. Its position changes, Earth pulls on it, and its motion becomes faster. These are three connected observations.

Motion describes the change. Forces describe interactions. Energy helps us account for what those interactions transfer or transform. Each answers a different question about the same event.

Physics studies these patterns. Chemistry studies substances and their transformations. A burning candle involves both: changing substances release energy, warm air rises, and light spreads.

Read an equation as a comparison

Imagine two walkers covering the same distance. The one taking less time has the greater average speed. We need a quantity meaning “distance for each second”.

average speed = distance travelled / time taken

The division shares the total distance across the elapsed time. m/s means metres per second. A larger answer means more distance covered per second, on average.

Keep time fixed: doubling distance doubles average speed. Keep distance fixed: doubling time halves it. Always say what stays fixed before predicting a change.

Units help check an equation. Distance divided by time can describe speed; distance multiplied by time cannot. A unit tells us what kind of comparison we made.

Three habits to carry through the guide

Choose what you are studying. A ball alone and the ball together with Earth are different systems. A system is simply the object or group we select.

Separate a description from a cause. A sloping motion graph describes changing position. It does not identify which object exerted the force.

Name the model. “Ignore air resistance” isolates gravity’s effect. It is a useful approximation, not a claim that air never matters.

The mathematical thread

“Per second” measures a rate. “Per kilogram” or “per square metre” compares equal portions. Multiplication often rebuilds a total from those portions. These ideas will reappear throughout physics.

1 · Motion and mechanics

A journey and its arrow

Walk along one side of a playground, then turn and walk along the next. Your route has two pieces. Your change of place needs only one arrow.

Distance adds the lengths you actually travel. Displacement points directly from your starting position to your finishing position. Physics needs both because route and result differ.

The coloured arrows show the two parts of the walk. Place the second arrow’s tail at the first arrow’s head. The blue arrow gives their combined displacement.

Its length represents how far the finish lies from the start. Its arrowhead represents direction. It need not follow the route you walked.

A vector is a quantity needing both size and direction. Displacement is one. A quantity needing only size, such as distance, is called a scalar.

That is all the vector machinery we need here: follow arrows head to tail; draw one arrow from the first start to the final finish.

On a straight line, signs do the drawing

Choose right as positive. A displacement of +3 m means three metres right; −3 m means three metres left. The minus sign supplies direction.

Three metres right, then two metres left, gives a displacement of +1 m. The distance is 5 m because both pieces contribute to the route.

displacement = final position − starting position

Positions are measured from the same chosen origin. Moving the origin changes the two position numbers together; their difference, the displacement, stays the same.

A tempting idea

“If I travelled a long way, my displacement must be large.” This seems natural because both use metres. But a complete lap ends at its start: distance is positive and displacement is zero. The arrow records the change of place, not the effort of travelling.

Predict from the picture

Two people follow different routes between the same start and finish. Their displacements match. Their distances need not. Later, force and velocity arrows will use this same language.

1 · Motion and mechanics

Speed, velocity and viewpoint

A bus travels along a road. A seated passenger is still relative to the bus but moving relative to a tree. “Moving” needs a stated viewpoint.

A reference frame is the set of surroundings we measure positions against. For everyday road journeys, the ground is usually a convenient choice.

Speed tells how quickly distance is covered. Velocity tells how quickly position changes, including direction. Returning to the start separates them immediately.

average speed = total distance / total time

average velocity = displacement / total time

Both have units m/s. Average speed cannot be negative. Along a chosen line, negative average velocity means the overall displacement points in the negative direction.

A speedometer estimates speed over a short interval. A whole-journey average cannot reveal each stop or burst of speed. These are different questions about the journey.

On this position–time graph, moving right means later time. Moving upward means a more positive position; the vertical axis is not the road’s physical height.

The slope is position change divided by time: velocity. A steeper rising line means greater positive velocity. A horizontal line means the position is unchanged.

A falling line means motion towards more negative positions. It need not mean slowing down. Equal slopes mean equal velocities, even at different positions.

Area under this graph is not distance. A graph’s meaning comes from its axes. We will use area with a velocity–time graph instead.

Walking inside the bus

Walking forwards along a steadily moving bus adds your motion to the bus’s motion, as seen from the road. Walking backwards subtracts it. The passengers and roadside observer describe different velocities for the same person.

A check on averages

Half the journey time at one speed and half at another permits a simple average of those speeds. Equal distances do not: the slower part occupies more time. Always return to total distance divided by total time.

1 · Motion and mechanics

Changing velocity needs its own quantity

Two trolleys can have the same velocity now, yet behave differently next second. One keeps moving steadily; the other gains speed. Velocity alone cannot describe that difference.

Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes. It can describe speeding up, slowing down, or turning. Direction matters because velocity is a vector.

average acceleration = change in velocity / time taken

The unit m/s² means “metres per second of velocity change, per second”. It is a rate of change of a rate, not a new kind of distance.

Choose right as positive. Leftward acceleration reduces a rightward velocity. But it increases the speed of an object already travelling left. Negative does not automatically mean slowing.

Here the vertical axis is velocity, not position. Slope means acceleration. A horizontal line means constant velocity; a steeper rise means greater positive acceleration.

For the same velocity change, doubling the time halves average acceleration. For the same time, doubling the velocity change doubles it.

When acceleration stays constant

Equal velocity changes in equal times give a straight graph. Let u be starting velocity, v final velocity, a constant acceleration and t elapsed time.

velocity gained = at, so v = u + at

The product’s units explain it: (m/s²) × s = m/s. Add that velocity change to the starting velocity. This rule needs constant acceleration.

Each narrow strip under a velocity–time graph represents velocity × time: a little displacement. Adding the strips gives the whole displacement.

For the straight graph, average velocity is (u + v)/2. Therefore displacement s = (u + v)t/2. The rectangle plus triangle makes the same area.

Read the signs physically

Area below the time axis contributes negative displacement. Adding the sizes of all areas gives distance. At the top of a thrown ball’s flight, upward velocity is momentarily zero while downward acceleration continues: zero velocity does not require zero acceleration.

1 · Motion and mechanics

What changes motion?

Push a trolley, then let go. It slows on a rough floor. Everyday experience suggests that motion needs a continuing push. Now imagine reducing the friction.

The trolley keeps moving longer. Remove all opposing interactions in the ideal model and there is no reason for its velocity to change.

Newton’s first law: with zero net external force, an object keeps the same velocity. That includes remaining still. Inertia names this persistence of motion.

Several objects may push or pull on the trolley. Their combined effect, added with directions, is the net force. A force is an interaction, measured in newtons, N.

This is a force diagram: isolate one object and draw only forces acting on it. Name their sources. An arrow showing motion would be a different kind of arrow.

How strongly will the velocity change?

For the same mass, a greater net force produces greater acceleration. For the same net force, a greater mass produces less acceleration.

acceleration = net force / mass, or Fnet = ma

Here m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in m/s². One newton changes a 1 kg mass’s velocity by 1 m/s each second.

Double net force while mass stays fixed: acceleration doubles. Double mass while net force stays fixed: acceleration halves. Reverse net force: acceleration reverses.

Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity. Balanced forces can act on a moving object. Newton’s second law describes ordinary motion of a fixed mass.

The starting bus

As the bus accelerates, your feet move with the floor while the rest of you initially keeps its earlier velocity. You lean back relative to the bus. From the roadside, no mysterious backward force is needed; the bus is moving ahead beneath you.

Choose the object first

A book rests on a table. Earth pulls it down; the table pushes it up. Equal forces on this one book give no acceleration. Next we must distinguish this balance from an interaction pair.

1 · Motion and mechanics

Weight and falling together

Press your palm against a wall. Your hand pushes the wall, and the wall pushes your hand. Both forces belong to one interaction and occur together.

Newton’s third law: the forces two objects exert on each other are equal in size and opposite in direction. They act on different objects.

The wall’s force on your hand belongs on your hand’s force diagram. Your hand’s force on the wall belongs on the wall’s diagram. They do not cancel there.

The book’s upward support and downward weight can balance, but they are not a third-law pair: both act on the book and have different partners.

Mass, weight and support are different

Mass measures inertia and is measured in kg. Weight is Earth’s gravitational pull, measured in N. Near Earth’s surface, weight is W = mg.

Here g is the downward acceleration of free fall, about 9.8 m/s², also expressible as 9.8 N/kg. “Newtons per kilogram” says how much pull each kilogram experiences.

Double mass at the same location: weight doubles. Move where g is smaller: weight decreases while mass stays the same.

Gravity gives a heavier object more force but it also has proportionally more inertia. Ignoring air resistance, a = mg/m = g: both effects grow together.

Support force is a surface’s push, perpendicular to it; “normal force” is its technical name. A scale senses support. A freely falling scale reads zero although gravity acts.

Drop one ball; throw another sideways

Release both at the same height and time, with no initial vertical velocity. Ignoring air resistance, their downward motion matches. The thrown ball also travels sideways. The two motions combine; the sideways launch does not delay its fall.

At the top is not “no gravity”

A ball thrown upward has zero vertical velocity at its highest point. Gravity is still changing that velocity downward. Before the top it slows the rise; afterwards it speeds the fall. The interaction never switched off.

1 · Motion and mechanics

Friction and turning motion

Push a heavy box gently. It stays still even though your push is real. The floor exerts an opposing sideways force: static friction.

Push harder while the box still stays still. Friction increases to match you. It adjusts up to a maximum; it is not automatically at that maximum.

At contact, tiny regions deform and adhere. Friction is a combined effect of these microscopic interactions. Roughness alone cannot predict it reliably.

Once sliding begins, sliding friction opposes the relative sliding of the surfaces. It is often smaller than maximum static friction, though materials can behave differently.

Pressing the same surfaces together more strongly usually increases the available friction. For many dry contacts, a useful model makes that maximum proportional to the normal force.

Friction need not point backwards

When you walk forwards, your shoe tends to push the ground backwards. Static friction from the ground pushes you forwards. Friction opposes slipping at contact, not all motion.

A driven tyre similarly pushes backwards on the road. The road’s forward force accelerates the vehicle. Without enough grip, the tyre slips instead.

Turning is also acceleration

Imagine a ball travelling around a circle at constant speed. Its velocity arrow continually changes direction. A continuing net force is therefore required.

For steady circular motion, that net force points towards the centre. Centripetal means “centre-seeking”; it names the required direction, not a new force source.

For a car turning on a level road, tyre friction can supply it. For a ball on a string, tension — the string’s pull — supplies it.

A tempting idea

“A released ball flies radially outwards.” Circular motion makes this feel plausible. But remove the inward force and the ball initially continues along its present velocity: the tangent, touching the circle at the release point. The curve needs a cause; straight motion does not need a continuing push.

Change one condition

Take the same bend faster. The velocity direction must change more quickly, and a larger inward force is needed. If grip cannot supply it, the car cannot follow that same circular path.

1 · Motion and mechanics

Forces can turn objects

Push a door near its hinge, then near its handle. The same sideways push turns it more effectively at the handle. Distance from the turning axis matters.

The relevant distance is the shortest, perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force’s line of action. We call it the lever arm.

turning effect = force × perpendicular lever arm

The turning effect is called moment of a force, or torque. Its unit N m records a force acting with a lever arm. It describes turning, not energy.

With force fixed, doubling the lever arm doubles torque. With the lever arm fixed, doubling force doubles torque. A force directed through the pivot has zero lever arm.

Choose clockwise as positive, or anticlockwise; either works if used consistently. Opposite torques can balance even when the individual forces have different sizes.

Where should you support an object?

Balance a ruler on a finger. Shift some mass towards one end and the balancing point shifts towards it. More mass there means more gravitational pull there.

The centre of mass is the average location of an object’s mass, weighted towards its heavier parts. In uniform gravity, its weight acts as if concentrated there.

A uniform ruler’s centre lies halfway along. An uneven object’s need not. The centre of mass can even lie in empty space, as for a ring.

For an upright object on a level base, the vertical line through this centre must fall within the support area. Beyond the edge, weight produces a tipping torque.

Why a wider stance helps

Spreading your feet widens the support area. You can lean farther before the line of your weight passes outside it. Carrying a heavy bag changes the combined centre of mass, so you adjust your posture.

Balance has two requirements

No overall acceleration needs balanced forces. No change in rotation also needs balanced torques. Two equal opposite forces at different places can form a turning pair. Later, this will explain why a motor coil rotates.

1 · Motion and mechanics

A push lasts: momentum and impulse

Compare two moving trolleys at the same velocity. The heavier one needs a greater stopping impulse. Compare equal masses: the faster one needs a greater impulse.

We combine mass and velocity into momentum, a vector that helps describe how motion is exchanged in interactions.

momentum p = mv

Its unit kg m/s means mass multiplied by velocity. At fixed velocity, doubling mass doubles momentum. At fixed mass, reversing velocity reverses momentum. Rest gives zero momentum.

Now let a steady net force act for time Δt. The symbol Δ means “change” or “interval”. From Fnet = ma and a = Δv/Δt:

Fnet Δt = m Δv = Δp

The left side is impulse: force multiplied by the time it acts. Its unit N s equals kg m/s. The right side is the momentum change of a fixed mass.

If the force varies, use its average over the interval. The area under a force–time graph gives impulse: each strip contributes force × a little time.

For the same stopping change, increasing stopping time reduces average force. Moving your hands back while catching a ball applies this idea.

Interactions exchange momentum

When two skaters push apart, equal opposite forces act for the same time. They gain equal opposite momentum changes. Their combined momentum stays unchanged.

This conservation holds for a chosen system with zero net external impulse. Individual momenta may change greatly. Internal forces exchange momentum within the boundary.

A rocket needs no surrounding air

The rocket pushes exhaust backwards; the exhaust pushes the rocket forwards. For rocket plus exhaust, momentum can balance without air to push against. The rocket alone loses mass, so we must account for the momentum carried away.

A collision can preserve one thing and change another

If external impulse is negligible, total momentum is conserved whether objects bounce or stick. Their kinetic energy need not be conserved: deformation, sound and warming can receive energy. Equal opposite momenta can cancel; positive kinetic energies cannot.

1 · Motion and mechanics

Pressure: the same push, differently spread

Press a book against your palm, first flat and then edge-on, using the same force. The smaller contact area concentrates the push. Force alone misses that difference.

pressure p = perpendicular force F / area A

One pascal, Pa, means one newton per square metre: N/m². Pressure describes force per area; it is not an extra force alongside the contact force.

At fixed area, doubling force doubles pressure. At fixed force, doubling area halves pressure. Snowshoes spread a person’s load; a sharp blade concentrates a force.

A fluid can push in every direction

A gas pushes through countless molecular collisions. A stationary liquid also transmits pressure in all directions. At a surface, its pressure force acts perpendicular to that surface.

Why does pressure increase with depth? Imagine a vertical water column with cross-sectional area A and height h. The lower water must support the column’s weight.

Density, ρ (rho), is mass per volume: ρ = m/V, in kg/m³. Thus the column’s mass is ρAh, and its weight is ρAhg.

Divide that weight by A: the pressure increase is ρgh. The area cancels because a wider column weighs more but spreads its weight over proportionally more area.

pressure at depth = pressure at the surface + ρgh

Here g is gravitational field strength and h is vertical depth. This model uses stationary liquid of uniform density and approximately constant g.

Double depth or density, holding the other quantities fixed: double the pressure increase. At h = 0, that increase vanishes; surface pressure need not be zero.

Why water supports a submerged object

The bottom experiences greater pressure than the top. Their forces leave an upward result: buoyancy. It equals the weight of fluid displaced. Floating requires this upward force to balance weight; density alone is not a force.

Connect the ideas

A hollow steel boat can float although solid steel is denser than water. Its large outer volume displaces enough water before it is submerged. For floating, count the boat and its enclosed air together.

2 · Energy

Force acting through a distance

Push a trolley along the floor. Your force changes its motion while the trolley moves. Physics calls this mode of energy transfer work.

Energy is tracked through motion, stored arrangements and internal changes. It can transfer between systems. Work names one such transfer, not a separate substance.

For a constant force acting along an object’s displacement, more force or more distance means more transferred energy.

work W = Fd

Here F is force in newtons and d is displacement along the force in metres. The unit is N m, called a joule, J.

At fixed force, doubling distance doubles work. At fixed distance, doubling force doubles work. The equation applies directly when force and displacement point the same way.

An opposing force does negative work: it removes kinetic energy from the object. A perpendicular force does zero work on its motion along that displacement.

For a slanting force, only its part along the displacement contributes. The sideways part changes direction instead. This is one reason force needs an arrow.

Following the energy account

If an object starts from rest and gains speed, it gains kinetic energy: energy associated with motion. Net work on an object changes its kinetic energy.

net work = change in kinetic energy

“Net” matters again. Your push may do positive work while friction does negative work. Add the work of all forces to find the kinetic energy change.

A tempting idea

“Holding a heavy bag still must count as work.” Your muscles tire, so this seems obvious. But the bag has no displacement: you do no mechanical work on it while holding it still. Chemical processes within your muscles still use energy and warm your body.

A turn without a speed change

In ideal steady circular motion, the inward force is perpendicular to the instantaneous motion. It changes velocity’s direction without changing speed or kinetic energy. Forces can change direction without transferring energy to that motion.

Force acting through time changes momentum; force acting through distance transfers energy. The units N s and N m distinguish impulse from work.

2 · Energy

Why kinetic energy contains speed squared

Imagine identical trolleys brought to rest by the same steady braking force. One starts twice as fast. Predict the stopping distance before looking at an equation.

Same force and same mass give the same deceleration. The faster trolley has twice as much speed to lose, so it takes twice as long to stop.

During each stop, speed falls steadily to zero. The faster trolley’s average speed is also twice as large. Twice the average speed for twice the time gives four times the distance.

The vertical axis is speed; the horizontal axis is time. Area means distance because speed × time has units of metres. Each stopping graph is a triangle.

The larger triangle is twice as high and twice as wide. Its area is four times as large. The same braking force therefore does four times as much stopping work.

Compress the reasoning into an equation

Let m be mass, v starting speed, F the positive braking-force magnitude and t stopping time. The fixed-mass impulse relation gives Ft = mv, hence t = mv/F.

The triangle gives d = ½vt. Substituting the stopping time is allowed because it describes this same stop: d = mv²/(2F).

The magnitude of braking work is Fd. The F cancels, leaving the energy removed:

kinetic energy K = ½mv²

Here v is speed, so K is never negative. The unit kg m²/s² equals a joule. Zero speed gives zero kinetic energy in that reference frame.

At fixed speed, doubling mass doubles K. At fixed mass, doubling speed quadruples K. Halving speed leaves one-quarter of the kinetic energy.

What the derivation does and does not assume

Steady braking made the graph easy to read. For ordinary speeds, ½mv² describes translational kinetic energy regardless of how the object was accelerated. We have used a simple process to discover a general relationship.

Separate momentum from energy

Reverse the trolley at the same speed: its momentum reverses, but its kinetic energy stays the same. They measure different aspects of motion. A harder collision also depends on stopping time, deformation and where the energy goes.

2 · Energy

Storing energy in an arrangement

Lift a book slowly. You push upward while it moves upward, transferring energy. When released, it can regain motion. What changed was the book–Earth arrangement.

The stored energy is gravitational potential energy. Near Earth, weight mg is approximately constant. Lifting through height h against that force requires work mgh.

increase in gravitational potential energy = mgh

Here m is mass, g gravitational field strength and h the rise in height. The units kg × N/kg × m give N m, or joules.

At fixed g and height, double mass: double the increase. At fixed mass and g, double height: double it. A sideways move at the same height makes no gravitational change.

We choose a reference height where gravitational potential energy is zero. Heights below it can have negative values. The physically useful quantity is the energy difference.

A spring stores energy differently

Stretch a spring gently. The farther you stretch it, the stronger it pulls back. For an ideal spring within its elastic range, pulling-force magnitude F = kx.

Here x is extension from the relaxed length, and k is stiffness in N/m. Double x at fixed k: double the force. The spring’s restoring force points back towards its relaxed position.

On a force–extension graph, each strip represents force × a little extension: work. The total area under the straight line is a triangle.

elastic energy = ½ × x × kx = ½kx²

The one-half appears because force rises from zero, rather than staying at kx throughout. Double extension within this model: stored energy quadruples. At zero extension it is zero.

A landscape picture that has a precise meaning

A marble in a bowl loses gravitational potential energy as it rolls towards the bottom. At rest there, a small displacement creates a restoring tendency: stable equilibrium. On an upside-down bowl, a small displacement carries it farther away: unstable equilibrium. Energy and force describe the same arrangement from different viewpoints.

Check the boundary

A spring stretched too far may stay deformed. Then F = kx and ½kx² are no longer reliable. A model’s boundary matters as much as its equation.

2 · Energy

Energy, power and efficiency

A pendulum speeds up while descending and slows while rising. Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy exchange. With negligible friction, their sum stays constant.

Real pendulums gradually stop. Air and the pivot receive energy as internal motion and sound. Mechanical energy — kinetic plus potential — decreases, while total energy remains accounted for.

For an isolated system, no energy enters or leaves, so total energy stays constant. For a system with transfers across its boundary, include those transfers in the account.

The same energy can be transferred at different rates

Two motors lift the same load to the same height. Their useful work matches. The one completing the lift sooner has greater average power.

average power P = energy transferred / time taken

One watt, W, is one joule per second. At fixed energy, halving the time doubles power. At fixed time, transferring twice the energy doubles it.

If power stays constant, energy transferred = Pt. A power–time graph’s area gives energy: add the contributions from successive intervals when power varies.

Be careful: W as a unit means watt; W as a symbol earlier meant work. The surrounding words and units tell you which is intended.

Useful for which purpose?

A lamp transfers energy into visible light and thermal energy. If our purpose is lighting, only some output is useful. The rest still exists.

efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input

Multiply by 100% to express the fraction as a percentage. The ratio has no units because both energies use the same unit. The useful purpose must be stated.

A tempting idea

“Friction destroys energy.” Motion disappearing makes this believable. But rough surfaces deform and excite microscopic motion: they warm. Energy is conserved; energy concentrated in organised motion becomes dispersed and harder to recover fully as useful work.

Connect the chapters

Two objects stick together in an isolated collision. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy may decrease. Ask where that energy went before calling it “lost”. The next chapter examines the microscopic part of that account.

3 · Heat and temperature

Heat is energy in transit

Rub your hands together. Their organised motion slows, yet your hands warm. Friction transfers energy into the microscopic motion and interactions of particles.

The energy associated with these microscopic motions and arrangements is internal energy. We distinguish it from a whole object’s motion across the room.

Temperature tells us about thermal state and predicts which way energy transfers spontaneously through thermal contact. At the same temperature, there is no net heat flow between objects.

For an ideal gas, temperature in kelvin is directly related to average random translational kinetic energy per particle. In solids and liquids, interactions also matter.

So “temperature is total energy” cannot be right. A large amount of warm water may have more internal energy than a tiny amount at a higher temperature.

Heat names a transfer

Bring a warm spoon into cooler water. Interactions transfer energy from spoon to water. Heat means energy transferred because of a temperature difference; its unit is the joule.

Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or kelvin. It is not measured in joules. Internal energy belongs to a system; heat describes energy crossing its boundary.

If two bodies are at different temperatures, spontaneous net transfer goes from hotter to colder. Individual microscopic exchanges occur both ways; their net effect favours that direction.

As the temperatures approach equality, the net transfer weakens. Thermal equilibrium is the state with no temperature difference driving net heat transfer.

Why a metal handle feels colder

Metal and wood in the same room can have the same temperature. Metal transfers energy away from a warm hand faster, so it often feels colder.

Touch senses the resulting energy transfer as well as temperature. It is not a reliable thermometer for comparing materials.

A tempting idea

“Cold flows into my hand.” The sensation encourages that picture. In this situation, energy flows out of your warmer hand into the cooler object. Cold is not a second flowing substance. To cool something, energy must be removed from its internal store.

A careful microscopic bridge

Warming commonly increases random particle motion; melting can instead change particle arrangements while temperature stays steady. Internal energy includes both. That distinction will explain a flat part of the heating graph.

3 · Heat and temperature

Three routes for thermal energy

Put one end of a metal spoon in hot soup. The handle warms without soup travelling through the spoon. Energy can move through material without bulk flow.

Conduction passes energy through interactions among neighbouring particles. In metals, mobile electrons also carry energy through the material. The particles themselves are never completely motionless.

With other conditions fixed, a larger temperature difference usually gives faster conduction. A thicker layer slows transfer; a larger area gives more paths for it.

Air conducts poorly. Trapping it in small pockets restricts circulation too, helping insulation work. Conductivity describes transfer; it differs from a material’s capacity to store energy.

A fluid can carry its internal energy with it

Heat water from below. Over ordinary temperatures, warming makes it expand, so the same mass occupies more volume: its density decreases.

Denser surrounding water produces a greater upward buoyant force than the warmed region’s weight. The warmed water rises; cooler water moves into its place.

This circulation transfers energy by convection. It connects microscopic expansion to density, pressure, buoyancy and visible motion. The same force ideas explain the thermal effect.

“Heat rises” is a misleading shortcut. Buoyant warm fluid may rise under gravity. Conduction and radiation can transfer energy in any direction. Pumps can also force fluid circulation.

Radiation crosses empty space

Sunlight reaches Earth across space. Thermal radiation carries energy as electromagnetic waves; it needs no material medium. You will meet these waves again in optics.

All ordinary objects emit and absorb radiation. Hotter objects generally emit more intensely. Net exchange with surroundings depends on both temperatures and surface properties.

Dark, matt surfaces often absorb and emit thermal radiation effectively. Polished metallic surfaces often reflect it. Visible colour alone does not determine every infrared property.

A flask interrupts all three routes

The vacuum gap greatly reduces conduction and prevents convection across the gap. Reflective walls reduce radiative transfer. A stopper reduces transfer through the opening. The contents still cool eventually because the insulation is not perfect.

Predict the direction

Put an ice cube above a warm object without contact. Can the object lose energy to it? Yes, through net radiation. No rule requires thermal energy to travel upwards.

3 · Heat and temperature

Warming is different from melting

Equal masses of water and metal receiving equal energy can warm by different amounts. Materials need different energy inputs for the same temperature rise.

Specific heat capacity, c, is energy needed per kilogram per degree of temperature rise. Its unit is J/(kg °C), equivalently J/(kg K) for a temperature change.

One kilogram and one degree need c joules. Multiply by the number of kilograms, then by the number of degrees:

energy transferred Q = mcΔT

Here m is mass and ΔT is the temperature change. This applies without a change of state and with c approximately constant over the interval.

At fixed c and temperature rise, twice the mass needs twice the energy. At fixed mass and energy, a larger c means a smaller temperature rise.

On this graph, moving right means more energy supplied. Moving upward means higher temperature. For the same mass, a gentler rising slope means larger heat capacity.

Where does the energy go on the flat part?

During melting at fixed pressure, added energy changes the particles’ arrangements and interactions while solid and liquid coexist. The temperature can remain steady.

Vaporisation similarly requires energy to separate particles against attractions. We are not normally breaking water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen.

Specific latent heat, L, is energy per kilogram for a particular change of state, in J/kg. “Latent” means hidden from a temperature rise.

energy for the change of state Q = mL

Double the mass undergoing the same change at the same conditions: double the energy. The reverse change releases the corresponding energy.

Sweating and steam

Evaporation needs energy even below the boiling point. Sweat that evaporates draws energy from skin and surroundings. Steam condensing on skin releases latent energy, then the resulting water can cool further. A phase change can transfer substantial energy without an initial temperature change.

Read the flat line correctly

A melting mixture receives energy without warming. Its internal energy changes through particle arrangement. Temperature cannot track every energy change.

4 · Waves

An oscillation passed from place to place

Displace a hanging mass on a spring and release it. The spring pulls it back. At the middle, its velocity carries it onwards before the spring slows it.

A restoring force and inertia together produce repeated motion, called an oscillation. Without damping, energy moves between kinetic and stored forms. Damping transfers energy to the surroundings.

Now connect many neighbouring particles. Disturb one: its interaction with the next passes the disturbance along. A wave is a travelling pattern of disturbance.

Each point of a rope moves mainly up and down while the wave travels sideways. The movement of the pattern differs from the movement of the material.

In this simple model, energy travels along the rope without the rope travelling along with it. Real waves may also produce secondary flows; the basic distinction remains useful.

Two directions to compare

In a transverse wave, disturbance is perpendicular to travel: the rope moves up and down while the pattern moves horizontally.

In a longitudinal wave, disturbance is parallel to travel. Push and pull one end of a spring: crowded and spread-out regions travel along it.

Sound in air is longitudinal. Air particles oscillate backwards and forwards as compression patterns travel. Water surface waves are more complicated: particles generally move in loops.

Does every wave need a substance?

Rope and sound waves need a medium, the material carrying the disturbance. Its inertia and restoring interactions together determine how quickly the disturbance propagates.

Electromagnetic waves, including light, can travel through a vacuum. Their changing electric and magnetic fields carry energy; no material chain of particles is required.

A tempting idea

“If the wave reaches the far end, the original particles must reach it.” Watching a moving crest encourages this. Mark one rope point: it moves locally as the crest passes. Interactions pass the disturbance on; the marked material does not travel with the crest.

The mechanics connection

For one oscillator, force restores motion towards equilibrium while inertia carries it through. Couple neighbouring oscillators and the disturbance can spread. Waves grow naturally from the force and energy ideas already developed.

4 · Waves

Wavelength, period and frequency

Photograph a repeating rope wave. The distance from one crest to the next is a spacing in space. Watching one marked point gives a repeating pattern in time.

Wavelength, λ (lambda), is distance between successive matching points, in metres. Period, T, is the time for one complete cycle, in seconds.

Frequency, f, counts cycles per second. Its unit hertz, Hz, means one cycle per second. Taking twice as long per cycle means half as many cycles per second.

f = 1/T

Amplitude, A, is the largest displacement from equilibrium. For a rope, it is measured in metres. Peak-to-trough height is twice the amplitude.

The upper graph is a snapshot across positions. Moving right means farther along the rope. The lower graph follows one point; moving right means later time.

Their similar shapes can hide their different meanings. A horizontal gap between crests gives λ in the first and T in the second.

Build the speed relationship

During one cycle, the repeating pattern advances one wavelength. Distance travelled by the pattern divided by time gives its speed:

wave speed v = λ/T = fλ

Read fλ as “cycles each second × metres in each cycle”. The result is metres per second. The v here is pattern speed, not particle speed.

For a given rope tension and mass per length, small waves travel at approximately fixed speed. Driving twice as frequently then produces half the wavelength.

Do not hold v, f and λ independently fixed. If f doubles while v remains fixed, λ must halve. The equation constrains how the quantities fit together.

Height is not spacing

A larger amplitude means a larger disturbance, not necessarily a higher frequency or greater wave speed. Within the small-wave model and unchanged medium, you can make taller waves without making their crests arrive more frequently.

Read a slope carefully

On a displacement–time graph, slope gives the marked particle’s velocity. On a displacement–position snapshot, slope describes the rope’s tilt. Neither graph’s vertical height is the wave’s travel speed.

4 · Waves

Waves meet boundaries and waves

A pulse reaches a rope’s end and returns. Boundary interactions can send energy backwards, pass it onwards, or absorb it.

Reflection is a returning wave. Reflection and transmission can both occur at the same boundary; a wave need not be entirely blocked to reflect.

A change of speed can change direction

Imagine a broad wavefront entering a slower region at an angle. One side enters and slows first, so the front turns. This is refraction.

Frequency remains fixed by the source at a stationary boundary. Since v = fλ, slower travel means a shorter wavelength. At perpendicular entry, speed changes without bending.

Diffraction is spreading around edges and through openings. It becomes especially noticeable when the opening is comparable to the wavelength. A wide opening still has edge spreading.

This helps sound bend around a doorway. Visible light has much shorter wavelengths, so ordinary doorways produce much less noticeable diffraction of light.

What happens where two waves meet?

For small waves in a linear medium, their displacements add at each point: superposition. Two upward disturbances reinforce; equal upward and downward disturbances can cancel there.

Reinforcement and cancellation produce interference. The waves continue afterwards. Zero displacement at one place does not mean all wave energy is destroyed.

For repeating waves, their timing matters. In phase means matching points in their cycles arrive together. Opposite timing can make a crest meet a trough.

Noise cancellation

A microphone detects sound and electronics produce a second pressure variation. At the intended listening position, opposing variations reduce the sound. The cancellation depends on location and timing; it does not erase the original sound everywhere.

A tempting idea

“Shorter wavelength means lower frequency” confuses spacing with timing. At fixed speed, closer crests pass more often: frequency is higher. Across a boundary, wavelength can shrink because speed falls while frequency stays fixed. State what remains fixed.

The bridge to optics

Reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference describe light as well as material waves. A ray diagram will track the travel direction; a wavefront picture helps explain why that direction changes.

5 · Sound

Sound is a pressure pattern

A loudspeaker cone moves forwards. It crowds nearby air slightly, raising pressure. That air pushes on the next region, so the compression travels away.

When the cone moves backwards, a lower-pressure region follows. Compression means pressure and density above their undisturbed values; rarefaction means below them.

The particles mainly move back and forth locally. The pressure pattern travels from speaker to listener. Your eardrum responds to those changing pressures.

Sound needs matter: a gas, liquid or solid can carry suitable disturbances. A vacuum has no material particles and interactions to carry ordinary sound.

What sets the speed?

Compressing matter produces a restoring response. Greater stiffness tends to pass a disturbance on more rapidly; greater inertia tends to resist rapid changes.

The balance of these properties determines sound speed. “Particles are closer together” is not a complete explanation. Sound often travels faster in solids, but density alone cannot predict it.

For small sound waves in unchanged air, making the source louder or raising its pitch does not significantly change travel speed. Frequency changes wavelength instead.

Why an echo needs a round trip

A sound pulse travels to a wall, reflects, and returns. If the wall is distance d away, the sound travels 2d. The measured delay covers both journeys.

distance to reflector d = vt/2

Here v is sound speed in the medium and t the round-trip time. Metres per second multiplied by seconds gives metres; the two divides out the return journey.

With sound speed fixed, twice the delay means twice the distance. If the reflector is very close, the return may blend into the original sound instead of sounding separate.

Echolocation

A bat emits sound and uses returning echoes to learn about objects. Sonar follows the same travel-time idea in water. The method needs the relevant sound speed; the speed in air cannot simply be reused in water.

Connect pressure with motion

Why can sound pass through still air? “Still” describes the air’s average bulk motion. Its particles can still oscillate locally, and those oscillations transmit a changing pressure. No steady wind from speaker to ear is needed.

5 · Sound

Pitch, loudness and resonance

A tuning fork vibrates rapidly, producing repeating pressure changes. The number of cycles per second largely determines pitch: higher frequency is usually heard as a higher note.

How large those pressure changes are helps determine loudness. Amplitude and frequency answer different questions: how strong each disturbance is, and how often it repeats.

Intensity is power carried per area, measured in W/m². It describes energy delivery at a place. Perceived loudness also depends on frequency and the listener.

For a simple travelling sound wave in the same medium, intensity is proportional to pressure-amplitude squared. Double that amplitude and intensity becomes four times as large.

The square echoes kinetic energy: greater particle motion carries disproportionately more energy. This relationship concerns small waves and fixed medium properties.

Why decibels do not count evenly

A sound intensity level in decibels, dB, compares intensity with a reference using a compressed scale. Adding 10 dB means ten times the intensity.

Another increase of 10 dB multiplies by ten again. This is a ratio scale, not “ten extra units” of intensity. Zero dB marks the reference, not total silence.

Resonance: energy supplied with the right timing

A child on a swing moves at a natural rhythm. Small pushes timed with the motion add energy repeatedly. Poorly timed pushes may remove energy instead.

A vibrating system driven near one of its natural frequencies can build a large response: resonance. Energy accumulates because the driving force repeatedly does positive work.

Damping removes energy as the system oscillates. The final amplitude reflects the balance between energy supplied and energy dissipated; resonance does not create energy.

Air in instruments and strings have natural vibration patterns. Resonance helps select strong notes. A vibrating surface can also transfer energy to surrounding air more effectively.

A tempting idea

“A louder note has a higher pitch.” Harder blows can make both seem to change in some everyday objects. But the quantities differ: frequency mainly sets pitch; amplitude influences loudness. A speaker can produce the same frequency at different amplitudes.

Apply the energy idea

Why does a swing grow slowly after several small, well-timed pushes? Each push transfers a little energy. The growing motion records accumulated transfers, not one unusually large force.

5 · Sound

Why a passing siren changes pitch

Imagine a siren emitting successive wavefronts at equal time intervals. If it stays still, each front expands around the same position.

Now move the siren forwards. Before releasing the next wavefront, it has moved closer to the one ahead. Fronts become crowded in front and spread out behind.

In still air, the sound travels at the air’s sound speed. The source’s motion changes the spacing between successive fronts, not that speed through the air.

An observer ahead meets the closer fronts more frequently: a higher received frequency. An observer behind meets wider-spaced fronts less frequently: a lower received frequency.

This shift in received frequency because of relative motion is the Doppler effect. The source can keep vibrating at the same frequency throughout.

A moving listener provides a second route

Keep the source still and move towards it. You meet oncoming wavefronts more often, even though their spacing in the air is unchanged.

Move away and you meet them less often. For sound, motion of source and listener relative to the medium matters; their effects have different detailed formulas.

We do not need those formulas to predict the basic result: approaching usually raises received frequency; separating usually lowers it, for ordinary motion along the sound’s path.

What the changed note tells you

An approaching siren is heard higher than its emitted note; a receding one lower. Passing the observer changes the direction of approach into retreat.

Loudness may also change because distance and sound direction change. That is a separate effect: getting louder does not cause the Doppler frequency shift.

Read this with the wave equation

At the listener, v = fλ still fits the travelling sound in stationary air. Closer fronts mean smaller λ and higher received f. The siren’s emission rate and the listener’s encounter rate are different quantities.

Connect frames and rates

Walk alongside a slowly moving siren at the same speed, keeping separation fixed in still air. Their source and listener effects cancel: you meet the emitted cycles at their emission rate. Relative motion and “events per second” are the ideas doing the work.

6 · Light and optics

Light travels; mirrors redirect it

You see a lamp because light reaches your eyes. You see a book because it redirects some incident light towards you. Seeing needs a path into the eye.

Light is an electromagnetic wave. In vacuum it travels at c, about 300 million metres per second (3 × 10⁸ m/s). It needs no material medium.

Visible light occupies a small frequency range. From lower to higher frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.

The same vacuum speed applies to all these waves. Since c = fλ, higher frequency means shorter wavelength. Different frequencies interact differently with matter.

Reflection and the normal line

A ray marks light’s travel direction. At a reflecting surface, draw a line perpendicular to the surface: the normal. Measure both ray angles from that line.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection

Rough surfaces also reflect, but their differently tilted patches send rays in different directions. A smooth mirror preserves an orderly pattern of reflected directions.

Your eye interprets incoming light as travelling straight. Extend reflected rays backwards on paper: they seem to meet behind a plane mirror.

That apparent source is a virtual image. Light does not actually meet behind the mirror. The image is the same size and distance behind as the object is in front.

The usual “left–right reversal” describes how we compare ourselves with the image. Geometrically, a plane mirror reverses the direction perpendicular to its surface: front versus back.

Curved mirrors change the pattern

A concave mirror, curving inward, brings rays parallel to its axis towards a focus. Far objects can form real, inverted images; objects inside the focal distance give enlarged virtual images.

A convex mirror, bulging outward, spreads rays. They appear to come from a smaller upright virtual image behind the mirror, giving a wider field of view.

Real or virtual?

A real image forms where rays actually meet and can be caught on a screen. A virtual image forms where their backward extensions meet. “Virtual” means apparent ray origin; it does not mean the image cannot be seen.

6 · Light and optics

Refraction and lenses

A wavefront enters glass at an angle. The part entering first slows first, turning the front. Light’s direction bends towards the normal. This is refraction.

Refractive index, n, compares vacuum speed c with light speed v in the material:

n = c/v

The speeds’ units cancel. Greater n means slower light in that material. This is an optical property, not simply mass density. Frequency stays unchanged at a stationary boundary.

Entering a higher-index medium obliquely bends light towards the normal; entering a lower-index medium bends it away. At normal incidence, speed changes without a direction change.

Since v = fλ, lower speed at unchanged frequency means shorter wavelength. Refraction therefore connects a ray’s direction with a wave’s spacing.

Two surfaces can work together

A glass convex lens in air is thicker in the middle. Refraction at its surfaces bends parallel incoming rays towards a focus: it is converging.

A concave lens is thinner in the middle and spreads incoming parallel rays: it is diverging. Their backward extensions meet at a virtual focus.

Focal length is the distance from a thin lens’s centre to its focus. Shorter focal length means stronger convergence or divergence for parallel incident rays.

A converging lens can make a real inverted image on a camera sensor or projector screen. Place an object within its focal length and it acts as a magnifier, producing an upright virtual image.

The eye’s cornea and lens together focus light on the retina. Changing the lens’s shape adjusts focus as object distance changes.

Correcting the focus

In a short-sighted eye, distant light focuses in front of the retina; a diverging lens reduces convergence. In a long-sighted eye, the available convergence is insufficient in relevant viewing conditions; a converging lens helps focus onto the retina.

The apparently shallow pool

Light from the bottom bends away from the normal as it exits water. Your eye traces those rays backwards in straight lines, locating an apparent source nearer the surface. The water did not move the bottom; it changed the ray paths.

6 · Light and optics

Colour and trapped light

White light can contain many visible wavelengths. A prism separates them because its refractive index depends on wavelength. This separation is dispersion.

For ordinary glass in visible light, violet bends more than red. We see a spread of colours because their outgoing directions differ.

A primary rainbow combines refraction, one internal reflection, and refraction again inside raindrops. Different colours reach your eye from different directions: red forms the outer edge.

A secondary rainbow involves two internal reflections and reverses the colour order. The drops redirect and separate existing sunlight; they do not manufacture the colours.

An object’s colour needs a light source

A red surface reflects some red wavelengths more strongly than others. Under pure green illumination, an ideal red-only reflector appears dark because no red light arrives to reflect.

Real surfaces reflect ranges of wavelengths, so actual colours depend on the material, illumination and observer. Absorbed light transfers energy to the material.

Air molecules scatter shorter visible wavelengths more strongly. This helps make the sky blue. A low Sun’s direct light crosses more atmosphere, losing more short-wavelength light from that path.

When the outward ray disappears

Light travelling from higher to lower refractive index bends away from the normal. Increase the incident angle: eventually the refracted ray runs along the boundary.

That incident angle is the critical angle. Beyond it, an ideal transparent boundary returns the light into the original medium: total internal reflection.

Both conditions matter: travel towards lower refractive index, and incidence beyond the critical angle, measured from the normal. A steep angle measured from the surface can be misleading.

Keeping light inside a fibre

A fibre’s core has higher refractive index than its surrounding cladding. Suitable rays repeatedly undergo total internal reflection, carrying signals along it. Real fibres still have losses; “total” describes reflection at the ideal boundary, not perfect transmission through any length.

Connect energy and rays

A converging mirror concentrates sunlight: similar power falls on less area, so intensity increases. The mirror redirects energy; it creates none. Pressure uses similar “per area” reasoning.

7 · Electricity

Charge, interaction and energy per charge

Rub a balloon against hair. Electrons may transfer between them. An object gaining electrons becomes negatively charged; losing them leaves a positive imbalance.

Charge is measured in coulombs, C. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. An overall neutral object contains positive and negative charges whose totals balance.

Charge is conserved: transfer changes where it is, not the total of an isolated system. In this example, protons remain bound inside atomic nuclei.

The balloon can attract a neutral wall because charges in the wall shift slightly. Opposite charge is drawn nearer; the nearer attraction can exceed the more distant repulsion.

A field separates source from test object

Earth produces a gravitational field; a mass placed there feels a force. Electric charges similarly produce an electric field; another charge responds to it.

electric field strength E = force on a positive test charge / its charge

Its unit N/C means force per coulomb. Field direction is the force direction on a positive charge. A negative charge experiences the opposite force direction.

The test charge must be small enough not to change the source arrangement appreciably. At fixed field, doubling the test charge doubles force. Field and force are not interchangeable.

Voltage is an energy comparison

Moving charge between two places can change electric potential energy. Potential difference, or voltage, is that energy change per charge, measured in J/C, called volts.

potential difference = change in electric potential energy / charge

One volt means one joule per coulomb. Double the transferred charge at the same voltage difference: double the associated energy, not the voltage.

Like gravitational height, electric potential needs a reference; potential differences are what matter for transfers. Electric potential energy belongs to the interacting charge arrangement.

Keep three questions separate

Charge: how much electric quantity? Field: what force would each positive coulomb experience here? Potential difference: how much energy changes per coulomb between these places? A battery uses chemical processes to maintain a potential difference between its terminals.

7 · Electricity

Current, voltage and resistance

Choose a cross-section of a wire and count the charge passing it. More charge in the same time means a larger current.

average current I = charge Q / time t

One ampere, A, means one coulomb per second. Twice the charge in the same time gives twice the average current. The same charge taking twice as long gives half.

In a metal, mobile electrons drift through the material. Conventional current points in the direction positive charge would move; electron drift is opposite.

Closing a circuit establishes an electric field through it. Electrons already throughout the wire respond; a lamp need not await electrons travelling all the way from the battery.

Voltage and current answer different questions

Voltage V measures energy transferred per charge across a component. Current I measures charge passing per second. A battery can have a terminal voltage while an open circuit has zero current.

In a resistor, electrons interact with the material, transferring energy to it. This impedes drift. Resistance compares the voltage across a component with its current.

resistance R = V/I; equivalently V = IR

The unit ohm, Ω, means volt per ampere. Larger R means less current for the same applied voltage. At fixed voltage, doubling resistance halves current.

Ohm’s law makes an additional physical claim: for an ohmic conductor under fixed conditions, especially temperature, current is proportional to voltage, so R stays constant.

On this I–V graph, voltage increases rightwards and current upwards. A straight line through the origin shows that proportionality. Its slope is 1/R; a steeper line means lower resistance.

A tempting idea

“Every component has one fixed resistance.” A simple resistor encourages this model. But a hot filament changes resistance, and a diode or LED has a strongly non-proportional I–V relationship. One measured V/I ratio does not predict its behaviour at every voltage.

A limited water comparison

A circulating flow helps picture charge per second; a pump helps picture energy supplied. But voltage is energy per charge, not literal pressure, and electrons are not consumed like fuel. Keep the quantities distinct.

7 · Electricity

Circuits conserve charge and energy

Join two resistors end-to-end with a battery. In a steady state, charge cannot continually pile up between them. The same current must pass through both.

This is a series circuit: one path. Across each resistor, electrical energy is transferred. Around the loop, total energy supplied per charge matches total energy transferred per charge.

series: I is the same; Vtotal = V₁ + V₂

For resistors, substitute V = IR. Since the same I multiplies both resistances, Rtotal = R₁ + R₂. More series resistance gives less current at fixed supply voltage.

Parallel paths share endpoints

Now connect each resistor across the same two battery terminals. Both span the same potential difference. This is a parallel circuit.

In steady operation, charge entering a junction each second equals charge leaving. Therefore:

parallel: V is the same; Itotal = I₁ + I₂

At fixed terminal voltage, lower-resistance branches carry more current. Adding a branch increases total current, lowering equivalent resistance.

Existing branches retain their voltage if the supply maintains it. Real batteries have internal resistance, so heavy loading can reduce terminal voltage and dim lamps.

The battery supplies energy, not fresh charge

Charge circulates around the complete conducting loop. A component receives energy while passing charge onwards. Current is not gradually “used up” through successive components.

A tempting idea

“The first lamp gets more current because it is nearer the battery.” This resembles consuming fuel along a route. But sustained unequal currents would accumulate charge between lamps. In a steady series circuit, current is the same throughout; energy per charge is shared between the components.

Independent branches

Household appliances use parallel branches so each can receive the supply voltage and be switched independently. An open break in one branch need not stop the others. A short circuit is different: a very low-resistance path can draw excessive current and activate protection.

Return to the foundations

The current rule at a junction expresses conservation of charge. The voltage rule around a loop expresses conservation of energy. Circuit rules grow from ideas already used in mechanics.

7 · Electricity

Electrical power: two “per” quantities multiply

A battery transfers V joules of energy per coulomb. A current of I amperes carries I coulombs per second. Multiply the two rates of comparison.

power P = IV

The units explain why: (C/s) × (J/C) = J/s. The coulombs cancel, leaving watts. Voltage says energy per charge; current says charge per time.

At fixed current, doubling voltage doubles power. At fixed voltage, doubling current doubles power. For constant power, energy transferred in time t is E = Pt.

An electricity meter totals energy, not instantaneous power. A kilowatt-hour, kWh, is the energy transferred by 1 kW for one hour; it equals 3.6 million joules.

Two forms that need their conditions stated

For a resistor obeying V = IR, replace V in P = IV. This gives P = I²R. At fixed R, twice the current gives four times the heating power.

Alternatively use I = V/R, giving P = V²/R. At fixed V, twice the resistance halves the power. These are compatible statements under different fixed conditions.

Microscopic interactions turn electrical energy into internal energy in the resistor. A heater exploits this; supply wires generally should minimise this unwanted transfer.

Why transmit at high voltage?

To deliver the same power, increasing voltage allows smaller current because P = IV. Smaller current means less heating of the same wires, following I²R.

This comparison holds transmitted power and wire resistance fixed. It does not mean high voltage always makes every device produce less heat.

Potential difference is essential

A bird touching only one wire has nearly the same electric potential at both feet, so little voltage is applied across its body. Bridging different potentials, such as another wire or Earth, changes the situation. A wire’s potential alone does not specify current through an object.

Charge or energy?

A lamp passes the same charge onward that enters it in steady operation, yet it transfers energy to light and surroundings. Which decreases around the resistor: charge flow rate or electric potential energy per charge? The latter; that is the voltage drop.

8 · Magnetism and electromagnetism

Magnetic fields and electromagnets

A compass turns near a magnet. The small magnet in the compass responds differently at different places. A magnetic field describes that local influence.

Draw an arrow showing where the compass’s north-seeking end points. Repeat at nearby positions. Joining those directions produces a field-line diagram.

Lines are our representation, not material threads. Their direction shows field direction; closer spacing conventionally represents greater strength. Outside a bar magnet, lines run from north to south.

A bar magnet has two poles. Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Cut an ordinary bar magnet and each piece has both a north and a south pole.

Magnetised materials contain many microscopic magnetic contributions aligned enough to reinforce. Cutting the material divides these aligned regions; it does not detach an isolated pole.

Current produces a magnetic field

A compass near a current-carrying wire turns. Around a straight wire, the magnetic field forms circles. Reverse the current and the field direction reverses.

Wind the wire into a coil. The turns’ fields reinforce inside, producing a bar-magnet-like pattern. Such a coil is called a solenoid.

An iron core can strengthen the field by aligning magnetic regions. More current or more turns per length generally strengthens a similar coil, until material limitations matter.

A switchable interaction

An electromagnet can lift suitable iron or steel objects when energised. A doorbell uses a related cycle: current attracts an arm, contact breaks, a spring returns the arm, and contact closes again. The circuit changes its own motion, which changes the circuit: feedback.

Earth and the compass

Earth’s field helps align a compass approximately north–south. The north-seeking end points towards the northern geographic region, which behaves magnetically like a south pole. Geographic names and magnetic attraction must be kept distinct.

Reconnect with electricity

Electric fields act on charge. Magnetic fields act on moving charges and magnetic objects. A compass maps field direction, but that arrow is not automatically the force direction on every moving charged particle. Different interactions need their own force rules.

8 · Magnetism and electromagnetism

Motors and generators

A current-carrying wire placed suitably in a magnetic field experiences a force. The field acts on moving charges; their interaction with the wire transfers the force to it.

Force direction depends on both current and field directions. Reversing either reverses the force; a wire parallel to the field has no magnetic force from this effect.

A motor uses a turning pair

In opposite sides of a coil, currents run opposite ways. A suitably directed magnetic field gives opposite forces on those sides. Because they act at different places, they produce torque.

Remember the door: force at a distance from an axis can turn an object. The coil’s forces can have zero net push while their turning effects reinforce.

A simple DC motor reverses coil current each half-turn using a split-ring contact, a commutator, so the useful torque continues in the same rotational direction.

Electrical input supplies mechanical output and internal-energy increases. A magnetic field helps direct the interaction; it is not a source of unlimited motor energy.

A generator changes the magnetic situation

Move a magnet relative to a coil, or turn a coil in a magnetic field. A changing magnetic field through the coil can produce an induced voltage: electromagnetic induction.

A complete conducting path allows that voltage to drive current. An open coil can have voltage without a sustained current. Mere motion is insufficient if the magnetic situation does not change appropriately.

Faster change or more coil turns generally gives a larger induced voltage. Reversing the change reverses its direction.

Why generating requires effort

The induced current’s magnetic effect opposes the change producing it: Lenz’s law. A generator supplying a load resists the driving motion. Mechanical work supplies electrical energy, with losses. If induction helped its own cause without an energy source, the energy account would fail.

Braking can recover energy

In regenerative braking, a moving vehicle drives its motor as a generator. The vehicle’s kinetic energy supplies electrical energy, and the opposing torque slows it. The motor need not rotate backwards; the direction of energy transfer changes.

8 · Magnetism and electromagnetism

Transformers: changing voltage

Place two coils around a shared iron core. A changing current in the first changes the magnetic field through both. The second coil receives an induced voltage.

The first is the primary; the second is the secondary. This arrangement is a transformer. The core guides a large part of the magnetic field through both coils.

If each turn links approximately the same changing magnetic field, each receives the same induced voltage per turn. More turns add more of these contributions.

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np

Here V means voltage and N number of turns; subscripts s and p mean secondary and primary. This is the ideal transformer relation for corresponding AC voltage measures.

Twice as many secondary turns gives twice the secondary voltage. Fewer secondary turns gives a smaller voltage. Turns are counts, so both ratios have no units.

Higher voltage does not mean extra energy

For an ideal transformer powering a resistor, input and output powers match. Use effective AC values — values giving the same heating as DC:

VpIp = VsIs

At the same transferred power, stepping voltage up steps current down. Real transformers also transfer some input energy into heating and other losses.

Alternating current, AC, continually changes direction and magnetic field. A steady direct current, DC, gives no sustained induction in the secondary after switching effects end.

Follow one connected energy story

A turbine turns a generator. A transformer raises voltage for lower-current transmission. Wires then waste less power through I²R heating. Other transformers reduce voltage near users.

Phone chargers add switching and control circuits. Induction cooktops use changing fields to drive currents that heat a pan; the pan can warm the cooktop.

Test the complete account

Connect a motor to a generator and feed the generator’s output back. Could it keep producing surplus energy? No: even ideal conversion gives no extra energy, and real losses leave less useful output than input. Following each transfer exposes the mistake.

Interactions change motion or energy; equations connect their sizes, directions and rates, while conservation keeps the complete account consistent.
