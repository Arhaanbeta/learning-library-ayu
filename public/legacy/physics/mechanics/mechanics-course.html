Mechanics, rebuilt from the ground up

Imagine filming a moving object. Its positions make the film; differences between frames reveal motion; interactions explain changes. Separate accounts track what transfers through time, distance and rotation.

A thrown, tumbling spanner makes the distinction real: gravity bends its centre’s path, while a twist changes its spin. Both stories belong to one motion.

The conceptual map

Follow the arrows as dependencies. A later idea reuses an earlier one; each branch answers a different question about the same physical world.

  1. 1 · Describe space and changeArrows and components → rates and accumulation → straight-line motion
  2. 2 · Add directions and observersMotion in a plane → relative motion → circular paths
  3. 3 · Explain the changesInteractions and mass → body boundaries and constraints → friction → accelerating observers
  4. 4 · Keep accounts of interactionsWork and kinetic energy → stored energy; impulse and momentum → centre of mass → collisions → mass flow
  5. 5 · Describe and explain turningAngle and spin → where the mass sits → turning effects

Scope: these attachments cover mechanics through torque and couples. Electricity, heat, waves and later rotational topics are outside the supplied material.

Lesson 01 · the language of direction

When a number needs an arrow

Two people push a trolley equally hard. It may move easily or remain still. The amount of each push alone cannot tell us which.

Draw each push as an arrow: its length represents size, and its pointing direction represents where it acts. Put the arrows head to tail to find their combined effect.

This is a vector (a quantity whose direction matters when combined). Force, displacement and velocity are vectors. Mass and elapsed time are scalars (quantities that need only a size).

Identical vector arrows combine head to tail. Electric current has a direction along a wire, but currents meeting at a junction add as amounts, not spatial arrows.

A + B = resultant arrow; A − B = A + (reversed B)

Walk east, then north: final displacement points diagonally. Draw A and B from one tail; B − A points from A’s head to B’s.

Choose horizontal and vertical axes. A diagonal arrow has a horizontal shadow and a vertical shadow, called components (the portions in chosen directions). Add shadows separately, then rebuild the diagonal.

Aₓ = A cos θ; Aᵧ = A sin θ; |A| = √(Aₓ² + Aᵧ²)

Here A is the arrow’s length, θ its angle from positive horizontal, and Aₓ and Aᵧ its signed components. Right and up are positive only if we choose them so.

The square-root relation comes from a right-angled triangle. Double A while holding θ fixed and both components double; rotate A while holding its length fixed and their shares change.

Coordinates belong to a chosen origin. Position changes when you move the origin; displacement, final position minus initial position, does not, because both positions shift equally.

Two arrow products ask different questions. The dot product (how much arrows align) gives A·B = AB cos θ; its sign can be positive, zero or negative.

The cross product (how strongly two arrows span a turning plane) has size AB sin θ and points perpendicular to that plane. Reversing the order reverses its direction.

We will meet these naturally: work uses force along movement, so it uses a dot product; torque uses force across a lever, so it uses a cross product.

Bridge: three arrows make a signed volume through A·(B×C); nested products obey geometric rules. They are compact tools, not additional forces or motions.

Tempting idea: two forces of 3 N and 4 N always make 7 N.

Why it seems reasonable: ordinary numbers add that way. What happens: the total also depends on their relative directions; opposite arrows partly cancel.

Two geometric shortcuts worth keeping

At right angles, arrow lengths combine by Pythagoras. For a general angle θ, |A+B|²=A²+B²+2AB cos θ; this follows by resolving B along and across A.

If three forces hold one point still, their head-to-tail arrows close a triangle. That closed shape expresses equilibrium more reliably than adding three unsigned magnitudes.

Three arrows can describe a signed volume: A·(B×C). It is zero when all three lie in one plane, because a flat shape encloses no volume.

Lesson 02 · mathematics built for motion

Rates are close views; totals are accumulated pieces

A speedometer asks how fast the car moves now, while distance divided by time needs an interval. Solve the tension by measuring over shorter and shorter intervals around now.

For smooth motion, those average rates settle towards one number. A limit (the value approached by a shrinking interval) defines an instantaneous rate without dividing zero by zero.

v(t) = limΔt→0 [x(t+Δt) − x(t)]/Δt = dx/dt

Here x is position in metres, t time in seconds, and v signed velocity in metres per second. The symbol dx/dt names this limiting rate, called a derivative.

On a position–time graph, an interval gives a chord’s slope. Shrink the interval: the chord approaches a tangent, whose signed slope is velocity at that instant.

To see where a rule comes from, take x=t². Over a short time h, the change is (t+h)²−t²=2th+h². Divide by h: 2t+h; shrinking h leaves 2t.

Thus d(t²)/dt=2t. More generally d(tⁿ)/dt=ntⁿ⁻¹: among the tiny changes in n multiplied factors, the first-order change can occur in any one factor.

Rates of sums add. A nested change obeys the chain rule (rates through an intermediate variable multiply): dy/dt=(dy/du)(du/dt). It will explain why v² grows with distance.

If both factors change, d(uv)/dt=u(dv/dt)+v(du/dt). Picture the added area of a widening, lengthening rectangle: two thin strips count, while their tiny overlapping corner vanishes in the limit.

Taking a second rate tells us how the first rate changes: acceleration is d²x/dt². For x=t², velocity 2t grows steadily and acceleration is 2, even though position curves.

Run the operation backwards to recover a total. A narrow strip under a velocity–time graph has area velocity × time, with units metres; adding signed strips gives displacement.

Δx = ∫t₁t₂ v(t) dt; Δv = ∫t₁t₂ a(t) dt

The long ∫ means add indefinitely thinner pieces. Differentiation finds the local rate; integration accumulates it. Reversing a derivative evaluates that accumulation, with starting conditions fixing any missing constant.

Area below the time axis counts negative, so ∫v dt gives displacement. Add areas under |v| instead for distance. A horizontal velocity graph gives a rectangle.

A force changing with position, or a mass spread through space, can be handled by the same slicing: find one small contribution, then add all contributions.

Bridge: equations that prescribe a rate, such as dv/dt=−kv, predict how a state evolves. Small-angle sin θ≈θ requires θ in radians and only applies near zero.

Tempting idea: a derivative is a tiny distance divided by zero time.

Why it seems reasonable: the interval shrinks to zero. What happens: ratios are calculated for nonzero intervals; only their approached value is taken.

When the rate itself depends on a variable

If acceleration is given as a function of position, a=dv/dt=(dv/dx)(dx/dt)=v(dv/dx). This is the chain rule; it replaces time by position, not acceleration by velocity.

A graph’s smooth maximum or minimum has zero slope. That condition finds candidates only; compare neighbouring slopes and endpoints to decide whether it is truly largest or smallest.

A quantity growing by the same fraction in equal time intervals follows an exponential curve. Its rate follows its size; a shrinking gap to a final value behaves similarly.

The exceptional accumulated rate 1/x gives ln|x|, the natural logarithm. That is why a rocket’s repeated gains proportional to 1/m produce a logarithm later.

Approximations carry conditions: (1+ε)ⁿ≈1+nε when |ε| is small, and sin θ≈θ when θ is small and measured in radians. The discarded pieces shrink faster than ε.

Lesson 03 · motion on one line

Position, velocity and acceleration answer different questions

Mark a runner’s place every second. Equal gaps mean steady motion; widening gaps mean speeding up. A complete description needs where, which way, and how the motion changes.

Choose an origin and a positive direction before assigning signs. Position x says where relative to that origin; displacement Δx=x₂−x₁ says how far and which way from start to finish.

Distance counts the entire path as positive length. Running out and back gives positive distance but zero displacement. Physics needs both because paths and changes of place answer different questions.

Average velocity is displacement divided by elapsed time; average speed is distance divided by elapsed time. Instantaneous speed is |v| on a line, but the two averages often differ.

Acceleration measures a change of velocity, not the amount of velocity. If eastward velocity gains 1 m/s each second, acceleration is +1 m/s² when east is positive.

a = Δv/Δt for an interval; a = dv/dt at an instant

Metres per second squared means metres per second of velocity change per second. A negative value means velocity shifts towards the negative direction, whether the object is slowing or speeding.

On a line, matching signs of v and a increase speed; opposing signs decrease it. A thrown ball has v=0 at its top but still accelerates downward.

Read three stacked graphs. Position–time slope is v; velocity–time slope is a; area under velocity–time is signed displacement; area under acceleration–time is change in velocity.

A horizontal position line means rest, while a horizontal velocity line means constant nonzero velocity if above zero. A downward velocity slope means negative acceleration, not necessarily deceleration.

When acceleration stays constant, equal time steps add equal velocity changes. The velocity graph is a straight line, so its area is a rectangle plus a triangle.

v = u + at;   s = ut + ½at²;   v² = u² + 2as

Here u and v are initial and final signed velocities, a constant acceleration, t elapsed time, and s signed displacement. The first counts repeated velocity changes.

The second adds initial motion ut and the triangular extra distance ½at². The third removes time: since a=dv/dt=v(dv/dx), distance determines a change in squared speed.

Double t while u=0 and fixed a: displacement quadruples. Set a=0 and the equations reduce to uniform motion. Reverse a and the added changes reverse.

Near Earth with little air resistance, free-falling objects share a nearly constant downward acceleration g. With up positive, use a=−g throughout rising, turning and falling.

Constant-a equations fail when a changes. Divide the journey into phases or use graphs; (u+v)/2 is average velocity only when the velocity graph is straight.

Tempting idea: the ball’s acceleration vanishes when it momentarily stops at the top.

Why it seems reasonable: stillness feels like no action. What happens: gravity keeps changing its velocity downward, including at that instant.

Lesson 04 · motion in a plane

Two directions share one clock

Flick one coin sideways off a table as another drops from the same height. Without air resistance, they land together. Sideways motion does not delay downward motion.

Keep one time variable but track horizontal and vertical components separately. If gravity is the only force, horizontal acceleration is zero and vertical acceleration is −g with up positive.

x = uₓt;   y = uᵧt − ½gt²;   vₓ = uₓ;   vᵧ = uᵧ − gt

Here uₓ and uᵧ are launch velocity components; x and y are displacements from launch. The horizontal gap grows evenly, while the vertical gaps change each second.

At the highest point vᵧ=0, but vₓ generally remains nonzero and gravity still acts. Eliminate time using t=x/uₓ and the path becomes a parabola (a quadratic curve).

For launch and landing at the same height, time aloft is 2uᵧ/g and range is uₓ times that time. Increasing upward speed prolongs flight; increasing sideways speed stretches range.

If launch speed is u at angle θ above horizontal, uₓ=u cos θ and uᵧ=u sin θ. Range becomes u² sin(2θ)/g for level landing, with no air resistance.

Two complementary angles have the same range: the steep shot flies longer but travels sideways slower. A 45° shot maximises range only under those same ideal conditions.

For a launch from height, or onto a slope, reuse the component equations and change the landing condition. The familiar level-range expression no longer follows.

Acceleration along velocity changes speed; acceleration across it bends direction. At a projectile’s apex, gravity bends its path without changing speed at that instant.

a = aalong + aturn;   |aturn| = v²/ρ

Here ρ is local radius of curvature (the circle best fitting a short path segment). At fixed speed, a tighter bend needs more turning acceleration.

Air drag points against velocity and depends on speed. It changes horizontal as well as vertical motion, so the ideal parabola and ascent–descent symmetry no longer apply.

Bridge: choosing axes along a hill makes landing a zero perpendicular displacement. For a fixed launch speed, the boundary of all reachable ideal targets forms an envelope curve.

Tempting idea: faster sideways motion weakens the downward pull.

Why it seems reasonable: a fast projectile stays aloft farther away. What happens: it spends the same falling time covering more horizontal distance.

Three extensions from the same component method

A projectile aimed onto a straight slope lands when its perpendicular displacement to that slope returns to zero. Rotate your axes; then gravity has components along and into the slope.

At fixed launch speed, every ideal trajectory lies below an outer boundary. At horizontal position x its height is at most u²/(2g)−gx²/(2u²); a target above it is unreachable.

That boundary follows by asking whether the trajectory equation has any real launch angle. At the boundary the two possible angles merge into one; inside, there are usually two.

The boundary and the equal-range angle pair require constant downward g, no air resistance, and freely chosen launch direction. They are model predictions rather than universal rules.

Lesson 05 · the observer matters

One journey can have several correct velocities

A passenger tosses a ball straight up on a smooth train. On the platform, its path moves forward. Neither observer changes the ball; they compare it with different origins.

A reference frame (an observer’s axes and clock) defines what position and velocity mean. Subtract the observer’s motion to describe what that observer sees.

vA relative to B = vA − vB

These are arrows, so subtract their components. A boat’s velocity over the ground equals its velocity through water plus the water’s velocity over the ground.

Aim upstream to land opposite the starting point; aim straight across to maximise the cross-river component for a fixed boat speed. These are different goals.

For steadily moving frames, velocities differ by a constant, so accelerations agree. Thus the same Newtonian force law works on a smooth, steady train.

Two freely falling objects have the same gravitational acceleration; subtract and their relative acceleration is zero. One then sees the other travel in a straight line at steady relative velocity.

To find the closest approach of two steadily moving objects, ride with one. Draw the other’s straight relative path; the shortest separation is the perpendicular distance to that path.

Accelerating or rotating observers require extra care: a subtraction of velocities at one instant does not make their frames obey Newton’s laws without correction. We will rebuild that correction later.

Bridge: the subtraction rule is Galilean (the everyday, slow-speed frame rule). At speeds near light, time comparisons also change; that lies outside this course.

Tempting idea: there is one true path of the tossed ball.

Why it seems reasonable: the ball is one object. What happens: paths record positions relative to frames; both paths describe the same events.

Aiming and apparent direction

Walk forward into vertically falling rain. Relative to you, the rain has a backward horizontal component, so you tilt an umbrella towards your direction of travel.

For a river, aim upstream enough that your boat’s upstream component cancels current to land directly opposite. If current exceeds your maximum upstream speed, that goal is impossible.

For closest approach, relative position is r(t)=r₀+vt. The squared distance stops decreasing when r(t)·v=0, meaning separation is perpendicular to the relative path.

The candidate time is −(r₀·v)/|v|². If it is negative and you consider only the future, the objects are already moving apart and their closest future point is now.

Lesson 06 · interactions cause changes

Why an object can keep moving without a push

Slide a book across a desk and it stops. Repeat on a smoother surface: it goes farther. Imagine removing every resisting interaction; nothing then changes its forward velocity.

Newton’s first law describes this persistence and identifies inertial frames (observers for whom an unforced object moves steadily in a straight line). A smooth, steadily moving train nearly qualifies.

A force is an interaction: one body pushes or pulls another. Draw each force as an arrow on the body receiving it, name the other body, then add all arrows.

Mass measures resistance to changing velocity. Double the net push at fixed mass and acceleration doubles; double mass under the same push and acceleration halves.

ΣF = ma;   1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

ΣF means the vector sum of external forces, m is mass in kilograms, and a is acceleration in metres per second squared. Only the net force fixes acceleration.

Zero net force means zero acceleration, which permits rest or steady straight motion. A force opposite current velocity can slow a body; one sideways can turn it.

Weight is Earth’s gravitational pull, approximately mg near the ground; mass is an object’s resistance to acceleration. Mass stays the same in a lift while its scale reading changes.

The scale senses its normal force (the surface’s push perpendicular to contact). On an upward-accelerating lift it must push harder than mg; during free fall it reads zero.

Other contact forces include tension along a taut string and spring force toward a spring’s natural length. For moderate stretches, Fspring=−kx, with k measuring stiffness.

Newton’s third law pairs equal and opposite forces between A and B. They act on different bodies, so they never cancel on one body’s diagram.

For a fixed-mass object, net force changes momentum (mass times velocity) each second. This more general wording becomes crucial for collisions and rockets.

Tempting idea: a moving object needs a forward force continuously.

Why it seems reasonable: friction stops ordinary objects. What happens: force is needed to change motion; with friction absent, steady motion persists.

What the ideal contact models hide

An ideal light rope transmits one tension along a smooth massless pulley; a massive pulley requires a difference in tensions to accelerate its spin.

A stretched spring’s force depends on its extension. Immediately after another supporting rope is cut, the spring has not yet changed length, so its force initially persists.

Equal inertial mass and gravitational mass explain why different bodies share free-fall acceleration when air is negligible: stronger gravitational pull is matched by proportionally greater resistance to acceleration.

Lesson 07 · choose a body, then its restrictions

Forces and geometry solve different parts of a system

Place a roped block on a table. Rope, table and Earth interact with it; studying the block means replacing each surrounding body with its force arrow.

That isolated sketch is a free-body diagram (a chosen body with only its external forces). Never add an acceleration arrow as though it were another force.

Choose axes along a surface and perpendicular to it. Write ΣFₓ=maₓ and ΣFᵧ=maᵧ. On an incline, weight contributes mg sin θ downslope and mg cos θ into it.

The normal force is whatever contact requires; it is not automatically mg. On a smooth incline with no motion through the surface, N=mg cos θ.

Draw a boundary around two blocks instead. Their pushes on each other become internal pairs, so they cancel in the total-force equation; the combined mass responds to external forces.

But force equations alone cannot say how a fixed-length string coordinates different movements. That missing information comes from geometry, called a constraint (a rule restricting possible positions).

L = x₁ + x₂ + constant ⇒ v₁ + v₂ = 0 ⇒ a₁ + a₂ = 0

Here the two string sections change length oppositely under these chosen coordinates. Since the total length L stays fixed, its first and second time-rates also vanish.

A pulley supported by two rope sections rises one unit when the free end moves two units. This relation also sets speed and acceleration ratios.

Ideal light ropes and smooth massless pulleys give equal tension along one continuous rope. A pulley with rotational inertia can have unequal tensions; the difference turns it.

Surfaces impose constraints too: two bodies staying in contact have equal velocity components perpendicular to their common surface. Along-surface motion may still differ through sliding.

Separate bodies to find contact forces; combine them to find shared motion. A negative acceleration reveals that an assumed direction was wrong.

Tempting idea: connected objects always have identical accelerations.

Why it seems reasonable: one rope links them. What happens: its fixed total length can create different magnitudes, as in a movable pulley.

A movable pulley trades force against distance. Without losses, two tensions support its load while the free end travels twice as far; input and output work agree.

Lesson 08 · a force that adjusts until it cannot

Friction is a response to attempted sliding

Push gently on a stationary crate. It stays put because the floor pushes back through friction; push harder and that opposing force grows with your push.

At a microscopic scale (the scale of tiny surface contacts), apparently smooth surfaces touch at scattered spots. Adhesion and deformation at those spots resist relative motion.

Static friction (the sideways contact force before sliding) takes the value needed to prevent sliding, up to a maximum. The rough-surface model writes that ceiling as μₛN.

|fₛ| ≤ μₛN;   |fₖ| ≈ μₖN while sliding

Here N is the perpendicular contact force in newtons, fₛ and fₖ are friction forces, and μₛ and μₖ are unitless coefficients for the two surfaces together.

Before motion, friction may be zero, small or maximal. Once surfaces slide, a common approximation gives kinetic friction μₖN opposite their relative sliding, usually below the static maximum.

On a tilted board, gravity tries to pull a block down with mg sin θ; the surface squeezes it with N=mg cos θ. Rest is possible while tan θ≤μₛ.

The angle where sliding begins is the angle of repose; its tangent equals μₛ in this model. Mass cancels from the competing forces.

For a top block riding a moving lower block, first assume no slipping. Work out how much friction that shared acceleration needs; compare the answer with μₛN.

If required friction exceeds its ceiling, blocks slip and the kinetic rule applies. Friction opposes relative sliding or its tendency, not necessarily travel.

The dry-friction rules are approximations; lubricated contacts, tyres, sticky materials and changing speed can behave differently. A coefficient need not be less than one.

Tempting idea: stationary friction always equals μₛN.

Why it seems reasonable: that expression appears on formula sheets. What happens: it is a maximum, reached only when slipping is about to begin.

Lesson 09 · the turning part of acceleration

Constant speed around a circle still needs a net force

Whirl a stone on a string at steady speed: its velocity direction turns continuously. Release it, and the stone travels along its tangent.

A circle of radius r has arc length s=rθ when θ is measured in radians (angles measured by arc length divided by radius). One complete turn is 2π radians.

v = ωr;   ainward = v²/r = ω²r

Angular speed ω is angle turned per second, measured in rad/s; v is distance along the rim per second. Outer points move faster than inner points on one rigid wheel.

Why v²/r? During a short step, velocity turns through roughly distance/r, changing by roughly v times that angle. Divide by step time.

The inward acceleration grows fourfold when speed doubles at fixed radius. It tends to zero as speed tends to zero; at fixed speed, a gentler, larger circle needs less.

Newton’s law then demands an inward net force mv²/r. This is a requirement for the real forces, not an extra force to add to the diagram.

Tension turns a stone; static friction turns a car; gravity bends an orbit; part of a banked road’s normal force turns a car.

For a banked road without friction, vertical support N cos θ balances mg and inward N sin θ supplies mv²/r. Dividing gives tan θ=v²/(rg).

In a vertical circle, gravity also changes speed. Energy relates speeds at different heights; inward force balance separately determines tension. A slack string cannot pull outward, so contact conditions matter.

If speed changes while turning, add tangential acceleration dv/dt along the path and inward acceleration v²/r across it. These are perpendicular jobs, not competing descriptions.

Tempting idea: an outward force throws the released stone away.

Why it seems reasonable: the stone seems to pull on your hand. What happens: after release its forward velocity continues tangentially as the inward pull vanishes.

A loop and a cone

At the top of a vertical string loop, gravity and tension both point inward. The condition T+mg=mv²/r requires v²≥gr for a taut string; below that the string goes slack.

A conical pendulum’s bob circles horizontally while its string slants. Vertical tension component balances weight, and horizontal tension component supplies the required inward force.

These are force balances at particular positions. Energy can separately tell how speed changes with height; using both descriptions avoids inventing a new centripetal force.

Lesson 10 · accelerating viewpoints

Why a turning carriage makes you feel pushed outward

Stand in a bus as it accelerates forward. You seem to lurch backwards. From the road, your body initially keeps its old velocity while the bus moves forward beneath you.

An accelerating observer sees apparent acceleration without a matching interaction. Newton’s equation in that frame needs a pseudo-force (a frame correction, not another body’s push).

ΣFreal − mA = ma′

Here A is the observer’s acceleration relative to an inertial frame, and a′ is the object’s acceleration relative to that observer. The correction points opposite A.

Combine gravity g with −A into effective gravity geff=g−A. A hanging string or liquid surface responds to this apparent downward direction.

If a cabin falls freely with its contents, both cabin and contents have the same downward acceleration. They exert little contact force on each other, producing weightlessness without removing gravity.

A rotating frame needs more than the bus correction. Objects fixed on the turntable appear to feel an outward centrifugal correction mω²r; moving objects also show a sideways Coriolis correction.

FCoriolis = −2m(ω × v′)   in a uniformly rotating frame

Here v′ is velocity on the turntable, ω its rotation arrow, and × sets the sideways direction. The term vanishes for an object stationary on it.

Earth’s rotation deflects moving air and water. Its effect is usually tiny for short motions; weather spans enough time and distance to show it.

Before writing a force equation, name the frame. Use only interaction forces in an inertial frame; add the appropriate correction terms when choosing an accelerating or rotating frame.

Bridge: a small freely falling cabin and a small non-gravitating cabin can behave alike locally. This observation led towards a deeper theory of gravity; it does not erase Earth’s gravity.

Tempting idea: astronauts float because gravity is absent in orbit.

Why it seems reasonable: they feel no supporting push. What happens: astronaut and spacecraft fall together while gravity continuously bends their path.

For a ball moving outward on a turntable, outer points have greater sideways speed and its motion changes direction. Both effects make the factor two.

The displayed Coriolis expression assumes a frame rotating at constant rate. If rotation rate itself changes, an additional correction is required.

Lesson 11 · force acting through movement

Work tracks what a force transfers along a path

Pull a suitcase with a sloping strap. Forward force moves it; upward force changes the floor’s push, but contributes no direct forward work.

Work (energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement) counts the component of force along movement. It can add or remove kinetic energy (energy associated with motion).

W = Fd cos θ for constant force;   K = ½mv²;   Wnet = ΔK

F is force in newtons, d displacement in metres, and θ their angle. A newton metre of work is one joule; K is measured in joules too.

Parallel force does positive work; opposite force negative work; perpendicular force zero work. Holding a stationary bag may tire your muscles but transfers no mechanical work to the stationary bag.

Why the square in K? From F=ma and a=v(dv/dx), work over a small movement is F dx = mv dv. Accumulating from rest gives half m times v².

Thus the work–energy theorem says net work equals change in ½mv². Twice the mass at fixed speed doubles K; twice speed at fixed mass quadruples K.

When force varies with position, slice the path into short steps. On a force–distance graph, strip area F dx is work; signed area under the curve gives the total.

For a spring whose resisting force grows evenly from zero to kx, the work done by an external pull is the triangle’s area, ½kx².

Gravity’s work near Earth depends only on vertical change: Wg=−mgΔh with up positive. Friction’s negative work usually grows with the length of the actual route.

Power asks how quickly energy transfers: P=ΔW/Δt on average, or P=F·v at an instant. One watt is one joule per second.

Energy methods often answer “how fast at this position?” without finding how long the journey took. Forces remain essential for direction, contact conditions and timing.

Tempting idea: a force that acts must always do work.

Why it seems reasonable: effort feels like energy use. What happens: mechanical work on a body needs displacement along the force at its point of action.

Lesson 12 · stored energy and landscape

When work depends only on where you start and finish

Lift a book and let it fall: gravity returns the energy that lifting transferred. Drag it around a rough loop and friction does negative work the whole way back.

A conservative force (one whose work between two places ignores the route) permits potential energy U (a record of energy stored by an arrangement of interacting bodies).

ΔU = −Wconservative;   Ugravity = mgh;   Uspring = ½kx²

Here h is height above a chosen reference, x is spring extension or compression, and k its stiffness. Only changes of U matter; the zero level is a convenient choice.

Gravity does negative work when you lift the book, so gravitational U increases. The spring’s restoring force does negative work while stretched, storing ½kx² in the spring arrangement.

Start with net work equals ΔK. Replace conservative work by −ΔU. The result explains when mechanical energy E=K+U stays constant and when another process changes it.

Wother = Δ(K+U);   if Wother=0, then K+U is constant

Friction can convert mechanical energy into internal energy such as microscopic heating. Total energy still balances, while K+U falls in this chosen mechanical account.

Plot U against position x. At fixed total energy E, the vertical gap E−U is K. If U exceeds E, motion there would require negative K and cannot occur.

At U=E the object can turn around. A low point of U gives stable equilibrium: a small displacement meets a restoring push. A high point is unstable.

Fₓ = −dU/dx   for a conservative force in one dimension

The minus sign says force points downhill on a U graph. A steeper slope means a stronger force; a flat tangent means zero force at that point.

A potential-energy curve is a map of allowed positions and local pushes, not an ordinary landscape in space. It also foreshadows oscillation near a smooth minimum.

Tempting idea: potential energy belongs to a body alone and has an absolute value.

Why it seems reasonable: we say “the book’s energy”. What happens: it describes an interaction and reference choice; physical predictions use differences.

Near a smooth minimum, U often looks approximately like a bowl-shaped quadratic curve. Its slope produces a restoring force approximately proportional to displacement, setting up repeated oscillation.

Lesson 13 · force acting over time

Impulse changes momentum, even during a violent collision

A heavy trolley moving slowly and a light ball moving quickly can carry the same momentum. Both need the same total impulse to stop, although their kinetic energies can differ.

Momentum p (mass times velocity, with direction) describes how movement is shared between interacting bodies. It is measured in kg·m/s; force changes it over time.

p = mv;   ΣFexternal = dp/dt;   J = ∫F dt = Δp

J is impulse (force accumulated over time), in N·s or kg·m/s. Constant force gives FΔt; changing force gives signed area under its force–time graph.

A crumple zone lengthens stopping time. For the same momentum change, average force Δp/Δt falls as the time grows; its exact peak also depends on the force’s shape.

Choose a system boundary around two colliding objects. Their equal and opposite interaction forces act for the same duration, giving opposite impulses that cancel in total.

Δpsystem = Jexternal;   if Jexternal≈0, total p stays constant

External forces can still act during a brief collision, but their impulse may be negligible compared with the contact impulse. Over a long interval, that approximation may fail.

Momentum is an arrow: conserve horizontal and vertical components separately. An explosion from rest sends pieces apart with momentum arrows that add to zero.

Energy and momentum answer different questions. A moving object has p=mv and K=½mv²; reversing direction reverses p but leaves K unchanged.

Integrate force over time to find impulse and momentum change. Integrate force along displacement to find work and kinetic-energy change. One interaction has both descriptions.

Tempting idea: momentum conservation guarantees kinetic energy conservation.

Why it seems reasonable: both describe motion. What happens: collisions can convert kinetic energy into heat, sound and deformation while total momentum still balances.

Force–time area fixes impulse, not peak force. A longer event lowers average force for fixed impulse; its force shape controls the peak.

Lesson 14 · many bodies, one useful point

The centre of mass follows external forces

Throw a spanner and watch it tumble. Its ends trace complicated loops, but one weighted-average point follows the smooth path gravity gives to any thrown object.

The centre of mass (the mass-weighted average position) moves towards the heavier parts. For separate pieces, multiply each position by its mass, add, and divide by total mass.

rCM = (Σmᵢrᵢ)/M;   M = Σmᵢ

rᵢ are position arrows, mᵢ the corresponding masses, and M total mass. Take the average independently for each coordinate. A symmetric uniform object has its centre on its symmetry axes.

For a spread-out body, imagine tiny pieces dm: rCM=(1/M)∫r dm. Density turns each tiny length, area or volume into its mass.

Remove a hole by treating it as material subtracted from a full shape. The remaining centre shifts away from the removed part; the subtraction is bookkeeping, not physical negative matter.

Differentiate the weighted average for a fixed collection. Total momentum equals M times centre-of-mass velocity; internal interaction forces cancel when differentiating again.

ptotal = MvCM;   ΣFexternal = MaCM

Internal shuffling or an explosion can change where parts go, but cannot independently bend the centre’s path. Gravity can bend that path because Earth lies outside the chosen system.

In the centre-of-mass frame (an observer moving with that point), the system’s total momentum is zero. Two bodies approach with opposite momenta even when their masses differ.

Total kinetic energy separates into centre motion and motion relative to the centre. Momentum conservation protects the centre’s movement; collisions can dissipate some relative motion.

Tempting idea: the centre of mass must lie inside the material.

Why it seems reasonable: it sounds like a balancing point. What happens: a ring’s centre sits in empty space; it remains a useful weighted position.

Density (mass per unit of space) gives a slice’s mass. Use mass per length for wire, per area for sheet, or per volume for solid.

Lesson 15 · quick exchanges

A collision preserves one account while changing another

Two carts collide and push each other for a short time. Their internal impulses cancel. Their kinetic energy can become deformation, heat and sound before they separate.

For an isolated pair, total momentum before equals total momentum after. In one-dimensional motion that gives one equation for two final velocities, so another condition is needed.

Elastic means kinetic energy is also unchanged after separation; inelastic means some has changed form. Perfectly inelastic means the bodies leave stuck together, sharing one velocity.

If carts stick: v = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁+m₂)

Here m₁,m₂ are masses, u₁,u₂ signed incoming velocities, and v their shared outgoing velocity. This v is the original centre-of-mass velocity, forced by momentum conservation.

How springy is a direct collision? The coefficient of restitution e (speed of separation divided by speed of approach along the contact direction) lies commonly between zero and one.

e = (v₂−v₁)/(u₁−u₂)   when u₁>u₂ on one line

e=1 represents an elastic direct collision; e=0 means no relative separation. Combined with total momentum, it predicts both outgoing velocities without knowing the changing contact force.

Equal masses in a direct elastic collision exchange velocities. In the centre-of-mass frame, each reverses direction with the same speed it had before.

During a two-dimensional collision, momentum components balance independently. Restitution applies along the line of impact; tangential components may need a friction model.

If stuck carts later climb a slope, split the event. Use momentum during the short collision, then energy for the climb if later non-conservative work is negligible.

Momentum describes total movement shared among the bodies; kinetic energy depends on squared speeds. Sticking leaves the centre’s kinetic energy even when relative kinetic energy is lost.

Tempting idea: sticking destroys all kinetic energy.

Why it seems reasonable: it removes relative motion. What happens: unless total momentum was zero, the combined body still moves and retains kinetic energy.

For two direct-impact masses, the kinetic energy of motion relative to their centre shrinks by a factor e². The centre-of-mass kinetic energy stays because total momentum fixes its speed.

For equal masses and an elastic two-dimensional impact with one initially stationary, the outgoing velocity arrows are perpendicular when the contact is smooth and both final velocities are nonzero.

Lesson 16 · matter crossing a boundary

A rocket accelerates by sending momentum away

Imagine a trolley catching stationary sand. Its mass grows while sand must acquire the trolley’s velocity. Choosing only the trolley as “the system” now lets matter cross the boundary.

For such open systems (chosen objects gaining or losing matter), blindly applying F=ma or d(mv)/dt as the external force misses momentum carried across the boundary.

First choose the same collection of matter before and after a short interval. Account for both the remaining body and the matter that enters or leaves; then apply momentum change.

A rocket ejects gas backward at relative speed u, losing mass at a positive rate q kilograms per second. Exhaust takes backward momentum, so the rocket gains forward thrust uq.

m(dv/dt) = Fexternal + uq   for this one-dimensional rocket model

m is the rocket’s remaining mass, v its forward speed, and Fexternal includes gravity and drag if present. Rockets therefore work in vacuum; they push gas, not air.

With no external force and constant exhaust speed u, each small loss of mass gives dv=u(−dm)/m. A smaller remaining mass gains more speed from the same lost amount.

Δv = u ln(m₀/mf)   for free space and constant u

m₀ and mf are initial and final rocket masses. The logarithm (a slowly growing function) arises from adding many steps proportional to lost mass divided by mass still carried.

Double the mass ratio and speed gains only u ln 2 more, under these assumptions. More propellant must itself be accelerated, so gains become increasingly expensive.

A table supports landed chain and stops incoming links, removing their downward momentum. A conveyor similarly gives horizontal momentum to falling material.

These are momentum-flow questions. Choose the boundary, find what momentum enters or leaves each second, and name the external forces supplying the difference.

Tempting idea: a rocket needs something outside itself to push against.

Why it seems reasonable: walking and driving use ground contact. What happens: gas and rocket push each other apart, carrying opposite changes of momentum.

Sand dropped onto a steady belt gains momentum at mass-arrival rate times belt speed. The motor supplies force times belt speed; some supplied energy becomes heat while the sand slips.

A chain landing on a table similarly needs support for accumulated links and extra force to stop incoming links. Weight alone misses the arriving momentum each second.

Lesson 17 · describing rotation

One angle describes every part of a rigid wheel

Paint dots near the centre and rim of a wheel. In one turn they share the same change of angle, although the rim dot travels a much longer path.

A rigid body (parts keep fixed distances) rotates about an axis by angle θ. In radians, a point at radius r travels arc length s=rθ.

Angular velocity ω measures angle change per second; angular acceleration α measures change of ω per second. Choose anticlockwise positive and clockwise negative.

ω = dθ/dt;   α = dω/dt;   v = ωr;   atangent = αr

ω has units rad/s and α rad/s²; radians are ratios of lengths. Points at larger r have larger tangential speed and tangential acceleration for the same wheel-wide rates.

Even with α=0, a rotating point’s velocity direction changes. Its inward acceleration remains ω²r; zero angular acceleration means steady spin, not zero acceleration everywhere.

With constant α, the straight-motion equations reappear with angle in place of displacement and ω in place of velocity: ω=ω₀+αt and Δθ=ω₀t+½αt².

Also ω²=ω₀²+2αΔθ. These depend on constant α; otherwise slopes and accumulated areas remain the reliable description.

On θ–time graphs slope gives ω. On ω–time graphs slope gives α and signed area gives angle change. Negative slope can increase clockwise speed.

Belts with no slip connect rim speeds: ω₁r₁=ω₂r₂ in magnitude. Two wheels locked to one shaft instead share the same ω, even if their radii differ.

A wheel rolling without slipping advances one circumference per turn: vCM=ωR. At contact, forward centre motion and backward rotation cancel instantaneously.

Bridge: ω points along the spin axis by a right-hand rule; local velocity is ω×r. Large turns about different axes need not commute.

Tempting idea: every point on a rigid wheel has the same speed.

Why it seems reasonable: they turn together. What happens: they share angular speed, while distance travelled each second grows with radius.

An angle’s sign records turning sense, not speed. If ω and α share a sign, spin speeds up; if their signs oppose, spin slows even when α is positive.

Lesson 18 · mass spread around an axis

Why mass far from the axle resists spinning more

Hold equal-mass dumbbells with their weights near and far from the handle. The far-weight version is harder to spin up, although both have the same mass.

Every piece at distance r from the axis has tangential speed ωr. Its kinetic energy is ½m(ωr)², so distant pieces contribute disproportionately.

Kspin = ½Iω²;   I = Σmᵢrᵢ², or I = ∫r² dm

Moment of inertia I (resistance to changing spin about an axis) has units kg·m². Each r is a piece’s perpendicular distance from that axis.

Its r² combines faster movement for outer mass at fixed ω with greater leverage for an outer force. Both effects reappear in rotational dynamics.

Double one piece’s distance from the axis, keeping its mass fixed, and its contribution to I quadruples. Mass exactly on the axis contributes zero about that axis.

A thin ring of mass M and radius R has I=MR² about its central axis; a uniform solid disc has I=½MR². The ring keeps all its mass far out.

For a rod through its centre, perpendicular to its length, I=ML²/12; moving the axis to its end gives I=ML²/3. An axis must always be specified.

Iparallel = ICM + Md²

The parallel-axis rule compares parallel axes separated by d, one through the centre of mass. Weighted cross-terms cancel at the centre, leaving Md².

For a flat sheet in the x–y plane, Iz=Ix+Iy about three axes crossing at one point. This perpendicular-axis rule needs the body to be flat.

Write I=Mk² to define radius of gyration k (the distance at which an equivalent thin ring could hold all its mass). Here k names a length, not a stiffness.

Add contributions for separate parts; subtract removed material from a full shape, always about the same axis. These methods grow directly from the weighted sum.

Bridge: for pure rolling, K=½MvCM²+½ICMω². Equal-mass bodies with more mass near their rims devote more energy to spin at the same centre speed.

Tempting idea: moment of inertia is a fixed property of the body.

Why it seems reasonable: its mass is fixed. What happens: its value changes when you choose a different axis or redistribute mass.

For a uniform sphere about its central axis, I=⅖MR²; for a thin spherical shell, I=⅔MR². Outer-heavy objects have greater I at equal M and R.

Lesson 19 · explaining rotation

A force turns only through its leverage

Push a door at its handle and it turns readily; push equally hard near its hinge and it barely turns. Push directly towards the hinge and it does not turn.

Torque (turning effect about an axis) combines force, lever distance and their angle. Only force across the lever turns the door.

τ = rF sin θ = F rperpendicular;   Στexternal = Iα

r is lever length, F applied force, θ their angle, and rperpendicular the shortest distance from the axis to the force’s line of action. Torque has units N·m.

If that extended line passes through the axis, torque is zero regardless of F. Double F or the perpendicular distance while holding the other factors fixed, and torque doubles.

For a fixed axis, choose anticlockwise positive. Add each force’s signed turning effect to get net torque. A pivot’s force passes through that pivot and has zero torque about it.

Why Iα? For one rigid-body piece, tangential acceleration is rα. Its tangential force is mrα; multiplying by the lever r gives torque mr²α.

Add all pieces: α is shared by the rigid body, and the remaining sum is Σmr²=I. Internal interaction torques cancel in the usual central-force model.

The law for a fixed axis says net external torque changes angular speed. A constant nonzero torque yields α=τ/I; a larger I yields smaller angular acceleration under the same torque.

Equal and opposite parallel forces along different lines form a couple (a pair with zero net force but nonzero torque). It can change spin without accelerating the centre of mass.

A couple’s torque has magnitude Fd, where d is separation of the force lines, independent of which origin you choose. Complete equilibrium needs ΣF=0 and Στ=0.

Torque as an arrow is r×F, pointing along the axis by the right-hand rule. Work by a turning force is τΔθ for constant aligned torque, with θ in radians.

Though torque and work both have dimensions N·m, torque is a turning tendency and work is transferred energy. One newton metre of torque is therefore written N·m rather than joule.

Rolling connects translation and rotation: ΣFexternal=MaCM, ΣτCM=ICMα, and no-slip gives aCM=Rα. The same contact force can affect both equations.

Tempting idea: equal and opposite forces always leave a body unchanged.

Why it seems reasonable: their vector sum is zero. What happens: separated force lines can produce a couple, turning the body without moving its centre.

For constant torque aligned with turning, work is τΔθ and power is τω. Work needs actual turning; a held door can have torque with no rotational work.
